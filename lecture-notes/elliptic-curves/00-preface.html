Preface

These are the notes for Math 714, a first graduate course on the arithmetic of elliptic curves. The main reference is [Sil09], which we follow closely from Chapter 4 on.

Prerequisites

We assume the first-year graduate courses in algebra and complex analysis. More precisely, the reader should be comfortable with:

  • Algebra. Finite and infinite Galois theory, including profinite groups and the Krull topology; modules over a principal ideal domain; basic commutative algebra (localization, integral extensions, discrete valuation rings).
  • Algebraic number theory. Number fields and \(p\)-adic fields and their rings of integers; Dedekind domains; the decomposition of primes; and the finiteness of the class group and of the unit rank.
  • Complex analysis. Holomorphic and meromorphic functions, Liouville's theorem, the residue theorem, and analytic continuation. Chapter 1 builds the theory of Riemann surfaces it needs.

No algebraic geometry is assumed. In the spirit of [Poo06], we use the language of scheme theory and explain how to think concretely about scheme-theoretic constructions in terms of commutative algebra. Chapters 2 and 3 collect what we use from the theory of algebraic varieties and algebraic curves.

Notation and conventions

Rings are commutative with \(1\). We write \(\N\), \(\Z\), \(\Q\), \(\R\) and \(\C\) as usual, \(\F_q\) for the field with \(q\) elements, \(\Z_p\) and \(\Q_p\) for the \(p\)-adic integers and numbers, and \(\#S\) or \(\lvert S \rvert\) for the cardinality of a set \(S\).

Fields. We do not assume that fields are perfect, nor that residue field extensions are separable. We assume it only case by case, where it makes the theory considerably simpler, and we always say so. Each chapter states its notation and standing hypotheses in a Situation at its start. For a field \(k\), we fix an algebraic closure \(\kbar\), and write \(\ksep\) for the separable closure of \(k\) in \(\kbar\). We write \(\Gal_k \coloneqq \Gal(\ksep/k)\) for the absolute Galois group of \(k\). The group \(\Gal_k\) acts on \(X(\ksep)\); since restriction \(\Aut(\kbar/k) \to \Gal_k\) is an isomorphism, this also determines the action on \(X(\kbar)\).

Varieties. A \(k\)-variety is a separated scheme of finite type over \(k\). We do not assume varieties to be integral or reduced. A curve is an integral \(k\)-variety of dimension \(1\), and a curve is nice if it is smooth, projective, and geometrically irreducible.

Groups. \(\Ga\) and \(\Gm\) are the additive and multiplicative groups, and \(\Gm^{(d)}\) is the twisted multiplicative group, the norm-one elements of a quadratic algebra \(L_d\) (never \(\mathbb{G}_m[d]\), which would read as a torsion subgroup).

Local fields. For a non-archimedean place \(v\), \(k_v^{\unr}\) is the maximal unramified extension of \(k_v\) in \(k_v^{\operatorname{sep}}\), and \(I_v \subseteq \Gal_{k_v}\) is the inertia group.

Coordinates. Upper case letters are homogeneous coordinates on projective space, lower case letters are affine coordinates, and the two are related by dehomogenization. On the standard patch \(U_0 = \{X_0 \neq 0\}\) of \(\PP^n\) we write \(x_i = X_i/X_0\); for a plane curve in \(\PP^2\) with coordinates \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\) the affine chart is \(Z \neq 0\), with \(x = X/Z\) and \(y = Y/Z\). The same rule applies to the names of polynomials: \(F\) is homogeneous and \(f\) is its dehomogenization.

Elliptic curves. For an elliptic curve \(E\) over \(k\) and an integer \(m\), \(E[m]\) is the group scheme given by the kernel of multiplication by \(m\), and \(E[m](\kbar)\) is the \(m\)-torsion subgroup of \(E(\kbar)\). Likewise, \(\mu_m(\kbar)\) is the group of \(m\)-th roots of unity in \(\kbar^{\times}\). For a nice curve \(C\) we write \(\Div C\) and \(\Pic C\) for the divisor group and the Picard group, and \(\Div^0 C\), \(\Pic^0 C\) for their degree-zero parts. Isogenies in \(\Hom(E_1, E_2)\), \(\End(E)\) and \(\Aut(E)\) are defined over the ground field, and \(\Hom(E_{1,\kbar}, E_{2,\kbar})\) denotes those defined over \(\kbar\). For \(N\) prime to the characteristic, \(\bar\rho_{E,N}\) is the mod \(N\) Galois representation on \(E[N](\ksep)\), and \(\rho_{E,\ell}\) is the \(\ell\)-adic one on the Tate module \(T_\ell(E)\).

Frobenius. In characteristic \(p\), \(\Frob\) is the relative Frobenius morphism and \(\Versch\) is its dual, the Verschiebung; \(\Frob^r\) is the \(p^r\)-th power Frobenius \(E \to E^{(p^r)}\), and over \(\F_q\) with \(q = p^e\) the \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism is \(\Frob^e\), also written \(\Frob\) when the ground field is fixed.

A road map

The notes fall into four parts.

  • Foundations (Chapters 1–4). Chapter 1 treats elliptic curves over \(\C\) as complex tori \(\C/\Lambda\), as a source of geometric intuition for what follows. Chapters 2 and 3 develop varieties and curves over an arbitrary field, and Chapter 4 uses them to set up the general theory: Weierstrass equations, the group law, isogenies, the Tate module and the Weil pairing.
  • Local theory (Chapters 5 and 6). Elliptic curves over finite fields, including the Hasse bound, and over local fields: minimal models, reduction and the formal group.
  • Global theory (Chapters 7–10). Galois cohomology, the Mordell–Weil theorem, integral points; twists, Selmer groups, and the Tate–Shafarevich group.
  • Further topics (Chapters 11–15). Complex multiplication, the Tate curve, the \(L\)-function and the conjecture of Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer, the Sato–Tate conjecture, and an introduction to modular curves.

A thread running through the notes is the comparison of an elliptic curve with the multiplicative group \(\Gm\): over \(\C\), the torus \(\C/\Lambda\) against \(\C^\times = \C/2\pi i\Z\); over an arbitrary field, their invariant differentials, torsion points and Tate modules; and in Chapter 12, the Tate curve, which realizes an elliptic curve over a \(p\)-adic field as a quotient of \(\Gm\).

Chapter 1 can be read independently of Chapters 2 and 3, and Chapter 4 relies on Chapters 2 and 3. After Chapter 4, the main line runs through Chapters 5 to 8; Chapters 9 and 10 build on Chapter 8, and each of the further topics can be read after the chapters it cites. The worksheets for the problem sessions, with their solutions, are linked from the web page of the notes; each one says which sections it draws on.
