Chapter 5Elliptic curves over finite fields

This chapter follows [Sil09, Chapter V] and [Poo06, Chapter 3], with [Mil22] for the Galois theory of finite fields, [AH17] for the Cartier operator, and [Voi21] for the mass formula.

Situation 5.0.1. Let \(p\) be a prime, let \(q \coloneqq p^e\) for some \(e \geq 1\), and let \(k \coloneqq \F_q\) be the finite field with \(q\) elements. Since \(k\) is perfect, \(\ksep = \kbar = \Fbar_q\).

Draft6 Oct 20265.1 The Frobenius automorphism

We are in Situation 5.0.1. The algebraic closure of \(k\) is

\begin{equation*} \Fbar_q = \varinjlim_{r} \F_{q^r}. \end{equation*}

Here \(\F_{q^r}\) is the unique subfield of \(\Fbar_q\) with \(q^r\) elements, and \(\F_{q^r} \subseteq \F_{q^s}\) if and only if \(r \mid s\). The direct limit is the union \(\bigcup_{r \geq 1} \F_{q^r}\).

The Galois group \(\Gal_{\F_q} = \Gal(\Fbar_q/\F_q)\) is topologically generated by the Frobenius automorphism

\begin{equation*} \sigma\colon \Fbar_q \to \Fbar_q, \qquad x \mapsto x^q, \end{equation*}

that is, the powers of \(\sigma\) are dense in \(\Gal_{\F_q} = \varprojlim_{r} \Gal(\F_{q^r}/\F_q)\).

In particular, for every \(r > 0\),

\begin{equation*} \Gal(\F_{q^r}/\F_q) = \langle \sigma|_{\F_{q^r}} \rangle \cong \Z/r\Z, \end{equation*}

the isomorphism given by \((n \bmod r) \mapsto (\sigma|_{\F_{q^r}})^n\). Assembling these maps for all \(r \geq 1\) gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-zhat} \Zhat \coloneqq \varprojlim_{r} \Z/r\Z \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \Gal_{\F_q}, \qquad (n_r \bmod r)_{r \geq 1} \mapsto \bigl((\sigma|_{\F_{q^r}})^{n_r}\bigr)_{r \geq 1}. \tag{5.1.1} \end{equation}

For all of this, see [Mil22].

Draft6 Oct 20265.2 Hasse's bound

Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\). We know from Theorem 4.1.6 that \(E\) is given by a Weierstrass equation (4.1.1), that is, by \(f(x, y) = 0\) with

\begin{equation*} f \coloneqq y^2 + a_1xy + a_3y - (x^3 + a_2x^2 + a_4x + a_6) \in \F_q[x, y], \end{equation*}

and that the set \(E(\F_q)\) of rational points contains at least one rational point: \(O \coloneqq (0 : 1 : 0)\).

On the other hand,

\begin{equation*} E(\F_q) = \{O\} \cup \{(x, y) \in \F_q^2 : f(x, y) = 0\}. \end{equation*}

If \(x \in \F_q\) is fixed, the polynomial \(f(x, y) \in \F_q[y]\) has at most two roots in \(\F_q\), so we obtain the trivial upper bound

\begin{equation*} \#E(\F_q) \leq 2q + 1. \end{equation*}

Let \(\Frob \in \End(E)\) be the \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism (Definition 3.2.11 and Example 4.3.3). On \(\Fbar_q\)-points it acts as the Frobenius automorphism \(\sigma\) on coordinates:

\begin{equation*} \Frob\colon E(\Fbar_q) \to E(\Fbar_q), \qquad O \mapsto O, \qquad (x, y) \mapsto (x^q, y^q). \end{equation*}

E. Artin conjectured, in his thesis, and H. Hasse proved the optimal upper bound.

Theorem 5.2.1 (Hasse). Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\). Then for every \(r \geq 1\),

\begin{equation*} \lvert \#E(\F_{q^r}) - (q^r + 1) \rvert \leq 2q^{r/2}. \end{equation*}
Proof.

(Sketch. See [Sil09, Theorem V.1.1].) There are three key observations that go into the proof of Hasse's bound.

  1. \(E(\F_{q^r}) = \ker(1 - \Frob^r)(\Fbar_q)\). Indeed, since \(\F_{q^r} = \Fbar_q^{\langle \sigma^r \rangle}\), we know that \(P \in E(\F_{q^r}) \iff \Frob^r(P) = P\).
  2. The map \(\deg\colon \End(E) \to \Z\) is a positive definite quadratic form (Corollary 4.3.11).

  3. For any abelian group \(A\) and any positive definite quadratic form \(d\colon A \to \Z\), the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

    \begin{equation*} \lvert d(a_1 - a_2) - d(a_1) - d(a_2) \rvert \leq 2\sqrt{d(a_1)\, d(a_2)}. \end{equation*}

To combine them: \(1 - \Frob^r\) is separable (Corollary 4.4.4, applied over \(\F_{q^r}\)), so observation 1 and Theorem 4.3.8 give \(\#E(\F_{q^r}) = \deg(1 - \Frob^r)\), and \(\deg \Frob^r = q^r\) (Proposition 3.2.12). Now apply observation 3 with \(d = \deg\), \(a_1 = 1\) and \(a_2 = \Frob^r\).

Draft6 Oct 20265.3 The Weil conjectures

In 1949, Weil made a series of remarkable conjectures about the numbers \(\#X(\F_{q^n})\), \(n \geq 1\), for a variety \(X\) over \(\F_q\) [Wei49]. Their proof was one of the main motivations for the scheme-theoretic foundations of algebraic geometry and for étale cohomology (Remark 5.3.6). In this section we state the conjectures for curves, check them on \(\PP^1\), and prove them for elliptic curves. We keep Situation 5.0.1, and \(C\) denotes a nice curve over \(\F_q\) (Definition 2.4.1).

5.3.1 The zeta function

Each set \(C(\F_{q^n})\) is finite, and we encode all of their sizes in a single generating function.

Definition 5.3.1. The zeta function of \(C/\F_q\) is the power series

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-zeta} Z(C/\F_q; T) \coloneqq \exp\left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty \#C(\F_{q^n}) \frac{T^n}{n} \right) \in \Q[[T]]. \tag{5.3.1} \end{equation}

Here \(\exp F(T) \coloneqq \sum_{m \geq 0} F(T)^m/m!\) for a power series \(F(T)\) with no constant term, so \(Z(C/\F_q; 0) = 1\). The zeta function determines the numbers \(\#C(\F_{q^n})\): \(\#C(\F_{q^n})\) is the coefficient of \(T^{n-1}\) in \(\frac{d}{dT} \log Z(C/\F_q; T)\). The exponential in \eqref{eq:ff-zeta} looks odd at first: the more natural series is \(\sum_n \#C(\F_{q^n}) T^n\). The choice makes \(Z(C/\F_q; T)\) an Euler product over the closed points of \(C\).

Proposition 5.3.2. For \(d \geq 1\), let \(N_d\) be the number of closed points of \(C\) of degree \(d\).

  1. Each \(N_d\) is finite, and \(\#C(\F_{q^n}) = \sum_{d \mid n} d N_d\) for every \(n \geq 1\).

  2. In \(\Z[[T]]\),

    \begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-zeta-euler} Z(C/\F_q; T) = \prod_{P} \left( 1 - T^{\deg P} \right)^{-1} = \prod_{d \geq 1} \left( 1 - T^d \right)^{-N_d}, \tag{5.3.2} \end{equation}

    where the first product ranges over the closed points \(P\) of \(C\). In particular \(Z(C/\F_q; T) \in 1 + T\Z[[T]]\).

  3. For every \(d \geq 1\), \(N_d = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{e \mid d} \mu(d/e)\, \#C(\F_{q^e})\), where \(\mu\) is the Möbius function.
Proof.

By Proposition 3.1.2, the points of \(C(\Fbar_q)\) above a closed point \(P\) form a single Galois orbit with \(\deg P\) elements, because \(\F_q\) is perfect. Such an \(\Fbar_q\)-point lies in \(C(\F_{q^n})\) exactly when the residue field \(\F_q(P) \cong \F_{q^{\deg P}}\) embeds in \(\F_{q^n}\), that is, when \(\deg P \mid n\). So \(N_d \leq \#C(\F_{q^d}) < \infty\), and \(C(\F_{q^n})\) is the disjoint union of the orbits of size \(d \mid n\), which proves part 1.

For part 2, only the finitely many factors with \(\deg P < m\) are \(\not\equiv 1 \pmod{T^m}\), so the product converges in \(\Z[[T]]\). Taking its logarithm and expanding \(-\log(1 - x) = \sum_{k \geq 1} x^k/k\),

\begin{equation*} \sum_{d \geq 1} N_d \sum_{k \geq 1} \frac{T^{dk}}{k} = \sum_{n \geq 1} \left( \sum_{d \mid n} d N_d \right) \frac{T^n}{n} = \sum_{n \geq 1} \#C(\F_{q^n}) \frac{T^n}{n}, \end{equation*}

where we set \(n = dk\). Part 3 is Möbius inversion of part 1, applied to \(n \mapsto \#C(\F_{q^n})\) and \(d \mapsto dN_d\).

So the numbers of rational points over the extensions of \(\F_q\) and the numbers of closed points of each degree determine each other. Substituting \(T = q^{-s}\), and writing \(NP \coloneqq \#\F_q(P) = q^{\deg P}\) for the norm of a closed point, gives

\begin{equation*} \zeta_C(s) \coloneqq Z(C/\F_q; q^{-s}) = \prod_P \left( 1 - NP^{-s} \right)^{-1}, \end{equation*}

which has the shape of the Euler product \(\zeta(s) = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}\) of the Riemann zeta function, with closed points in place of primes.

Example 5.3.3. Let \(C \coloneqq \PP^1\) over \(\F_q\). Then \(\#\PP^1(\F_{q^n}) = q^n + 1\), so

\begin{equation*} \log Z(\PP^1/\F_q; T) = \sum_{n \geq 1} \frac{T^n}{n} + \sum_{n \geq 1} \frac{(qT)^n}{n}, \qquad Z(\PP^1/\F_q; T) = \frac{1}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)} \end{equation*}

[Sil09, Example V.2.1]. The Euler product gives the same answer in another way. The closed points of \(\PP^1\) are the rational point \(\infty\), of degree \(1\), and the closed points of \(\AA^1\), which are the monic irreducible polynomials \(f \in \F_q[t]\), with \(\deg P = \deg f\). Since \(\F_q[t]\) is a unique factorization domain, expanding each factor of \eqref{eq:ff-zeta-euler} as a geometric series gives

\begin{equation*} (1 - T)^{-1} \prod_f \left( 1 - T^{\deg f} \right)^{-1} = \frac{1}{1 - T} \sum_{g \text{ monic}} T^{\deg g} = \frac{1}{1 - T} \sum_{d \geq 0} q^d T^d, \end{equation*}

because there are \(q^d\) monic polynomials of degree \(d\). Part 3 of Proposition 5.3.2 counts them: since \(\sum_{e \mid d} \mu(d/e) = 0\) for \(d > 1\), the \(+1\) in \(q^e + 1\) drops out, and the number of monic irreducible polynomials of degree \(d\) over \(\F_q\) is \(\frac{1}{d} \sum_{e \mid d} \mu(d/e) q^e\) for every \(d \geq 1\). Over \(\F_2\), in degree \(2\), this is \(\frac12(4 - 2) = 1\): the polynomial \(t^2 + t + 1\), whose roots form the Galois orbit \(\{\omega, \omega^2\}\) of a primitive cube root of unity in \(\F_4\).

The zeta function of \(\PP^1\) is a rational function, and this is no accident. The next lemma turns a formula for \(\#C(\F_{q^n})\) as a trace of \(n\)-th powers into a formula for \(Z(C/\F_q; T)\).

Lemma 5.3.4. Let \(A\) be a square matrix over a field \(K\) of characteristic \(0\). Then

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-exp-trace} \exp\left( \sum_{n \geq 1} \tr(A^n) \frac{T^n}{n} \right) = \frac{1}{\det(1 - AT)} \qquad \text{in } K[[T]]. \tag{5.3.3} \end{equation}
Proof.

(See [Sil09, Exercise 5.3].) Over an algebraic closure \(\bar K\), triangularize \(A\), with its eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m\) on the diagonal. Then \(\tr(A^n) = \sum_i \lambda_i^n\) and \(\det(1 - AT) = \prod_i (1 - \lambda_i T)\). Since \(\exp(F + G) = \exp F \exp G\), both sides of \eqref{eq:ff-exp-trace} are the products over \(i\) of the corresponding sides for the \(1 \times 1\) matrix \((\lambda_i)\), and for these

\begin{equation*} \sum_{n \geq 1} \lambda^n \frac{T^n}{n} = -\log(1 - \lambda T), \qquad \exp\bigl( -\log(1 - \lambda T) \bigr) = (1 - \lambda T)^{-1} \end{equation*}

in \(\bar K[[T]]\), which needs characteristic \(0\). Both sides of \eqref{eq:ff-exp-trace} have coefficients in \(K\).

For example, if there are \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r \in \C\) and signs \(\varepsilon_i \in \{\pm 1\}\) with \(\#C(\F_{q^n}) = \sum_i \varepsilon_i \alpha_i^n\) for all \(n \geq 1\), then Lemma 5.3.4, applied to the \(1 \times 1\) matrices \((\alpha_i)\), gives \(Z(C/\F_q; T) = \prod_{i=1}^r (1 - \alpha_i T)^{-\varepsilon_i}\).

5.3.2 The statement

Theorem 5.3.5 (Weil conjectures for curves). Let \(C\) be a nice curve of genus \(g\) over \(\F_q\) (Definition 3.5.3).

  1. (Rationality) There is a polynomial \(P \in 1 + T\Z[T]\) of degree \(2g\) such that

    \begin{equation*} Z(C/\F_q; T) = \frac{P(T)}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)}. \end{equation*}

  2. (Functional equation)

    \begin{equation*} Z\left(C/\F_q; \frac{1}{qT}\right) = q^{1-g} T^{2-2g} Z(C/\F_q; T). \end{equation*}

  3. (Riemann hypothesis) Over \(\C\),

    \begin{equation*} P(T) = \prod_{i=1}^{2g} (1 - \alpha_i T), \qquad \lvert \alpha_i \rvert = \sqrt{q} \quad \text{for every } i. \end{equation*}
Proof.

Rationality and the functional equation are due to F. K. Schmidt, from the Riemann–Roch theorem (Theorem 3.5.4). The Riemann hypothesis is due to Weil [Wei49]. See Remark 5.3.6. We prove the theorem for elliptic curves in Theorem 5.3.9.

Taking the logarithm of the formula for \(Z(C/\F_q; T)\), as in Example 5.3.3, the \(\alpha_i\) determine all the numbers \(\#C(\F_{q^n})\):

\begin{equation*} \#C(\F_{q^n}) = q^n + 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{2g} \alpha_i^n, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \bigl\lvert \#C(\F_{q^n}) - (q^n + 1) \bigr\rvert \leq 2g\, q^{n/2}. \end{equation*}

For \(C = \PP^1\), of genus \(0\), Example 5.3.3 gives the theorem with \(P = 1\).

Remark 5.3.6 (A brief and incomplete history). F. K. Schmidt proved rationality and the functional equation for curves. Hasse proved the Riemann hypothesis for elliptic curves in the 1930s, and Weil proved it for all curves, and for abelian varieties, using intersection theory on surfaces [Wei49].

Weil's conjectures were guided by the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem. If \(f\colon M \to M\) is a reasonable map of a compact manifold of dimension \(d\), then the number of fixed points of \(f\), counted with multiplicity, is

\begin{equation*} \sum_{i=0}^{d} (-1)^i \tr\left( f^* \mid H^i(M, \Q) \right). \end{equation*}

The set \(C(\F_{q^n})\) is exactly the fixed-point set of the \(n\)-th power of the Frobenius morphism on \(C(\Fbar_q)\). If \(C\) comes from a curve over \(\C\), then \(H^0\) and \(H^2\) of the Riemann surface have rank \(1\) and \(H^1\) has rank \(2g\). So one expects cohomology groups that behave like these but exist in characteristic \(p\), with \(\Frob\) acting by \(1\) on \(H^0\), by \(q\) on \(H^2\), and with eigenvalues \(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{2g}\) on \(H^1\). This gives the shape of Theorem 5.3.5.

Weil stated his conjectures for varieties of any dimension. Dwork proved rationality in 1960 using \(p\)-adic analysis [Dwo60]. The cohomology theory Weil was looking for is étale cohomology, developed by M. Artin, Grothendieck and others: its Grothendieck–Lefschetz trace formula proves every part of the conjectures except the Riemann hypothesis, which Deligne proved in 1973 [Del74]. See [Oor14].

5.3.3 Elliptic curves

We now prove Theorem 5.3.5 for elliptic curves. The result is Theorem 5.3.9. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\), let \(\Frob \in \End(E)\) be its \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism (Section 5.2), let \(\ell \neq p\) be a prime, and let \(\Frob_\ell\) be the map induced by \(\Frob\) on \(T_\ell(E)\) (Section 4.5). Then \(\deg \Frob = q\) by Proposition 3.2.12, and \(\Frob^n\) is the \(q^n\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism. So the discussion after Corollary 4.4.4, applied to \(E\) over \(\F_{q^n}\), gives

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-count-degree} \#E(\F_{q^n}) = \deg(1 - \Frob^n). \tag{5.3.4} \end{equation}

Definition 5.3.7. The trace of Frobenius of \(E/\F_q\) is \(a \coloneqq q + 1 - \#E(\F_q)\).

The name comes from Proposition 4.6.7 and \eqref{eq:ff-count-degree}: for every \(\ell \neq p\),

\begin{gather*} \tr \Frob_\ell = 1 + \deg \Frob - \deg(1 - \Frob) = 1 + q - \#E(\F_q) = a, \\ \det \Frob_\ell = \deg \Frob = q \end{gather*}

[Sil09, Remark V.2.6].

Theorem 5.3.8. Let \(E/\F_q\) be an elliptic curve with trace of Frobenius \(a\).

  1. The characteristic polynomial of \(\Frob_\ell\) is \(T^2 - aT + q\). Its complex roots \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are complex conjugates with \(\lvert\alpha\rvert = \lvert\beta\rvert = \sqrt{q}\), and for every \(n \geq 1\),

    \begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-count} \#E(\F_{q^n}) = q^n + 1 - \alpha^n - \beta^n. \tag{5.3.5} \end{equation}
  2. \(\Frob^2 - a\Frob + q = 0\) in \(\End(E)\).
Proof.

By the computation above, \(\det(T - \Frob_\ell) = T^2 - \tr(\Frob_\ell) T + \det \Frob_\ell = T^2 - aT + q\).

For part 1, let \(m/n \in \Q\). By Proposition 4.6.7 and Corollary 4.3.11,

\begin{equation*} \det\left( \frac{m}{n} - \Frob_\ell \right) = \frac{\det(m - n\Frob_\ell)}{n^2} = \frac{\deg(m - n\Frob)}{n^2} \geq 0. \end{equation*}

So the real quadratic polynomial \(T^2 - aT + q\) is non-negative on \(\Q\), hence on \(\R\), and its discriminant satisfies \(a^2 - 4q \leq 0\). Its roots are therefore complex conjugates or a repeated real root, so in either case \(\lvert\alpha\rvert^2 = \alpha\beta = q\). (The inequality \(a^2 \leq 4q\) is Hasse's bound, Theorem 5.2.1 with \(r = 1\).)

Viewing \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) in an algebraic closure of \(\Q_\ell\), we can triangularize \(\Frob_\ell\) with \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) on the diagonal. Then \((\Frob^n)_\ell = \Frob_\ell^n\) is triangular with \(\alpha^n\) and \(\beta^n\) on the diagonal. By \eqref{eq:ff-count-degree} and Proposition 4.6.7,

\begin{equation*} \#E(\F_{q^n}) = \deg(1 - \Frob^n) = \det(1 - \Frob_\ell^n) = (1 - \alpha^n)(1 - \beta^n) = 1 - \alpha^n - \beta^n + q^n. \end{equation*}

Since \(\alpha^n + \beta^n\) is a polynomial in \(a = \alpha + \beta\) and \(q = \alpha\beta\) with integer coefficients, it does not matter whether \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are read in \(\C\) or in \(\overline{\Q}_\ell\).

For part 2, let \(\psi \coloneqq \Frob^2 - a\Frob + q \in \End(E)\). By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, \(\Frob_\ell\) satisfies its characteristic polynomial, so \(\psi_\ell = \Frob_\ell^2 - a\Frob_\ell + q = 0\). By Theorem 4.5.6, \(\psi = 0\) [Sil09, Theorem V.2.3.1].

Theorem 5.3.8 recovers Hasse's bound over every extension at once: by \eqref{eq:ff-count},

\begin{equation*} \lvert \#E(\F_{q^n}) - (q^n + 1) \rvert = \lvert \alpha^n + \beta^n \rvert \leq \lvert\alpha\rvert^n + \lvert\beta\rvert^n = 2q^{n/2}, \end{equation*}

which is Theorem 5.2.1 with \(r = n\).

Theorem 5.3.9 (Weil conjectures for elliptic curves). Let \(E/\F_q\) be an elliptic curve with trace of Frobenius \(a\), and let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be as in Theorem 5.3.8. Then

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-zeta-ec} \begin{gathered} Z(E/\F_q; T) = \frac{\det(1 - \Frob_\ell T)}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)} = \frac{1 - aT + qT^2}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)}, \\ 1 - aT + qT^2 = (1 - \alpha T)(1 - \beta T), \qquad \lvert\alpha\rvert = \lvert\beta\rvert = \sqrt{q}, \end{gathered} \tag{5.3.6} \end{equation}

and

\begin{equation*} Z\left(E/\F_q; \frac{1}{qT}\right) = Z(E/\F_q; T). \end{equation*}
Proof.

For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(M\), \(\det(1 - M) = 1 - \tr M + \det M\). By \eqref{eq:ff-count-degree} and Proposition 4.6.7, and since \(\det \Frob_\ell^n = q^n\),

\begin{equation*} \#E(\F_{q^n}) = \deg(1 - \Frob^n) = \det(1 - \Frob_\ell^n) = 1 - \tr(\Frob_\ell^n) + q^n. \end{equation*}

So, working in \(\Q_\ell[[T]]\) and applying Lemma 5.3.4 to \(1\), \(q\) and \(\Frob_\ell\),

\begin{equation*} Z(E/\F_q; T) = \exp\left( \sum_{n \geq 1} (1 + q^n) \frac{T^n}{n} \right) \exp\left( -\sum_{n \geq 1} \tr(\Frob_\ell^n) \frac{T^n}{n} \right) = \frac{\det(1 - \Frob_\ell T)}{(1 - T)(1 - qT)}. \end{equation*}

By Theorem 5.3.8, \(\det(1 - \Frob_\ell T) = T^2 \det(T^{-1} - \Frob_\ell) = 1 - aT + qT^2 = (1 - \alpha T)(1 - \beta T)\), and \(\lvert\alpha\rvert = \lvert\beta\rvert = \sqrt{q}\). For the functional equation, substitute \(T \mapsto 1/(qT)\) and multiply numerator and denominator by \(qT^2\):

\begin{equation*} \frac{1 - a/(qT) + 1/(qT^2)}{(1 - 1/(qT))(1 - 1/T)} = \frac{qT^2 - aT + 1}{(qT - 1)(T - 1)} = Z(E/\F_q; T) \end{equation*}

[Sil09, Theorem V.2.4].

So \(E\) satisfies Theorem 5.3.5 with \(g = 1\) and \(P(T) = 1 - aT + qT^2 = \det(1 - \Frob_\ell T)\).

Corollary 5.3.10. Let \(E/\F_q\) be an elliptic curve with trace of Frobenius \(a\). Put \(a_n \coloneqq q^n + 1 - \#E(\F_{q^n})\) for \(n \geq 1\), and \(a_0 \coloneqq 2\). Then \(a_1 = a\) and

\begin{equation*} a_{n+2} = a\, a_{n+1} - q\, a_n \qquad \text{for all } n \geq 0. \end{equation*}

In particular, the single number \(\#E(\F_q)\) determines \(\#E(\F_{q^n})\) for every \(n \geq 1\).

Proof.

By \eqref{eq:ff-count}, \(a_n = \alpha^n + \beta^n\) for \(n \geq 1\), and also for \(n = 0\). Since \(\alpha\) is a root of \(T^2 - aT + q\), \(\alpha^{n+2} = a\alpha^{n+1} - q\alpha^n\), and likewise for \(\beta\). Add the two [Sil09, Exercise 5.13].

For instance \(a_2 = a^2 - 2q\), so \(\#E(\F_{q^2}) = q^2 + 1 - a^2 + 2q\).

Remark 5.3.11. To see why part 3 of Theorem 5.3.5 is called the Riemann hypothesis, set \(T = q^{-s}\) as in Section 5.3.1:

\begin{equation*} \zeta_E(s) \coloneqq Z(E/\F_q; q^{-s}) = \frac{1 - aq^{-s} + q^{1-2s}}{(1 - q^{-s})(1 - q^{1-s})}. \end{equation*}

The functional equation becomes \(\zeta_E(s) = \zeta_E(1 - s)\), and if \(\zeta_E(s) = 0\), then \(q^s \in \{\alpha, \beta\}\), so \(\lvert q^s \rvert = \sqrt{q}\), that is, \(\Re s = 1/2\) [Sil09, Remark V.2.5].

Example 5.3.12. Let \(E\colon y^2 + y = x^3 - x\) over \(\F_2\), the reduction modulo \(2\) of the curve with LMFDB label 37.a1, which has good reduction away from \(37\) [LMFDB]. For \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\) the equation reads \(y^2 + y = 0\), which has the two solutions \(y = 0, 1\). With the point at infinity, \(\#E(\F_2) = 5\), so \(a = 2 + 1 - 5 = -2\) and

\begin{equation*} Z(E/\F_2; T) = \frac{1 + 2T + 2T^2}{(1 - T)(1 - 2T)}. \end{equation*}

The roots of \(T^2 + 2T + 2\) are \(\alpha, \beta = -1 \pm i\), of absolute value \(\sqrt{2}\). Since \(\alpha^2 = -2i\), \(\alpha^3 = 2 + 2i\) and \(\alpha^4 = -4\), \eqref{eq:ff-count} predicts

\begin{equation*} \#E(\F_4) = 5, \qquad \#E(\F_8) = 9 - 4 = 5, \qquad \#E(\F_{16}) = 17 + 8 = 25. \end{equation*}

Magma agrees [BCP97]. It writes the denominator of the zeta function expanded. 

E := EllipticCurve([GF(2) | 0, 0, 1, -1, 0]);
TraceOfFrobenius(E);                        // -2
[#BaseChange(E, GF(2^n)) : n in [1..4]];    // [ 5, 5, 5, 25 ]
Z := ZetaFunction(E); K<T> := Parent(Z); Z;
// (2*T^2 + 2*T + 1)/(2*T^2 - 3*T + 1)

Example 5.3.13. Let \(E\colon y^2 = x^3 + x\) over \(\F_3\). The values of \(x^3 + x\) at \(x = 0, 1, 2\) are \(0, 2, 1\), and \(2\) is not a square in \(\F_3\), so \(E(\F_3) = \{O, (0, 0), (2, 1), (2, 2)\}\). Hence \(a = 0\),

\begin{equation*} Z(E/\F_3; T) = \frac{1 + 3T^2}{(1 - T)(1 - 3T)}, \end{equation*}

\(\alpha, \beta = \pm i\sqrt{3}\), and \(\#E(\F_{3^n}) = 3^n + 1\) for \(n\) odd and \(3^n + 1 - 2(-3)^{n/2}\) for \(n\) even. Part 2 of Theorem 5.3.8 says that \(\Frob^2 = [-3]\): applying the \(9\)-th power map to the coordinates of a point of \(E(\Fbar_3)\) is the same as multiplying it by \(-3\). We check this on a random point of \(E(\F_{3^5})\). 

E := EllipticCurve([GF(3) | 1, 0]);
TraceOfFrobenius(E);                        // 0
[#BaseChange(E, GF(3^n)) : n in [1..4]];    // [ 4, 16, 28, 64 ]
E5 := BaseChange(E, GF(3^5));
P := Random(E5);
E5![P[1]^9, P[2]^9, P[3]^9] eq -3*P;        // true

By Corollary 5.5.7, an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\) is supersingular if and only if \(a \equiv 0 \pmod p\). So this curve is supersingular.

Example 5.3.14. Let \(E\) be 37.a1 again, now reduced modulo the prime \(q = 1013\): \(E\colon y^2 + y = x^3 + 1012x\) over \(\F_{1013}\). Magma computes \(\#E(\F_{1013}) = 978\), so \(a = 36\) and

\begin{equation*} Z(E/\F_{1013}; T) = \frac{1 - 36T + 1013T^2}{(1 - T)(1 - 1013T)}. \end{equation*}

The roots of \(T^2 - 36T + 1013\) are \(18 \pm \sqrt{689}\,i \approx 18 \pm 26.2488i\), of absolute value \(\sqrt{1013} \approx 31.8277\). Then Corollary 5.3.10 predicts \(\#E(\F_{1013^2}) = q^2 + 1 - (a^2 - 2q) = 1026900\). 

q := 1013;
E := EllipticCurve([GF(q) | 0, 0, 1, -1, 0]);
#E, TraceOfFrobenius(E);                    // 978 36
#BaseChange(E, GF(q^2));                    // 1026900
Z := ZetaFunction(E); K<T> := Parent(Z); Z;
// (1013*T^2 - 36*T + 1)/(1013*T^2 - 1014*T + 1)

Proposition 5.3.15. Let \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) be elliptic curves over \(\F_q\). The following are equivalent:

  1. \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) are isogenous over \(\F_q\).
  2. \(\#E_1(\F_q) = \#E_2(\F_q)\).
  3. \(Z(E_1/\F_q; T) = Z(E_2/\F_q; T)\).
  4. \(E_1\) and \(E_2\) have the same number of closed points of each degree.
Proof.

(See [Sil09, Exercise 5.4].) Let \(\Frob_1\) and \(\Frob_2\) be the \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphisms of \(E_1\) and \(E_2\). Parts 2, 3 and 4 of Proposition 5.3.15 are equivalent by Theorem 5.3.9 (the zeta function depends only on \(q\) and \(a\)) and by parts 1 and 3 of Proposition 5.3.2.

Suppose that \(\varphi\colon E_1 \to E_2\) is a non-zero isogeny over \(\F_q\). Its coordinate functions have coefficients in \(\F_q\), which the \(q\)-th power map fixes, so \(\varphi \circ \Frob_1 = \Frob_2 \circ \varphi\), and hence \(\varphi \circ (1 - \Frob_1) = (1 - \Frob_2) \circ \varphi\). Taking degrees and cancelling \(\deg \varphi \neq 0\), \eqref{eq:ff-count-degree} gives \(\#E_1(\F_q) = \#E_2(\F_q)\).

Conversely, suppose that \(\#E_1(\F_q) = \#E_2(\F_q)\), so that \(\Frob_{1,\ell}\) and \(\Frob_{2,\ell}\) have the same characteristic polynomial \(f(T) = T^2 - aT + q\). By Tate's theorem, mentioned after Theorem 4.5.6, the map \(\Hom(E_1, E_2) \otimes \Z_\ell \to \Hom_{\Gal_{\F_q}}(T_\ell(E_1), T_\ell(E_2))\) is an isomorphism [Sil09, Theorem III.7.7(a)]. Since \(\sigma\) acts on \(E_i(\Fbar_q)\) as \(\Frob_i\) and topologically generates \(\Gal_{\F_q}\), a \(\Z_\ell\)-linear map is \(\Gal_{\F_q}\)-equivariant if and only if it intertwines \(\Frob_{1,\ell}\) and \(\Frob_{2,\ell}\). It suffices to find a non-zero such map, since then \(\Hom(E_1, E_2) \otimes \Z_\ell \neq 0\), and \(\Hom(E_1, E_2)\) is a free \(\Z\)-module (Theorem 4.5.6), so it is non-zero.

  • If \(a^2 \neq 4q\), then \(\Frob_{i,\ell}\) is not a scalar, so \(V_i \coloneqq T_\ell(E_i) \otimes \Q_\ell\) has a basis \(v, \Frob_{i,\ell} v\) and \(V_i \cong \Q_\ell[T]/(f)\), with \(T\) acting by \(\Frob_{i,\ell}\). An isomorphism \(V_1 \cong V_2\) of \(\Q_\ell[T]\)-modules, multiplied by a large power of \(\ell\), maps \(T_\ell(E_1)\) into \(T_\ell(E_2)\).
  • If \(a^2 = 4q\), then \(f(T) = (T - a/2)^2\) with \(a/2 \in \Z\), and by Proposition 4.6.7, \(\deg(\Frob_i - [a/2]) = \det(\Frob_{i,\ell} - a/2) = f(a/2) = 0\). So \(\Frob_i = [a/2]\), and every \(\Z_\ell\)-linear map intertwines the two.

Draft6 Oct 20265.4 The endomorphism ring

In this section and the next, \(k\) is a field of characteristic \(p > 0\), not necessarily finite, and \(E\) is an elliptic curve over \(k\). We use the Frobenius isogeny \(\Frob^r\colon E \to E^{(p^r)}\) and its Verschiebung \(\Versch^r = \widehat{\Frob^r}\) of Definition 4.3.12.

Over \(\F_q\) with \(q = p^e\) the two readings agree: \(E^{(p^e)} = E\), and the \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism is \(\Frob^e\), with dual \(\Versch^e\).

Corollary 4.5.1 left two possibilities for \(E[p^r](\kbar)\), and Theorem 4.7.8 three possibilities for \(\End(E_{\kbar})\). The following theorem of Deuring says that these are not independent: the \(p\)-torsion decides the endomorphism ring.

Theorem 5.4.1 (Supersingular and ordinary elliptic curves). Let \(k\) be a field of characteristic \(p\), and let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(k\). The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. \(E[p^r](\kbar) = 0\) for some (all) \(r \geq 1\).
  2. \(\Versch^r\) is purely inseparable for some (all) \(r \geq 1\).
  3. \([p]\colon E \to E\) is purely inseparable and \(j(E) \in \F_{p^2}\).
  4. \(\End(E_{\kbar})\) is an order in a quaternion algebra.
  5. The formal group \(\hat E\) has height \(2\).

If they hold, \(E\) is supersingular. Otherwise \(E\) is ordinary, and then

\begin{equation*} E[p^r](\kbar) \cong \Z/p^r\Z \qquad \text{for all } r \geq 1; \end{equation*}

if moreover \(j(E) \in \Fbar_p\), then \(\End(E_{\kbar})\) is an order in an imaginary quadratic field.

Proof.

This is [Sil09, Theorem V.3.1], due to Deuring [Deu41]. Two of the conditions are already familiar. For conditions 1 and 2, the proof of Corollary 4.5.1 gives \(\#E[p^r](\kbar) = \deg_s[p^r] = \deg_s \Versch^r\), because \(\Frob^r\) is purely inseparable and \([p^r] = \Versch^r \circ \Frob^r\). So the \(p^r\)-torsion vanishes exactly when \(\Versch^r\) has separable degree \(1\). Condition 4 is case 3 of Theorem 4.7.8, and the ordinary case is case 2. Compare Remark 4.7.12.

By condition 3 of Theorem 5.4.1 there are only finitely many supersingular elliptic curves over \(\Fbar_p\) up to isomorphism, since each has \(j\)-invariant in the finite field \(\F_{p^2}\). Counting them is the subject of Theorem 5.5.10.

Draft6 Oct 20265.5 The Cartier operator and the Hasse invariant

None of the conditions of Theorem 5.4.1 is easy to check on a given Weierstrass equation. The Cartier operator turns the dichotomy into the vanishing of a single coefficient.

Situation 5.5.1. In this section \(k\) is a perfect field of characteristic \(p > 0\), not necessarily finite, so that \(a \mapsto a^p\) is an automorphism of \(k\) and we may write \(a^{1/p}\) for its inverse.

Perfectness is also what makes the definition below work at every closed point: it guarantees \(dt \neq 0\) for a uniformizer \(t\), which fails over an imperfect field (Warning 3.4.5).

Definition 5.5.2. In Situation 5.5.1, let \(C\) be a nice curve of genus \(g \geq 1\) over \(k\), let \(P\) be a closed point of \(C\) and let \(t\) be a uniformizer at \(P\). The functions \(1, t, \dots, t^{p-1}\) form a \(p\)-basis of \(\calO_{C,P}\): every differential regular at \(P\) is uniquely

\begin{equation*} \omega = \left( \sum_{j=0}^{p-1} f_j^p\, t^j \right) dt, \qquad f_j \in \calO_{C,P}. \end{equation*}

The Cartier operator is

\begin{equation*} \calC\colon \Omega_{k(C)/k} \to \Omega_{k(C)/k}, \qquad \calC(\omega) \coloneqq f_{p-1}\,dt. \end{equation*}

The value \(\calC(\omega)\) does not depend on the uniformizer, the local definitions agree on overlaps, and the result extends from differentials regular at \(P\) to all of \(\Omega_{k(C)/k}\). See [AH17, Section 2.2], which follows [Ser58, Section 10]. The operator is due to Cartier [Car57]. There is a second description, the one used in computations: if \(x \in k(C)\) is a separating element, so that \(dx\) is a basis of \(\Omega_{k(C)/k}\) (Proposition 3.4.2), then every differential is uniquely

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-cartier-global} \omega = \left( \sum_{j=0}^{p-1} g_j^p\, x^j \right) dx, \qquad g_j \in k(C), \qquad \text{and} \qquad \calC(\omega) = g_{p-1}\,dx. \tag{5.5.1} \end{equation}

Proposition 5.5.3. In Situation 5.5.1, let \(C\) be a nice curve of genus \(g \geq 1\) over \(k\).

  1. \(\calC(\omega_1 + \omega_2) = \calC(\omega_1) + \calC(\omega_2)\).
  2. \(\calC(f^p\omega) = f\,\calC(\omega)\) for \(f \in k(C)\). In particular \(\calC(c\,\omega) = c^{1/p}\,\calC(\omega)\) for \(c \in k\), so \(\calC\) is semilinear for the inverse of the \(p\)-th power map on \(k\).
  3. \(\calC(df) = 0\) for \(f \in k(C)\).
  4. If \(\omega\) is regular, then so is \(\calC(\omega)\). So \(\calC\) restricts to an operator on the space of regular differentials of \(C\), which has dimension \(g\).
Proof.

Parts 1 to 3 are immediate from \eqref{eq:ff-cartier-global}, and part 4 is the statement that \(\calC\) acts on \(H^0(C, \Omega_{C/k})\). See [AH17, Section 2.2] and [Ser58, Section 10]. Dividing by a fixed non-zero regular differential identifies the space of regular differentials with \(\mcL(K_C)\), whose dimension is the genus (Definition 3.5.3).

Note that the semilinearity in part 2 is for the inverse of the \(p\)-th power map, not for the Frobenius automorphism \(\sigma\colon x \mapsto x^q\) of Section 5.1. The two agree only when \(q = p\).

For an elliptic curve the space of regular differentials is one-dimensional, spanned by the invariant differential \(\omega\), so \(\calC\) acts on it by a single scalar. That scalar is the Hasse invariant.

Definition 5.5.4. Let \(q\) be a power of \(p \geq 3\) and let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\), given by \(y^2 = f(x)\) with \(f \in \F_q[x]\) a cubic with distinct roots in \(\Fbar_q\) (Lemma 4.1.4), with invariant differential \(\omega = dx/(2y)\). Write

\begin{equation*} f(x)^{(p-1)/2} = \sum_{m \geq 0} c_m x^m. \end{equation*}

The Hasse invariant of the equation is \(A \coloneqq c_{p-1}\), the coefficient of \(x^{p-1}\) in \(f(x)^{(p-1)/2}\).

The Hasse invariant is exactly the scalar by which the Cartier operator acts. Indeed, using \(y^2 = f(x)\),

\begin{equation*} \frac{dx}{y} = \frac{(y^2)^{(p-1)/2}}{y^p}\,dx = y^{-p} f(x)^{(p-1)/2}\,dx = y^{-p} \left( \sum_{m \geq 0} c_m x^m \right) dx, \end{equation*}

so part 2 of Proposition 5.5.3 and \eqref{eq:ff-cartier-global} give

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-cartier-omega} \calC\!\left( \frac{dx}{y} \right) = \frac{1}{y} \, \calC\!\left( \sum_{m \geq 0} c_m x^m \, dx \right) = A^{1/p}\,\frac{dx}{y}. \tag{5.5.2} \end{equation}

Only the terms with \(m \equiv p - 1 \pmod p\) survive, and \(f^{(p-1)/2}\) has degree \(3(p-1)/2 < 2p - 1\), so \(m = p - 1\) is the only one. Since \(2 \in \F_p\) is fixed by \(c \mapsto c^{1/p}\), the same scalar appears for \(\omega = dx/(2y)\): \(\calC(\omega) = A^{1/p}\,\omega\). This is the genus-\(1\) case of the computation in [AH17, Section 3].

Remark 5.5.5. Three warnings. First, \(A\) depends on the equation and not only on \(E\): the change of coordinates \((x, y) \mapsto (u^2x, u^3y)\) of (4.1.3) replaces \(A\) by \(u^{-(p-1)}A\). What is intrinsic is whether \(A\) vanishes, equivalently whether \(\calC = 0\) on the regular differentials of \(E\). Second, the definition asks for \(p \geq 3\), although the Cartier operator itself does not. Over \(\Fbar_2\) the only supersingular elliptic curve is \(y^2 + y = x^3\) [Sil09, Section V.4]. Third, Silverman [Sil09] uses "Hasse invariant" for the binary invariant of Theorem 5.4.1, which is \(0\) for a supersingular curve and \(1\) for an ordinary one. Here it is the scalar \(A\) attached to an equation.

The proof that \(A\) detects supersingularity counts points with the quadratic character.

Lemma 5.5.6. Let \(q\) be a power of \(p \geq 3\), let \(\chi\colon \F_q^\times \to \{\pm 1\}\) be the unique character of order \(2\), extended by \(\chi(0) \coloneqq 0\), and let \(E/\F_q\) be given by \(y^2 = f(x)\) as in Definition 5.5.4. Then

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-character-sum} \#E(\F_q) = 1 + q + \sum_{x \in \F_q} \chi(f(x)). \tag{5.5.3} \end{equation}
Proof.

For a fixed \(x \in \F_q\), the number of \(y \in \F_q\) with \(y^2 = f(x)\) is \(2\) if \(f(x)\) is a non-zero square, \(1\) if \(f(x) = 0\) and \(0\) otherwise, that is, \(1 + \chi(f(x))\). Summing over the \(q\) values of \(x\) and adding the point at infinity gives \eqref{eq:ff-character-sum} [Sil09, Application V.1.3].

Corollary 5.5.7. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\), \(q = p^e\), with trace of Frobenius \(a\) (Definition 5.3.7). Then \(E\) is supersingular if and only if \(a \equiv 0 \pmod p\).

Proof.

Write \(\Frob \in \End(E)\) for the \(q\)-th power Frobenius endomorphism. It is the \(e\)-fold composite of the \(p\)-th power Frobenius, so its dual is \(\Versch^e\) in the notation of Definition 4.3.12. By part 2 of Theorem 5.3.8 and \(\widehat{\Frob} \circ \Frob = [q]\),

\begin{equation*} \bigl( \Frob - [a] + \widehat{\Frob} \bigr) \circ \Frob = \Frob^2 - a\Frob + q = 0, \end{equation*}

and \(\End(E)\) has no zero divisors (Proposition 4.3.7) with \(\Frob \neq 0\), so \(\Versch^e = \widehat{\Frob} = [a] - \Frob\). By Corollary 4.4.4, \([a] - \Frob\) is separable if and only if \(p \nmid a\). Finally \(\deg_s \Versch^e = \#E[p^e](\kbar) \in \{1, p^e\}\) by part 2 of Corollary 4.5.1, so \(\Versch^e\) is inseparable if and only if it is purely inseparable, and part 2 of Theorem 5.4.1, with \(r = e\), finishes the proof [Sil09, Exercise 5.10].

Theorem 5.5.8. Let \(q\) be a power of \(p \geq 3\) and let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\F_q\), given by \(y^2 = f(x)\) as in Definition 5.5.4, with Hasse invariant \(A\). For \(r \geq 1\), let \(A_{p^r}\) be the coefficient of \(x^{p^r-1}\) in \(f(x)^{(p^r-1)/2}\), so that \(A_p = A\). The following are equivalent.

  1. \(E\) is supersingular.
  2. \(A_q = 0\).
  3. \(A = 0\).
  4. \(\calC = 0\) on the regular differentials of \(E\).
Proof.

This is [Sil09, Theorem V.4.1(a)]. The argument is Manin's [Man61].

Since \(\F_q^\times\) is cyclic of order \(q - 1\), we have \(\chi(z) = z^{(q-1)/2}\) in \(\F_q\) for every \(z \in \F_q\), including \(z = 0\). So Lemma 5.5.6 reads

\begin{equation*} \#E(\F_q) = 1 + \sum_{x \in \F_q} f(x)^{(q-1)/2} \qquad \text{in } \F_q, \end{equation*}

using \(q = 0\) in \(\F_q\). Again because \(\F_q^\times\) is cyclic, \(\sum_{x \in \F_q} x^n\) is \(-1\) when \(n > 0\) and \(q - 1 \mid n\), and \(0\) otherwise. The polynomial \(f(x)^{(q-1)/2}\) has degree \(3(q-1)/2 < 2(q-1)\), so the only surviving term is the one with \(n = q - 1\), and \(\#E(\F_q) = 1 - A_q\) in \(\F_q\). Comparing with \(\#E(\F_q) = q + 1 - a\) gives \(a \equiv A_q \pmod p\), so conditions 1 and 2 are equivalent by Corollary 5.5.7.

For conditions 2 and 3, write

\begin{equation*} f(x)^{(p^{r+1}-1)/2} = \left( f(x)^{(p^r-1)/2} \right)^p f(x)^{(p-1)/2}. \end{equation*}

A term of the right-hand side contributing to \(x^{p^{r+1}-1}\) is \(x^{pn}\) times \(x^m\) with \(pn + m = p^{r+1} - 1\), so \(m \equiv p - 1 \pmod p\). Since \(m \leq 3(p-1)/2 < 2p - 1\), the only possibility is \(m = p - 1\), and then \(n = p^r - 1\). Hence \(A_{p^{r+1}} = A_{p^r}^p\, A\), and by induction \(A_q = 0\) if and only if \(A = 0\).

For conditions 3 and 4, the regular differentials of \(E\) are the \(\F_q\)-multiples of \(\omega\), and \(\calC(\omega) = A^{1/p}\omega\) by \eqref{eq:ff-cartier-omega}.

Example 5.5.9. Let \(E\colon y^2 = x^3 - x\), and write \(m \coloneqq (p-1)/2\). Then \(f^{(p-1)/2} = x^m (x^2-1)^m\), whose exponents are all congruent to \(m\) modulo \(2\). So the coefficient \(A\) of \(x^{p-1} = x^{2m}\) vanishes unless \(m\) is even, that is, unless \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). And when \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) the one surviving term gives \(A = (-1)^{m/2}\binom{m}{m/2}\), which is non-zero in \(\F_p\) because \(\binom{m}{m/2}\) is a quotient of factorials of integers smaller than \(p\). Hence \(E\) is supersingular exactly when \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\). For \(p = 11\) we get \(A = 0\), and for \(p = 13\), \(A = (-1)^3\binom{6}{3} = -20 = 6\) in \(\F_{13}\). Magma agrees [BCP97], and lists the supersingular \(j\)-invariants of each characteristic. Note \(1728 = 1\) in \(\F_{11}\). 

for p in [11, 13] do
  P<x> := PolynomialRing(GF(p));
  f := x^3 - x;
  E := EllipticCurve([GF(p) | -1, 0]);
  A := Coefficient(f^((p-1) div 2), p-1);
  print p, A, IsSupersingular(E);
  print [ j : j in GF(p^2) | IsSupersingular(EllipticCurveFromjInvariant(j)) ];
end for;
// 11 0 true
// [ 0, 1 ]
// 13 6 false
// [ 5 ]

Both lists are as short as part 3 of Theorem 5.4.1 promises, and they satisfy the mass formula of Theorem 5.5.10: in characteristic \(11\) the two supersingular curves have \(j = 0\) and \(j = 1728\), so \(\#\Aut(E)\) is \(6\) and \(4\) by Theorem 4.7.11, and \(1/6 + 1/4 = 5/12 = (11-1)/24\); in characteristic \(13\) the only one has \(j = 5\), with \(\#\Aut(E) = 2\) and \(1/2 = (13-1)/24\).

We close with a count of the supersingular curves, weighted by their automorphisms.

Theorem 5.5.10 (Mass formula). Let \(p\) be a prime. Then

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ff-deuring-mass} \sum_{E} \frac{1}{\#\Aut(E)} = \frac{p - 1}{24}, \tag{5.5.4} \end{equation}

where \(E\) runs over the isomorphism classes of supersingular elliptic curves over \(\Fbar_p\).

Proof.

Due to Deuring [Deu41]. See [Voi21, (42.3.9)] and [Sil09, Exercise 5.9]. Remark 5.5.11 explains what a proof needs.

Remark 5.5.11. The mass formula does not follow from Theorems 5.4.1 and 5.5.8 alone: those say which curves are supersingular, not how many there are. Two routes to \eqref{eq:ff-deuring-mass} are worth recording.

The first is analytic, and is the one that explains the denominator. By part condition 4 of Theorem 5.4.1 the endomorphism ring of a supersingular curve is an order in a quaternion algebra, and Deuring's correspondence [Voi21, Corollary 42.3.7] makes this precise: the supersingular elliptic curves over \(\Fbar_p\), up to isomorphism, are in bijection with the class set of a maximal order \(\calO\) in the definite quaternion algebra over \(\Q\) ramified exactly at \(p\) and \(\infty\), and under the bijection \(\Aut(E)\) is the unit group of the corresponding order. The count on the quaternionic side is the Eichler mass formula [Voi21, Theorem 25.1.1]: for a maximal order in a definite quaternion algebra over \(\Q\) of discriminant \(D\),

\begin{equation*} \sum_{[J]} \frac{1}{w_J} = \frac{\varphi(D)}{12}, \qquad w_J \coloneqq \#\left( \calO_L(J)^\times/\{\pm 1\} \right), \end{equation*}

which for \(D = p\) gives \((p-1)/12\). The quotient by \(\{\pm 1\}\) accounts for the factor \(2\) between that and \eqref{eq:ff-deuring-mass}. The Eichler mass formula is proved by analytic means, from the analytic class number formula and the zeta function of the order [Voi21, Main Theorem 26.1.5 and Section 26.2]. Over \(\Q\) it goes back to Hey and to Brandt.

The second route is a count. The supersingular \(j\)-invariants in characteristic \(p\) are the roots of an explicit polynomial, and there are \(\lfloor p/12 \rfloor + \varepsilon\) of them, with \(\varepsilon \in \{0, 1, 2\}\) depending on \(p \bmod 12\) [Sil09, Theorem V.4.1]. This is due to Igusa [Igu58]. Weighting each by \(\#\Aut(E)\), which is \(2\), \(4\) or \(6\) according as \(j(E) \neq 0, 1728\), \(j(E) = 1728\) or \(j(E) = 0\) when \(p \geq 5\) (Theorem 4.7.11), and checking \(p = 2, 3\) by hand, gives \eqref{eq:ff-deuring-mass} case by case.
