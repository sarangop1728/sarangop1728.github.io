Chapter 3Algebraic curves

This chapter follows [Poo06, Chapter 2] and [Sil09, Chapter II], with material on plane curves from [Mil20, Sections I.1 and I.6]. Unlike these sources, we do not assume that the ground field is perfect.

Definition 3.0.1. A curve over \(k\) is an integral \(k\)-variety of dimension \(1\). A nice curve is a curve that is nice (Definition 2.4.1).

Situation 3.0.2. We keep Situation 2.0.1: \(k\) is an arbitrary field, and \(\Gal_k \coloneqq \Gal(\ksep/k)\). Unless stated otherwise, \(C\), \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) denote nice curves over \(k\).

Remarks point out where a curve that is only regular behaves differently. The basic example is Example 2.4.6.

Draft6 Oct 20263.1 Closed points and local rings

Definition 3.1.1. A closed point of a \(k\)-variety \(X\) is a point whose residue field \(k(P)\) is a finite extension of \(k\). Its degree is \(\deg P \coloneqq [k(P) : k]\) [Poo06, Definition 2.2.1].

The rational points of \(X\) are the closed points of degree \(1\).

Proposition 3.1.2. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety. Sending a \(\kbar\)-point to its image is a surjection from \(X(\kbar)\) onto the set of closed points of \(X\), whose fibers are the \(\Aut(\kbar/k)\)-orbits. The fiber over \(P\) has \([k(P) : k]_s\) elements, and it meets \(X(\ksep)\) if and only if \(k(P)/k\) is separable.

Proof.

A \(\kbar\)-point over \(P\) is the same as a \(k\)-embedding \(k(P) \hookrightarrow \kbar\). There are \([k(P) : k]_s\) of these, permuted transitively by \(\Aut(\kbar/k)\), and they land in \(\ksep\) exactly when \(k(P)/k\) is separable.

Example 3.1.3. On \(E\colon y^2 = x^3 + 17\) over \(\Q\), the closed point \(P \coloneqq V(x, y^2 - 17)\) has residue field \(\Q(\sqrt{17})\) and degree \(2\). It corresponds to the orbit \(\{(0, \sqrt{17}), (0, -\sqrt{17})\}\) in \(E(\Qbar)\).

Example 3.1.4. Let \(k \coloneqq \F_p(t)\). The closed point \(P \coloneqq V(x^p - t)\) of \(\AA^1_k\) has degree \(p\) and residue field \(k(t^{1/p})\). It has a single \(\kbar\)-point above it, and no \(\ksep\)-point.

Proposition 3.1.5. Let \(X\) be a curve over \(k\), and \(P\) a closed point. The local ring \(\calO_{X,P}\) is a discrete valuation ring if and only if \(X\) is regular at \(P\). In particular this holds at every closed point of a nice curve.

Proof.

A noetherian local domain of dimension \(1\) is a discrete valuation ring if and only if its maximal ideal is principal, i.e. it is regular [Sil09, Proposition II.1.1 and Exercise 2.1].

Definition 3.1.6. Let \(P\) be a closed point of \(C\). The normalized valuation of \(\calO_{C,P}\) extends to \(\ord_P\colon k(C)^\times \to \Z\). A uniformizer at \(P\) is a \(t \in k(C)\) with \(\ord_P(t) = 1\). If \(\ord_P(f) > 0\), then \(f\) has a zero at \(P\); if \(\ord_P(f) < 0\), a pole. If \(\ord_P(f) \geq 0\), then \(f\) is regular at \(P\), with value \(f(P) \in k(P)\) [Sil09, Section II.1].

Proposition 3.1.7. Let \(f \in k(C)^\times\). Then \(f\) has only finitely many zeros and poles. If \(f\) has no poles, then \(f \in k\).

Proof.

See [Sil09, Proposition II.1.2]. For the last statement over \(k\), note that \(k\) is algebraically closed in \(k(C)\), since \(C\) is geometrically integral (Proposition 2.2.4).

Example 3.1.8. Let \(f(x, y) = 0\) be an affine patch of \(C\), and \(P = (a, b) \in C(k)\). If \(\partial f/\partial x(P) \neq 0\), then \(y - b\) is a uniformizer at \(P\). If \(\partial f/\partial y(P) \neq 0\), then \(x - a\) is [Poo06, Example 1.8.5]. For instance, on \(y^2 = x^3 + x\) with \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), the function \(y\) is a uniformizer at \(P = (0, 0)\). Since \(x = y^2/(x^2 + 1)\), we get \(\ord_P(x) = 2\), and \(\ord_P(2y^2 - x) = \ord_P\bigl(x(2x^2 + 1)\bigr) = 2\) [Sil09, Example II.1.3].

Proposition 3.1.9. Let \(P \in C(k)\), and let \(t\) be a uniformizer at \(P\). Then \(k(C)\) is a finite separable extension of \(k(t)\).

Proof.

[Sil09, Proposition II.1.4] proves this for perfect \(k\), by taking \(p\)-th roots of coefficients. In general: since \(k(P) = k\), the map \(\mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 \to \Omega_{C/k, P} \otimes k(P)\) is an isomorphism [Har77, Proposition II.8.7]. So \(dt\) generates the stalk \(\Omega_{C/k,P}\), and in particular \(dt \neq 0\) in \(\Omega_{k(C)/k}\). Conclude by Proposition 3.4.2.

The hypothesis \(P \in C(k)\) cannot be dropped: see Warning 3.4.5.

Draft6 Oct 20263.2 Morphisms of curves

Proposition 3.2.1. Let \(X\) be a curve over \(k\), let \(P\) be a closed point at which \(X\) is regular, and let \(Y \subseteq \PP^n_k\) be a projective variety. Then every rational map \(\varphi\colon X \dashrightarrow Y\) is regular at \(P\). In particular, if \(X\) is regular, then \(\varphi\) is a morphism.

Proof.

Write \(\varphi = (f_0 : \dots : f_n)\), let \(t\) be a uniformizer at \(P\) and \(m \coloneqq \min_i \ord_P(f_i)\). Then every \(t^{-m}f_i\) is regular at \(P\), and some \((t^{-m}f_i)(P) \in k(P)\) is non-zero [Sil09, Proposition II.2.1]. The argument only uses that \(\calO_{X,P}\) is a discrete valuation ring, so it applies to closed points of any degree.

If \(f\colon X \to Y\) is a holomorphic map between Riemann surfaces and \(X\) is compact, then \(f\) is either constant or surjective. The algebraic analog is the following.

Proposition 3.2.2. A morphism from a smooth projective curve to a curve is either constant or surjective.

Proof (Har77).

See [Har77, Proposition II.6.8].

Theorem 3.2.3. The functor \(X \mapsto k(X)\) is an equivalence between the category of regular projective curves over \(k\) with non-constant morphisms, and the opposite of the category of finitely generated extensions \(K/k\) of transcendence degree \(1\) with \(k\)-embeddings. Under this equivalence, \(X\) is geometrically integral if and only if \(k\) is algebraically closed in \(k(X)\) and \(k(X)/k\) is separable.

Proof.

See [Poo06, Section 2.1.2 and Remark 2.1.3] and [Mil20, Theorem I.6.3] for the equivalence. The last statement follows from Proposition 2.2.4. If \(k(X)/k\) is separable, an element of \(k(X)\) that is algebraic over \(k\) is separable over \(k\).

Remark 3.2.4. [Sil09, Remark II.2.5] states the equivalence for smooth curves and function fields in which \(k\) is algebraically closed. Over an imperfect field, the regular projective model of such a function field need not be smooth. The function field \(\F_3(t)(x, y)\), with \(y^2 = x^3 - t\), satisfies both conditions of Theorem 3.2.3, but its regular projective model is the curve of Example 2.4.6, which is not smooth. So no nice curve has this function field.

Definition 3.2.5. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be a non-constant morphism, and \(\varphi^*\colon k(C_2) \hookrightarrow k(C_1)\) the induced embedding. The degree of \(\varphi\) is \(\deg\varphi \coloneqq [k(C_1) : \varphi^* k(C_2)]\). A constant morphism has degree \(0\). The morphism \(\varphi\) is separable, inseparable or purely inseparable if the extension \(k(C_1)/\varphi^*k(C_2)\) is, and \(\deg_s \varphi\) and \(\deg_i \varphi\) are its separable and inseparable degrees [Sil09, Section II.2], [Poo06, Definition 2.1.4]. The norm gives \(\varphi_* \coloneqq (\varphi^*)^{-1} \circ \Nm_{k(C_1)/\varphi^*k(C_2)}\colon k(C_1) \to k(C_2)\).

Corollary 3.2.6. A morphism of degree \(1\) between regular projective curves is an isomorphism.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem 3.2.3. Compare [Sil09, Corollary II.2.4.1].

Definition 3.2.7. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be non-constant, \(P\) a closed point of \(C_1\), and \(Q \coloneqq \varphi(P)\) with uniformizer \(t_Q\). The ramification index of \(\varphi\) at \(P\) is \(e_\varphi(P) \coloneqq \ord_P(\varphi^* t_Q) \geq 1\), and the residue degree is \(f_\varphi(P) \coloneqq [k(P) : k(Q)]\). The morphism \(\varphi\) is unramified at \(P\) if \(e_\varphi(P) = 1\) [Sil09, Section II.2].

Proposition 3.2.8. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be non-constant.

  1. For every closed point \(Q\) of \(C_2\), \(\sum_{P \mapsto Q} e_\varphi(P) f_\varphi(P) = \deg\varphi\).
  2. For all but finitely many \(Q \in C_2(\kbar)\), the fiber of \(\varphi_{\kbar}\) over \(Q\) has \(\deg_s\varphi\) elements.
  3. If \(\psi\colon C_2 \to C_3\) is non-constant, then \(e_{\psi\circ\varphi}(P) = e_\varphi(P)\,e_\psi(\varphi(P))\).
Proof.

Over \(\kbar\) all residue degrees are \(1\), and this is [Sil09, Proposition II.2.6]. Over \(k\), part 1 is the formula \(\sum e_i f_i = n\) for the finite extension of Dedekind domains \(\calO_{C_2,Q} \subseteq \calO_{C_1}\) above \(Q\) [Poo06, Remark 2.5.3].

Example 3.2.9. The morphism \(\varphi\colon \PP^1_k \to \PP^1_k\) given by \(\varphi = (X^3(X - Y)^2 : Y^5)\) has degree \(5\). The fiber over \((0 : 1)\) consists of \((0 : 1)\), with \(e_\varphi = 3\), and \((1 : 1)\), with \(e_\varphi = 2\). And indeed \(3 + 2 = 5\) [Sil09, Example II.2.9].

Example 3.2.10. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). Let \(E\colon y^2 = p(x)\) with \(p\) a separable cubic, a smooth plane cubic by Example 3.7.9 below, and let \(O = (0 : 1 : 0)\). The morphism \(x\colon E \to \PP^1_k\) has degree \(2\), since \(k(E) = k(x)(y)\) with \(y^2 \in k(x)\), and it is separable. Above \(\infty\) lies only \(O\), with \(e_x(O) = 2\). Above the closed point of \(\AA^1_k\) given by an irreducible factor \(g\) of \(p\) lies only the closed point \(V(g, y)\), with ramification index \(2\). All other closed points are unramified. In characteristic \(2\) the extension \(k(x, y)/k(x)\) defined by \(y^2 = p(x)\) is purely inseparable [Mil20, Example I.4.25], but in that case this curve is singular, again by Example 3.7.9. See Example 3.6.4 instead.

Definition 3.2.11. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k = p > 0\), and let \(q = p^r\). Let \(C \subseteq \PP^n_k\) be defined by homogeneous polynomials \(G_1, \dots, G_m\), and let \(C^{(q)}\) be defined by the polynomials \(G_i^{(q)}\) obtained by raising every coefficient to the \(q\)-th power. Equivalently, \(C^{(q)}\) is the base change of \(C\) along \(k \to k\), \(a \mapsto a^q\). The relative Frobenius morphism is

\begin{equation*} \Frob\colon C \to C^{(q)}, \qquad (X_0 : \dots : X_n) \mapsto (X_0^q : \dots : X_n^q). \end{equation*}

It lands in \(C^{(q)}\) because \(G^{(q)}(X_0^q, \dots, X_n^q) = G(X_0, \dots, X_n)^q\) [Sil09, Section II.2].

Proposition 3.2.12. Let \(\Frob\colon C \to C^{(q)}\) be the relative Frobenius morphism.

  1. \(\Frob^* k(C^{(q)}) = k \cdot k(C)^q\), the compositum of \(k\) and \(k(C)^q\) in \(k(C)\).
  2. \(\Frob\) is purely inseparable of degree \(q\).
Proof.

For perfect \(k\), part 1 reads \(\Frob^*k(C^{(q)}) = k(C)^q\), and this is [Sil09, Proposition II.2.11]. In general, suppose \(X_0\) does not vanish on \(C\), and let \(x_i = X_i/X_0\) be the affine coordinates on the patch \(U_0\). Then \(k(C)\) and \(k(C^{(q)})\) are generated over \(k\) by the \(x_i\), and \(\Frob^*(x_i) = x_i^q\). Since \(\Frob^*\) is \(k\)-linear, its image is \(k(x_i^q)\). This equals \(k \cdot k(C)^q\): for \(g \in k(C)\), written as a rational function in the \(x_i\) with coefficients in \(k\), we have \(g^q = g^{(q)}(x_i^q)\).

For part 2, pure inseparability follows from part 1. By induction on \(r\) it suffices to treat \(q = p\). Since \(C\) is geometrically reduced, \(k(C)/k\) is separably generated (Proposition 2.2.4): choose \(t \in k(C)\) with \(k(C)/k(t)\) finite separable. A separable algebraic extension \(L/K\) satisfies \(L = K L^p\), so \(k(C) = (k \cdot k(C)^p)(t)\), and \(t^p \in k \cdot k(C)^p\). So the degree is at most \(p\). Finally \(t \notin k \cdot k(C)^p\), since \(d\) vanishes on \(k \cdot k(C)^p\) but \(dt \neq 0\) (Proposition 3.4.2).

Warning 3.2.13. If \(k\) is imperfect, then \(k(C)^q \subsetneq k \cdot k(C)^q\), because an element \(a \in k - k^q\) is not a \(q\)-th power in \(k(C)\). Indeed, \(k\) is algebraically closed in \(k(C)\). So [Sil09, Proposition II.2.11(a)] must be read with \(k \cdot k(C)^q\) in place of \(k(C)^q\).

Corollary 3.2.14. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k = p > 0\). Let \(\psi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be non-constant, and \(q \coloneqq \deg_i \psi\). Then \(\psi = \lambda \circ \Frob\), with \(\Frob\colon C_1 \to C_1^{(q)}\) the relative Frobenius morphism and \(\lambda\) separable.

Proof.

Let \(K\) be the separable closure of \(\psi^*k(C_2)\) in \(k(C_1)\). Then \(k(C_1)/K\) is purely inseparable of degree \(q\), so \(k(C_1)^q \subseteq K\). Since also \(k \subseteq K\), we get \(k \cdot k(C_1)^q \subseteq K\). Both have index \(q\) in \(k(C_1)\), by Proposition 3.2.12, so they are equal. Theorem 3.2.3 then gives \(\lambda\). Compare [Sil09, Corollary II.2.12].

Example 3.2.15. If \(E\colon Y^2Z = X^3 + AXZ^2 + BZ^3\), then \(E^{(q)}\colon Y^2Z = X^3 + A^qXZ^2 + B^qZ^3\) [Sil09, Example II.2.10]. If \(A, B \in \F_q\), then \(E^{(q)} = E\), and the relative Frobenius morphism is an endomorphism of \(E\) of degree \(q\).

Draft6 Oct 20263.3 Divisors

Definition 3.3.1. The group \(\Div C\) of divisors on \(C\) is the free abelian group on the closed points of \(C\). The degree of \(D = \sum n_P P\) is \(\deg D \coloneqq \sum n_P \deg P\), and \(\Div^0 C\) is the kernel of the degree. \(D\) is effective, \(D \geq 0\), if all \(n_P \geq 0\). For \(f \in k(C)^\times\), the principal divisor of \(f\) is \(\div(f) \coloneqq \sum_P \ord_P(f) P\). Divisors are linearly equivalent if their difference is principal. The Picard group is \(\Pic C \coloneqq \Div C/\div(k(C)^\times)\), and \(\Pic^0 C\) is the image of \(\Div^0 C\) [Poo06, Sections 1.9 and 2.2].

Proposition 3.3.2. Let \(f \in k(C)^\times\). Then \(\div(f) = 0\) if and only if \(f \in k^\times\), and \(\deg \div(f) = 0\). So there is an exact sequence

\begin{equation*} 1 \to k^\times \to k(C)^\times \xrightarrow{\div} \Div^0 C \to \Pic^0 C \to 0. \end{equation*}
Proof.

See [Sil09, Proposition II.3.1 and Remark II.3.4], and [Poo06, Section 2.2.2]. The first statement is Proposition 3.1.7.

Definition 3.3.3. Let \(f \in \kbar(C)^\times\) and let \(D = \sum n_P P \in \Div C_{\kbar}\) be a divisor whose support is disjoint from that of \(\div(f)\). The value of \(f\) at \(D\) is

\begin{equation*} f(D) \coloneqq \prod_P f(P)^{n_P} \in \kbar^\times, \end{equation*}

the product over the points \(P \in C(\kbar)\) in the support of \(D\) [Poo06, Section 1.9.2].

Each factor is a well defined element of \(\kbar^\times\): the function \(f\) has neither a zero nor a pole at \(P\), since \(P\) is not in the support of \(\div(f)\). Two properties are immediate from the definition. First, \(D \mapsto f(D)\) is a homomorphism on the group of divisors supported away from \(\div(f)\), and \(f \mapsto f(D)\) is a homomorphism on the functions whose divisors are supported away from \(D\). Second, evaluation commutes with the Galois action: for \(\sigma \in \Aut(\kbar/k)\),

\begin{equation*} \sigma\bigl(f(D)\bigr) = f^\sigma(\sigma D), \end{equation*}

because \(\sigma(f(P)) = f^\sigma(\sigma P)\) on each factor. In particular, if \(f\) is defined over \(k\), then \(\sigma(f(D)) = f(\sigma D)\).

Theorem 3.3.4 (Weil reciprocity). Let \(f, g \in \kbar(C)^\times\) be functions on a nice curve \(C\) such that \(\div(f)\) and \(\div(g)\) have disjoint supports. Then

\begin{equation*} f(\div g) = g(\div f). \end{equation*}
Proof.

See [Poo06, Theorem 1.9.5] and [Sil09, Exercise 2.11]. Poonen states Weil reciprocity over an algebraically closed field. Since divisors on \(C_{\kbar}\) are supported on the points of \(C(\kbar)\), that is exactly the statement above.

The content of the theorem is already visible on \(\PP^1\). If \(f\) and \(g\) are polynomials in \(k[x]\) with no common root, then the affine zeros contribute \(\prod_{g(\beta) = 0} f(\beta)\) to the left side and \(\prod_{f(\alpha) = 0} g(\alpha)\) to the right, and both products are the resultant \(\Res(f, g)\) up to the sign \((-1)^{\deg f \deg g}\) and the leading coefficients. The poles at \(\infty\) supply exactly those factors. So reciprocity is a geometric form of the symmetry of the resultant. We use Theorem 3.3.4 to construct the Weil pairing in Section 4.6.

Definition 3.3.5. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be non-constant. On closed points, set

\begin{equation*} \varphi^* Q \coloneqq \sum_{P \mapsto Q} e_\varphi(P)\, P, \qquad \varphi_* P \coloneqq f_\varphi(P)\, \varphi(P), \end{equation*}

and extend linearly to \(\varphi^*\colon \Div C_2 \to \Div C_1\) and \(\varphi_*\colon \Div C_1 \to \Div C_2\) [Poo06, Section 2.2.3].

Proposition 3.3.6. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be non-constant.

  1. \(\deg \varphi^* D = (\deg\varphi)(\deg D)\) and \(\deg \varphi_* D = \deg D\).
  2. \(\varphi^* \div(f) = \div(\varphi^* f)\) and \(\varphi_* \div(f) = \div(\varphi_* f)\).
  3. \(\varphi_* \circ \varphi^*\) is multiplication by \(\deg\varphi\) on \(\Div C_2\).
  4. \((\psi \circ \varphi)^* = \varphi^* \circ \psi^*\) and \((\psi\circ\varphi)_* = \psi_* \circ \varphi_*\).

So \(\varphi^*\) and \(\varphi_*\) induce homomorphisms between \(\Pic C_1\) and \(\Pic C_2\), and between \(\Pic^0 C_1\) and \(\Pic^0 C_2\).

Proof.

Part 1 follows from Proposition 3.2.8. For the rest see [Sil09, Proposition II.3.6] and [Poo06, Section 2.2.3].

[Sil09, Section II.3] works with divisors on \(C_{\kbar}\). His \(\Div_k(C)\) consists of the \(\Aut(\kbar/k)\)-invariant divisors on \(C_{\kbar}\), and \(\Pic_k(C) \coloneqq (\Pic C_{\kbar})^{\Aut(\kbar/k)}\). The dictionary with our groups is as follows. Compare [Poo06, Warning 1.9.6].

Proposition 3.3.7.

  1. Pulling back along \(C_{\ksep} \to C\) identifies \(\Div C\) with \((\Div C_{\ksep})^{\Gal_k}\), compatibly with degrees and principal divisors. A closed point \(P\) goes to the sum, with multiplicity \(1\), of the closed points of \(C_{\ksep}\) above \(P\).
  2. Pulling back along \(C_{\kbar} \to C\) sends a closed point \(P\) to \([k(P) : k]_i\) times the sum of the points of \(C(\kbar)\) above \(P\). This is injective, compatible with degrees and principal divisors, and lands in \(\Div_k(C)\). It is onto \(\Div_k(C)\) if \(k\) is perfect, but not in general.
  3. The homomorphism \(\Pic C \to (\Pic C_{\ksep})^{\Gal_k}\) is injective, but need not be surjective.
Proof.

For part 1: \(k(P) \otimes_k \ksep\) is reduced, since \(\ksep/k\) is separable, so every closed point of \(C_{\ksep}\) above \(P\) has multiplicity \(1\). They form one \(\Gal_k\)-orbit. For part 2: \(k(P) \otimes_k \kbar\) has length \([k(P):k]_i\) at each of its \([k(P):k]_s\) \(\kbar\)-points (Proposition 3.1.2). Part 3 follows from Hilbert's Theorem 90 [Poo06, Section 1.9.3 and Exercise 1.6].

Warning 3.3.8. In general the injection \(\Pic C \to (\Pic C_{\ksep})^{\Gal_k}\) of Proposition 3.3.7 is not surjective: its cokernel embeds in the Brauer group \(\operatorname{Br} k\) [Poo17, Corollary 6.7.8]. If \(C\) has a rational point, then the obstruction vanishes and \(\Pic C \to (\Pic C_{\ksep})^{\Gal_k}\) is an isomorphism, so the two readings of \(\Pic^0 C\) agree. In particular Silverman's \(\Pic_k(C)\) need not be the quotient of \(\Div_k(C)\) by principal divisors, although it is when \(C\) has genus \(1\) and a rational point [Sil09, Exercise 2.13]. Whenever the distinction matters we say which rational, closed or \(\kbar\)-points are meant.

Example 3.3.9. The degree is an isomorphism \(\Pic \PP^1_k \cong \Z\). Indeed, the closed points of \(\PP^1_k\) other than \(\infty\) correspond to monic irreducible \(g_P \in k[x]\), with \(\deg P = \deg g_P\). A divisor \(D = \sum n_P P + n_\infty \infty\) of degree \(0\) is \(\div\bigl(\prod g_P^{n_P}\bigr)\), since \(\ord_\infty(g_P) = -\deg g_P\) [Sil09, Example II.3.2].

Example 3.3.10. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). Let \(E\colon y^2 = (x - e_1)(x - e_2)(x - e_3)\) with distinct \(e_i \in k\), let \(P_i \coloneqq (e_i, 0)\) and \(O \coloneqq (0 : 1 : 0)\). Then

\begin{equation*} \div(x - e_i) = 2P_i - 2O, \qquad \div(y) = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 - 3O \end{equation*}

[Sil09, Example II.3.3]. If instead \(y^2 = p(x)\) with \(p\) irreducible of degree \(3\), then \(\div(y) = P - 3O\), where \(P \coloneqq V(y, p(x))\) is a closed point of degree \(3\).

Draft6 Oct 20263.4 Differentials

Definition 3.4.1. Let \(X\) be a curve over \(k\). The space \(\Omega_{k(X)/k}\) of meromorphic differentials on \(X\) is the \(k(X)\)-vector space generated by symbols \(dx\), \(x \in k(X)\), subject to \(d(x + y) = dx + dy\), \(d(xy) = x\,dy + y\,dx\) and \(da = 0\) for \(a \in k\). A non-constant morphism \(\varphi\colon X_1 \to X_2\) induces \(\varphi^*\colon \Omega_{k(X_2)/k} \to \Omega_{k(X_1)/k}\) [Sil09, Section II.4], [Poo06, Section 2.3.1].

Proposition 3.4.2. Let \(X\) be a curve over \(k\) with \(k(X)/k\) separable, e.g. a geometrically reduced curve.

  1. \(\Omega_{k(X)/k}\) has dimension \(1\) over \(k(X)\).
  2. For \(x \in k(X)\), the differential \(dx\) is a basis if and only if it is non-zero, if and only if \(k(X)/k(x)\) is finite separable.
  3. A non-constant morphism \(\varphi\colon X_1 \to X_2\) of such curves is separable if and only if \(\varphi^* \neq 0\) on differentials.
Proof.

See [Sil09, Proposition II.4.2] and [Poo06, Proposition 2.3.1]. In characteristic \(p\), the last condition of [Poo06, Proposition 2.3.1], \(t \notin k(X)^p\), must be read as \(t \notin k \cdot k(X)^p\) when \(k\) is imperfect.

Since \(C\) is smooth of dimension \(1\), the sheaf \(\Omega_{C/k}\) is invertible. So for every closed point \(P\) the stalk \(\Omega_{C/k,P} \subseteq \Omega_{k(C)/k}\) is free of rank \(1\) over \(\calO_{C,P}\).

Definition 3.4.3. Let \(\omega \in \Omega_{k(C)/k}\) be non-zero, \(P\) a closed point, and \(\eta\) a generator of \(\Omega_{C/k,P}\). Write \(\omega = g\eta\), and set \(\ord_P(\omega) \coloneqq \ord_P(g)\). The divisor of \(\omega\) is \(\div(\omega) \coloneqq \sum_P \ord_P(\omega) P\). The differential \(\omega\) is regular if \(\div(\omega) \geq 0\). A canonical divisor is a divisor \(K_C \coloneqq \div(\omega)\). Its class in \(\Pic C\) does not depend on \(\omega\), since any other non-zero differential is \(f\omega\) with \(f \in k(C)^\times\) [Sil09, Section II.4], [Poo06, Definitions 2.3.2 to 2.3.4].

Proposition 3.4.4. Let \(P\) be a closed point of \(C\) with \(k(P)/k\) separable, e.g. \(P \in C(k)\), and let \(t\) be a uniformizer at \(P\). Then \(dt\) generates \(\Omega_{C/k,P}\), so \(\ord_P(\omega) = \ord_P(\omega/dt)\), as in [Sil09, Proposition II.4.3].

Proof.

For \(P \in C(k)\) see the proof of Proposition 3.1.9.

Warning 3.4.5. If \(k(P)/k\) is inseparable, a uniformizer need not work. Let \(k \coloneqq \F_p(a)\) and \(P \coloneqq V(x^p - a)\) on \(\PP^1_k\). Then \(t \coloneqq x^p - a\) is a uniformizer at \(P\), but \(dt = px^{p-1}\,dx - da = 0\). The stalk \(\Omega_{\PP^1/k,P}\) is generated by \(dx\), so \(\ord_P(dx) = 0\). This is why Definition 3.4.3 uses a generator of the stalk, rather than \(dt\) as in [Poo06, Definition 2.3.2].

Example 3.4.6. Let \(t\) be the coordinate on \(\PP^1_k\). On \(\AA^1_k = \Spec k[t]\), the module of differentials is free on \(dt\), so \(\ord_P(dt) = 0\) for every closed point \(P \neq \infty\). At \(\infty\), the function \(s \coloneqq 1/t\) is a uniformizer at a rational point, and \(dt = -s^{-2}\,ds\), so \(\div(dt) = -2\infty\). In particular \(\PP^1_k\) has no non-zero regular differentials [Sil09, Example II.4.5].

The next two examples start the comparison of elliptic curves with the multiplicative group \(\Gm\) of Example 2.1.6, which runs through Chapter 4.

Example 3.4.7. Let \(t\) be the coordinate on \(\Gm = \AA^1_k - \{0\} \subseteq \PP^1_k\). Then \(k(\Gm) = k(\PP^1_k) = k(t)\), so a differential on \(\Gm\) is the same thing as a differential on \(\PP^1_k\). Let \(\omega \coloneqq dt/t\).

  1. Invariance. For \(a \in k^\times\), the translation \(\tau_a\colon \Gm \to \Gm\), \(t \mapsto at\), gives \(\tau_a^*\omega = d(at)/(at) = \omega\). Conversely, suppose that \(g \in \kbar(t)\) and \(\tau_a^*(g\,dt) = g\,dt\) for every \(a \in \kbar^\times\). Then \(a\,g(at) = g(t)\), so \(g(a) = g(1)/a\) for all but finitely many \(a \in \kbar^\times\), and hence \(g\,dt = g(1)\,\omega\). So the invariant differentials on \((\Gm)_{\kbar}\) form the line spanned by \(\omega\).

  2. Divisor. Since \(t\) is a unit of \(k[t, t^{-1}]\), \(\omega\) generates \(\Omega_{\Gm/k}\), so \(\omega\) has no zeros or poles on \(\Gm\). At \(t = 0\) it has a simple pole. At \(\infty\), with the uniformizer \(s \coloneqq 1/t\),

    \begin{equation*} \omega = \frac{dt}{t} = -\frac{ds}{s^2} \cdot s = -\frac{ds}{s}, \end{equation*}

    again a simple pole. So \(\div(\omega) = -(0) - (\infty)\), of degree \(-2\), as Example 3.4.6 requires. The coefficients of \(dt/t\) and \(ds/s\), the residues, are \(1\) and \(-1\), and they sum to \(0\).

This is the algebraic form of Example 1.5.4. The invariant differential of \(\Gm\) has poles at the two points of \(\PP^1_k - \Gm\). By contrast, the invariant differential of an elliptic curve has no poles at all (Example 3.4.9), because an elliptic curve is already projective.

Example 3.4.8. Let \(m\) be a non-zero integer, and let \([m]\colon \PP^1_k \to \PP^1_k\) be the morphism extending \(t \mapsto t^m\) (Proposition 3.2.1). It maps \(\Gm\) to \(\Gm\), and on \(L\)-points it is the homomorphism \(L^\times \to L^\times\), \(x \mapsto x^m\). The polynomial \(T^{\lvert m\rvert} - t^{\lvert m\rvert}\) is Eisenstein over \(k[t^{\lvert m\rvert}]\), so \(\deg[m] = [k(t) : k(t^m)] = \lvert m\rvert\).

  1. Pullback. \([m]^*\omega = d(t^m)/t^m = m\,t^{m-1}\,dt/t^m = m\omega\). By Proposition 3.4.2, \([m]\) is separable if and only if \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid m\).
  2. Additivity. By the Leibniz rule, \(d(uv)/(uv) = du/u + dv/v\). Since \(t^{m + n} = t^m \cdot t^n\), this gives \([m + n]^*\omega = [m]^*\omega + [n]^*\omega\) without computing either side. The same statement for elliptic curves is Theorem 4.4.2, whose proof is much harder.
  3. Kernel. The fiber of \([m]\) over \(1\) is \(\mu_m \coloneqq \Spec k[t]/(t^m - 1)\) for \(m > 0\). If \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid m\), then \(t^m - 1\) is separable, so \(\mu_m(\kbar)\) has \(m = \deg[m]\) elements. If \(\operatorname{char} k = p\) and \(m = p^r\), then \(t^{p^r} - 1 = (t - 1)^{p^r}\), so \(\mu_{p^r}(\kbar) = \{1\}\), with \(e_{[p^r]}(1) = p^r\): all of the degree is inseparable degree. Indeed, \([p]\) is the relative Frobenius morphism of \(\PP^1_k = (\PP^1_k)^{(p)}\) (Definition 3.2.11).
  4. Ramification. Above \(0\) and \(\infty\) lie only \(0\) and \(\infty\), each with ramification index \(\lvert m\rvert\). If \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid m\), then \([m]\) is unramified elsewhere, by part 3 and translation, and the Hurwitz formula (Theorem 3.6.2) reads \(-2 = \lvert m\rvert(-2) + 2(\lvert m\rvert - 1)\).

For an elliptic curve, too, \([m]^*\omega = m\omega\) (Corollary 4.4.3), but \(\deg[m] = m^2\) (Theorem 4.3.10), and \([m]\) is unramified everywhere.

Example 3.4.9. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). Let \(E\colon y^2 = p(x)\) with \(p\) a separable cubic, and \(O = (0 : 1 : 0)\). On the affine part, \(2y\,dy = p'(x)\,dx\), and \(y\) and \(p'(x)\) have no common zero. So \(dx/y = 2\,dy/p'(x)\) generates \(\Omega_{E/k}\) there. At \(O\), \(\ord_O(x) = -2\) and \(\ord_O(y) = -3\), so \(\ord_O(dx) = -3\) and \(\div(dx/y) = 0\) [Sil09, Example II.4.6].

Draft6 Oct 20263.5 The Riemann–Roch theorem

Definition 3.5.1. For \(D \in \Div C\), let

\begin{equation*} \mcL(D) \coloneqq \{f \in k(C)^\times : \div(f) + D \geq 0\} \cup \{0\}, \end{equation*}

a finite-dimensional \(k\)-vector space, and \(\ell(D) \coloneqq \dim_k \mcL(D)\). If \(\deg D < 0\), then \(\mcL(D) = 0\). If \(D \sim D'\), then \(\ell(D) = \ell(D')\) [Poo06, Definition 2.4.1], [Sil09, Proposition II.5.2].

Proposition 3.5.2. Let \(D \in \Div C\), let \(L \supseteq k\) be a field extension, and \(D_L\) the pullback of \(D\) to \(C_L\). Then the natural map \(\mcL(D) \otimes_k L \to \mcL(D_L)\) is an isomorphism, and \(\deg D_L = \deg D\). In particular \(\ell(D_L) = \ell(D)\).

Proof.

\(\mcL(D)\) is the space of global sections of the invertible sheaf \(\calO_C(D)\), and \(\calO_C(D)\) pulls back to \(\calO_{C_L}(D_L)\). Taking global sections commutes with the flat base change \(\Spec L \to \Spec k\) [Har77, Proposition III.9.3]. For \(L = \kbar\) and perfect \(k\), [Sil09, Proposition II.5.8] proves this with Galois descent instead, and [Poo06, Section 2.4.1] states it.

Definition 3.5.3. The genus of \(C\) is \(g \coloneqq \ell(K_C)\), the dimension of the \(k\)-vector space of regular differentials on \(C\) [Poo06, Definition 2.4.5].

Theorem 3.5.4 (Riemann–Roch). Let \(C\) be a nice curve of genus \(g\), and \(D \in \Div C\). Then

\begin{equation*} \ell(D) - \ell(K_C - D) = \deg D - g + 1. \end{equation*}
Proof.

See [Sil09, Theorem II.5.4] and [Poo06, Theorem 2.4.7]. To reduce to \(k = \kbar\), note that \(\Omega_{C_{\kbar}/\kbar}\) is the pullback of \(\Omega_{C/k}\), so \(K_C\) pulls back to a canonical divisor. Then apply Proposition 3.5.2.

Corollary 3.5.5. Let \(C\) be a nice curve of genus \(g\).

  1. \(\deg K_C = 2g - 2\).
  2. If \(\deg D > 2g - 2\), then \(\ell(D) = \deg D - g + 1\).
  3. For every field extension \(L \supseteq k\), the nice curve \(C_L\) has genus \(g\).
Proof.

Parts 1 and 2 are [Sil09, Corollary II.5.5] and [Poo06, Corollaries 2.4.8 and 2.4.9]. Part 3 follows from Proposition 3.5.2, since \(K_C\) pulls back to a canonical divisor.

Example 3.5.6. By Example 3.4.6, \(\PP^1_k\) has genus \(0\), and \(\ell(D) = \deg D + 1\) whenever \(\deg D \geq -1\) [Sil09, Example II.5.6].

Example 3.5.7. Let \(E\colon y^2 = p(x)\) be as in Example 3.4.9. We may take \(K_E = 0\), so \(E\) has genus \(\ell(0) = 1\), and \(\ell(D) = \deg D\) whenever \(\deg D \geq 1\). In particular \(\ell(P) = 1\) for \(P \in E(k)\), so no function has a single simple pole. For \(O = (0 : 1 : 0)\), the functions \(1, x\) form a basis of \(\mcL(2O)\), and \(1, x, y\) a basis of \(\mcL(3O)\). The seven functions \(1, x, y, x^2, xy, x^3, y^2\) lie in the \(6\)-dimensional space \(\mcL(6O)\), and the linear relation between them is the equation of \(E\) [Sil09, Example II.5.7].

Draft21 Sep 20263.6 The Hurwitz formula

Definition 3.6.1. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be a non-constant separable morphism, and \(\omega\) a non-zero differential on \(C_2\), so that \(\varphi^*\omega \neq 0\) (Proposition 3.4.2). The ramification divisor of \(\varphi\) is

\begin{equation*} R_\varphi \coloneqq \div(\varphi^*\omega) - \varphi^*\div(\omega) \in \Div C_1. \end{equation*}

It does not depend on \(\omega\) [Poo06, Definition 2.5.1].

Theorem 3.6.2 (Hurwitz). Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be a non-constant separable morphism of nice curves of genera \(g_1\) and \(g_2\). Then

\begin{equation*} 2g_1 - 2 = (\deg\varphi)(2g_2 - 2) + \deg R_\varphi. \end{equation*}

For every \(P \in C_1(\kbar)\), the base change \(\varphi_{\kbar}\) satisfies \(\ord_P(R_{\varphi_{\kbar}}) \geq e(P) - 1\), where \(e(P) \coloneqq e_{\varphi_{\kbar}}(P)\), with equality if and only if \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid e(P)\). So if no ramification index is divisible by \(\operatorname{char} k\), then

\begin{equation*} 2g_1 - 2 = (\deg\varphi)(2g_2 - 2) + \sum_{P \in C_1(\kbar)} \bigl(e(P) - 1\bigr). \end{equation*}
Proof.

Take degrees in \(\div(\varphi^*\omega) = \varphi^*\div(\omega) + R_\varphi\), and use Proposition 3.3.6 and Corollary 3.5.5 [Poo06, Theorem 2.5.7]. Base change to \(\kbar\) preserves separability, degrees and genera, and over \(\kbar\) the rest is [Poo06, Proposition 2.5.4 and Corollary 2.5.8] and [Sil09, Theorem II.5.9].

Example 3.6.3. Let \(x\colon E \to \PP^1_k\) be the morphism of Example 3.2.10, with \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). Over \(\kbar\) it is ramified exactly at the three roots \((e_i, 0)\) of \(p\) and at \(O\), each with index \(2\). Hurwitz gives \(2g - 2 = 2 \cdot (-2) + 4 = 0\), so \(g = 1\), in agreement with Example 3.5.7.

Example 3.6.4. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k = 2\), and let \(C\colon Y^2Z + YZ^2 = X^3\), the curve \(y^2 + y = x^3\). The partial derivatives are \(X^2\), \(Z^2\) and \(Y^2\), with no common zero, so \(C\) is a smooth cubic, of genus \(1\) by the degree–genus formula (Corollary 3.7.7 below). The morphism \(x\colon C \to \PP^1_k\) has degree \(2\), and it is separable, since \(y^2 + y - x^3\) has derivative \(1\) in \(y\). Over each \(a \in \kbar\) the equation \(y^2 + y = a^3\) has two distinct roots. So the only ramification is at \(O = (0 : 1 : 0)\), with index \(2\), which is wild. One computes \(R_x = 4O\) [Poo06, Example 2.5.6], and Hurwitz gives \(2g - 2 = 2 \cdot (-2) + 4 = 0\). The formula for the tame case would give the impossible value \(2 \cdot (-2) + 1\).

Draft6 Oct 20263.7 Plane curves

We follow [Mil20, Section I.1], and use coordinates \((X : Y : Z)\) on \(\PP^2_k\), with \(x = X/Z\) and \(y = Y/Z\) on the affine patch \(Z \neq 0\).

Definition 3.7.1. Let \(F \in k[X, Y, Z]\) be a non-constant homogeneous polynomial of degree \(d\) with no repeated factor in \(\kbar[X, Y, Z]\). The projective plane curve defined by \(F\) is \(C_F \coloneqq V_+(F) \subseteq \PP^2_k\), and \(d\) is its degree. Affine plane curves \(V(f) \subseteq \AA^2_k\) are defined in the same way.

The condition on \(F\) says that \(C_F\) is geometrically reduced [Mil20, Aside I.1.7]. The affine patches of \(C_F\) are the affine plane curves \(F(x, y, 1) = 0\), \(F(x, 1, z) = 0\) and \(F(1, y, z) = 0\).

Proposition 3.7.2. Let \(C = V_+(F) \subseteq \PP^2_k\) be a projective plane curve of degree \(d\). Its singular points are the common zeros in \(\PP^2(\kbar)\) of \(F\), \(\partial F/\partial X\), \(\partial F/\partial Y\) and \(\partial F/\partial Z\). If \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid d\), the condition \(F = 0\) may be omitted, by the Euler relation

\begin{equation*} dF = X\frac{\partial F}{\partial X} + Y\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y} + Z\frac{\partial F}{\partial Z}. \end{equation*}
Proof (Poo06, Mil20).

See [Poo06, Example 1.7.5] and [Mil20, Exercise I.1.17].

When \(\operatorname{char} k \mid d\) the condition \(F = 0\) cannot be dropped: for \(F = Y^2Z - X^3 + tZ^3\) over \(\F_3(t)\) (Example 2.4.6) the three partial derivatives vanish at \((0 : 0 : 1)\), which is not a \(\kbar\)-point of the curve.

Definition 3.7.3. Let \(C = V_+(F)\) and \(D = V_+(G)\) be projective plane curves with no common component. Then \(C \cap D \coloneqq V_+(F, G)\) is a finite \(k\)-scheme. For a closed point \(P\) of \(C \cap D\), choose an affine patch containing \(P\), with dehomogenizations \(f\) and \(g\) of \(F\) and \(G\). The intersection number of \(C\) and \(D\) at \(P\) is

\begin{equation*} I_P(C, D) \coloneqq \dim_k \calO_{\PP^2_k, P}/(f, g). \end{equation*}

For \(P \in C(k) \cap D(k)\) this is the intersection number of [Mil20, Proposition I.1.8], and it does not change under base extension [Mil20, Remark I.1.9].

Theorem 3.7.4 (Bézout). Let \(C\) and \(D\) be projective plane curves over \(k\) of degrees \(m\) and \(n\), with no common component. Then

\begin{equation*} \sum_{P \in C \cap D} I_P(C, D) = mn. \end{equation*}
Proof (Mil20).

Base change to \(\kbar\), and apply [Mil20, Theorem I.1.18].

Corollary 3.7.5. A smooth projective plane curve is geometrically integral, hence nice.

Proof.

It is geometrically reduced by definition. Over \(\kbar\), two irreducible components would meet by Theorem 3.7.4, and a \(\kbar\)-point on two components is singular [Mil20, Remarks I.1.4 and I.1.21].

Proposition 3.7.6. Let \(C = V_+(F) \subseteq \PP^2_k\) be a smooth plane curve of degree \(d\), not the line \(Z = 0\), and let \(f(x, y) \coloneqq F(x, y, 1)\). Let \(H\) be the effective divisor cut out by the line \(Z = 0\). Then \(H\) has degree \(d\), and

\begin{equation*} \div\left(\frac{dx}{\partial f/\partial y}\right) = (d - 3)H. \end{equation*}

Here \(dx/(\partial f/\partial y)\) stands for \(-dy/(\partial f/\partial x)\) if \(\partial f/\partial y\) vanishes identically on \(C\).

Proof.

On the affine part, \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy = 0\), and the partial derivatives have no common zero (Proposition 3.7.2). So \(\omega \coloneqq dx/(\partial f/\partial y) = -dy/(\partial f/\partial x)\) generates \(\Omega_{C/k}\) there, and \(\ord_P(\omega) = 0\) at the closed points \(P\) of the affine part.

At a closed point \(P\) on the line \(Z = 0\), say with \(Y \neq 0\), use the coordinates \(u \coloneqq X/Y\) and \(v \coloneqq Z/Y\), so that \(x = u/v\) and \(y = 1/v\), and let \(g(u, v) \coloneqq F(u, 1, v)\). As before, \(\eta \coloneqq du/(\partial g/\partial v) = -dv/(\partial g/\partial u)\) generates \(\Omega_{C/k}\) near \(P\). The Euler relation gives \(u\,\partial g/\partial u + v\,\partial g/\partial v = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(u, 1, v)\) on \(C\), and \(\partial f/\partial y = v^{1 - d}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial Y}(u, 1, v)\) because \(\partial F/\partial Y\) is homogeneous of degree \(d - 1\). Substituting into \(dx = (v\,du - u\,dv)/v^2\) gives

\begin{equation*} \omega = -v^{d - 3}\,\eta, \end{equation*}

so \(\ord_P(\omega) = (d - 3)\ord_P(v)\), where \(v\) is a local equation of the line \(Z = 0\). The case \(X \neq 0\) is symmetric. Hence \(\div(\omega) = (d - 3)H\). The degree of \(H\) is \(d\) by Theorem 3.7.4: on a smooth curve, \(I_P(C, L) = \ord_P(\ell)\deg P\) for a local equation \(\ell\) of the line \(L\).

For \(d = 3\) and \(f = y^2 - p(x)\) this recovers Example 3.4.9. For \(d = 1\), with \(f = y\), it recovers Example 3.4.6.

Corollary 3.7.7. A smooth plane curve of degree \(d\) has genus \((d - 1)(d - 2)/2\).

Proof.

By Proposition 3.7.6, \(\deg K_C = d(d - 3)\), and \(d(d - 3) = 2g - 2\) by Corollary 3.5.5. See also [Sil09, Exercise 2.7] and [Poo06, Section 2.9.2].

Remark 3.7.8. Smoothness cannot be weakened to regularity here. The curve \(E\colon y^2 = x^3 - t\) over \(k = \F_3(t)\) of Example 2.4.6 is regular and geometrically integral, and as a plane cubic it has arithmetic genus \(1\). Over \(k' \coloneqq k(s)\), where \(s^3 = t\), it becomes the cuspidal cubic \(y^2 = (x - s)^3\), whose normalization \(u \mapsto (s + u^2, u^3)\) is \(\PP^1_{k'}\). So the genus drops under base extension, and Corollary 3.5.5 fails for \(E\). Also \(\Omega_{E/k}\) is not invertible at the non-smooth point, so Definition 3.4.3 does not apply.

Example 3.7.9. Let \(h \in k[x]\) have degree \(n \geq 3\) and leading coefficient \(c\), let \(H(X, Z) \coloneqq Z^nh(X/Z)\) be its homogenization, and let \(C \coloneqq V_+(F) \subseteq \PP^2_k\), where

\begin{equation*} F \coloneqq Y^2Z^{n - 2} - H(X, Z), \end{equation*}

the projective closure of the affine curve \(y^2 = h(x)\). Both \(F\) and \(C\) have degree \(n\).

  1. \(C\) is a plane curve. Since \(F \equiv -cX^n \pmod Z\), the polynomial \(Z\) does not divide \(F\), so a repeated factor of \(F\) is one of \(y^2 - h(x)\). A factorization \(y^2 - h(x) = (y - g)(y + g)\) over \(\kbar\) has a repeated factor only if \(2g = 0\), so \(F\) has one exactly when \(\operatorname{char} k = 2\) and \(h \in k[x^2]\). Exclude that case, so that Definition 3.7.1 applies.

  2. Singular points of the affine part. Here \(f = y^2 - h(x)\), so \(\partial f/\partial y = 2y\) and \(\partial f/\partial x = -h'(x)\), and the singular points of \(y^2 = h(x)\) are the pairs \((\alpha, \beta) \in \kbar^2\) with

    \begin{equation*} \beta^2 = h(\alpha), \qquad 2\beta = 0, \qquad h'(\alpha) = 0. \end{equation*}

    If \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), then \(\beta = 0\), and the conditions \(h(\alpha) = h'(\alpha) = 0\) say that \(\alpha\) is a repeated root of \(h\). So the affine curve is singular exactly at the \(\kbar\)-points \((e, 0)\) with \(e\) a repeated root of \(h\) in \(\kbar\), and it is smooth if and only if \(h\) is separable. For \(h = x^3 + Ax + B\) this says \(4A^3 + 27B^2 \neq 0\) [Mil20, Example I.1.5]. If \(\operatorname{char} k = 2\), the condition \(2\beta = 0\) is vacuous. When \(n\) is odd, \(h'\) has degree \(n - 1 \geq 2\), hence a root \(\alpha \in \kbar\), and any \(\beta\) with \(\beta^2 = h(\alpha)\) gives a singular point. For \(h = x^3 + Ax + B\) it is the \(\kbar\)-point with \(\alpha^2 = A\) and \(\beta^2 = \alpha^3 + A\alpha + B\).

  3. The point at infinity. Since \(n \geq 3\), we have \(F(X, Y, 0) = -cX^n\), so \(C\) meets the line \(L \coloneqq V_+(Z)\) only at \(O \coloneqq (0 : 1 : 0)\), and \(I_O(C, L) = n\) by Theorem 3.7.4. Now

    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial F}{\partial X} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial X}, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial Y} = 2YZ^{n - 2}, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial Z} = (n - 2)Y^2Z^{n - 3} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial Z}. \end{equation*}

    The partial derivatives of \(H\) are homogeneous of degree \(n - 1 \geq 2\) in \((X, Z)\), so they vanish at \(O\), as does \(\partial F/\partial Y\). For \(n = 3\) the remaining derivative is \(\partial F/\partial Z = Y^2 - \partial H/\partial Z\), with value \(1\) at \(O\). For \(n \geq 4\) it vanishes at \(O\) as well. So \(O\) is a smooth point of \(C\) if \(n = 3\), and a singular point if \(n \geq 4\), in every characteristic.

  4. Conclusion. If \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), then \(C\) is smooth if and only if \(n = 3\) and \(h\) is separable. It is then nice by Corollary 3.7.5 and of genus \(1\) by Corollary 3.7.7. If \(\operatorname{char} k = 2\), then \(C\) is singular: by part 2 when \(n\) is odd, and by part 3 when \(n\) is even, since then \(n \geq 4\). One needs the general Weierstrass equation instead [Sil09, Section III.1].

So a curve \(y^2 = h(x)\) with \(\deg h \geq 4\) has no smooth plane model, and must be studied through some other embedding. The case \(n = 3\) is the one we pursue, in Section 4.1.
