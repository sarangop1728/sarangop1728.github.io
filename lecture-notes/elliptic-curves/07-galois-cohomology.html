Chapter 7Galois cohomology

This chapter follows [Sil09, Appendix B] and [Sil09, Section VIII.2], with the left actions of [Poo02, Sections 2–6]. For the point of view on twists, torsors, Brauer groups and étale cohomology we follow [Poo17, Sections 1.3, 1.5, 4.4, 4.5, 5.12 and 6.2–6.6].

Situation 7.0.1.

  • \(k\) is a field, not assumed perfect, with a separable closure \(\ksep\) and absolute Galois group \(\Gal_k \coloneqq \Gal(\ksep/k)\). For an extension \(L\) of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), \(\Gal_L \coloneqq \Gal(\ksep/L)\).
  • \(G\) is a profinite group, often finite or \(\Gal_k\), with elements \(g, h\). The letter \(\sigma\) is kept for a generator of a cyclic or procyclic group.
  • \(A\) is a discrete \(G\)-module, written additively, with the left action \(a \mapsto ga\) (Definition 7.1.2).
  • For a discrete \(\Gal_k\)-module \(A\) we write \(H^i(k, A) \coloneqq H^i(\Gal_k, A)\). For a smooth commutative algebraic group \(A\) over \(k\), such as \(\Gm\), \(\mu_m\) or \(E\), we abuse notation and write \(H^i(k, A) \coloneqq H^i(\Gal_k, A(\ksep))\). Only here does \(A\) stand for \(A(\ksep)\).
  • \(E\) is an elliptic curve over \(k\).
  • \(m \geq 1\) is an integer and \(\mu_m \coloneqq \Gm[m]\), the kernel of \(x \mapsto x^m\) on \(\Gm\). Unless stated otherwise, \(\operatorname{char} k \nmid m\). Then \(\mu_m(\ksep) \cong \Z/m\Z\) and \(E[m](\ksep) \cong (\Z/m\Z)^2\) as groups.
  • For an abelian group \(B\), \(B/m \coloneqq B/mB\) and \(B[m]\) is its \(m\)-torsion subgroup. For a field \(L\), \(L^\times/L^{\times m}\) is \(L^\times/m\), written multiplicatively.

Draft8 Oct 20267.1 Profinite groups and discrete modules

We are in Situation 7.0.1. Galois cohomology studies the group \(\Gal_k\) through the abelian groups on which it acts: the multiplicative group \((\ksep)^\times\), its roots of unity, the group \(E(\ksep)\) and its torsion. Silverman [Sil09] assumes \(k\) perfect and works with an algebraic closure. Nothing in Silverman's Appendix B changes with \(\ksep\) in its place. Silverman also writes right actions, \(a \mapsto a^\sigma\), where we write left actions, \(a \mapsto ga\).

Restriction makes the finite groups \(\Gal(L/k)\), for \(L\) running over the finite Galois extensions of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), an inverse system, and \(\Gal_k = \varprojlim_L \Gal(L/k)\). So \(\Gal_k\) is a profinite group: an inverse limit of finite groups, compact, Hausdorff and totally disconnected. Its topology is the Krull topology.

Proposition 7.1.1 (Infinite Galois theory).

  1. The subgroups \(\Gal_L\), for \(L\) running over the finite Galois extensions of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), are open and normal in \(\Gal_k\), and they form a basis of open neighborhoods of \(1\).
  2. The maps \(H \mapsto (\ksep)^H\) and \(L \mapsto \Gal_L\) are inverse bijections between the closed subgroups of \(\Gal_k\) and the extensions of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\). The subgroup \(H\) is open if and only if \((\ksep)^H\) is finite over \(k\), and then \([\Gal_k : H] = [(\ksep)^H : k]\). It is normal if and only if \((\ksep)^H\) is Galois over \(k\), and then \(\Gal((\ksep)^H/k) \cong \Gal_k/H\) by restriction. In particular, \((\ksep)^{\Gal_k} = k\).
Proof (Sil09, Mil22).

See [Sil09, Section B.2] and [Mil22, Theorem 7.13].

In every profinite group the open normal subgroups form a basis of open neighborhoods of \(1\), and an open subgroup has finite index, since its cosets form an open cover of a compact group. We met a first example in Chapter 5: by (5.1.1), \(\Gal_{\F_q} \cong \Zhat\), and the Frobenius automorphism \(\sigma\colon \alpha \mapsto \alpha^q\) generates a dense subgroup \(\sigma^{\Z}\) of \(\Gal_{\F_q}\). We say that \(\sigma\) is a topological generator.

Definition 7.1.2. A discrete \(G\)-module is an abelian group \(A\) with a left action of \(G\) by group automorphisms, written \(a \mapsto ga\), such that the action map \(G \times A \to A\) is continuous for the discrete topology on \(A\). Equivalently, the stabilizer of every \(a \in A\) is open in \(G\), that is, \(A\) is the union of the subgroups \(A^U\) of elements fixed by \(U\), for \(U\) running over the open subgroups of \(G\). A morphism of discrete \(G\)-modules is a homomorphism that commutes with the action. A \(G\)-group is defined in the same way, without the condition that the group be commutative, and with its law written multiplicatively.

Example 7.1.3. The following are discrete \(\Gal_k\)-modules, with the action of \(\Gal_k\) on \(\ksep\).

  1. The additive group \(\Ga(\ksep) = \ksep\), the multiplicative group \(\Gm(\ksep) = (\ksep)^\times\) and its subgroup \(\mu_m(\ksep)\). The stabilizer of \(\alpha \in \ksep\) contains \(\Gal_{k(\alpha)}\), which is open because \(k(\alpha)\) is finite over \(k\) (part 2 of Proposition 7.1.1).
  2. Every abelian group, such as \(\Z\), \(\Z/m\Z\), \(\Q\) or \(\Q/\Z\), with the trivial action.
  3. The group \(A(\ksep)\) of \(\ksep\)-points of a commutative algebraic group \(A\) over \(k\), such as \(E(\ksep)\) and \(E[m](\ksep)\). The coordinates of a \(\ksep\)-point lie in a finite extension of \(k\), as in part 1. For any \(k\)-variety \(X\), the same argument shows that \(\Gal_k\) acts continuously on the set \(X(\ksep)\), and \(X(k) = X(\ksep)^{\Gal_k}\) by Proposition 2.1.17.
  4. The group \(\GL_n(\ksep)\) is a \(\Gal_k\)-group, acting on the entries of a matrix.

The two finite modules of the chapter are \(\mu_m(\ksep)\) and \(E[m](\ksep)\). Their actions are recorded by homomorphisms. Since \(\mu_m(\ksep)\) is cyclic of order \(m\), its automorphism group is \((\Z/m\Z)^\times\), and the action is the cyclotomic character

\begin{equation*} \chi_m\colon \Gal_k \to (\Z/m\Z)^\times, \qquad g(\zeta) = \zeta^{\chi_m(g)} \quad \text{for } \zeta \in \mu_m(\ksep). \end{equation*}

Its kernel is \(\Gal_{k(\mu_m)}\), where \(k(\mu_m)\) is the extension of \(k\) generated by \(\mu_m(\ksep)\). Likewise, the action on \(E[m](\ksep) \cong (\Z/m\Z)^2\) is the mod \(m\) representation \(\bar\rho_{E,m}\colon \Gal_k \to \GL_2(\Z/m\Z)\) of Chapter 4, after the choice of a basis. Its kernel is \(\Gal_{k(E[m])}\), where \(k(E[m])\) is the extension of \(k\) generated by the coordinates of the torsion points in \(E[m](\ksep)\). Both kernels are open, and \(k(\mu_m)\) and \(k(E[m])\) are finite Galois extensions of \(k\).

Proposition 7.1.4. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(k\). Then \(\det\bar\rho_{E,m} = \chi_m\). In particular \(k(\mu_m) \subseteq k(E[m])\).

Proof.

Let \((S, T)\) be the chosen basis of \(E[m](\ksep)\), and \(g \in \Gal_k\) with \(gS = aS + cT\) and \(gT = bS + dT\). The Weil pairing \(e_m\) is bilinear and alternating, by parts 1 and 2 of Proposition 4.6.3, so

\begin{equation*} e_m(gS, gT) = e_m(S, T)^{ad} e_m(T, S)^{cb} = e_m(S, T)^{ad - bc}. \end{equation*}

By part 4 of the same proposition, \(e_m(gS, gT) = g\,e_m(S, T)\), which is \(e_m(S, T)^{\chi_m(g)}\). The root of unity \(e_m(S, T)\) has order \(m\): if it had order \(d\), then \(e_m(S, [d]T) = e_m(S, T)^d = 1\) and \(e_m(T, [d]T) = 1\), so \(e_m(S', [d]T) = 1\) for every \(S' \in E[m](\ksep)\), and \([d]T = O\) by part 3 of Proposition 4.6.3. So \(d = m\). Hence \(ad - bc \equiv \chi_m(g) \pmod m\). If \(g\) acts trivially on \(E[m](\ksep)\), then \(\chi_m(g) = \det 1 = 1\).

This is the first use of the Weil pairing in this chapter. It returns in Section 7.9.

Example 7.1.5. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\) and \(E\colon y^2 = f(x)\), with \(f \in k[x]\) a separable cubic polynomial with roots \(e_1, e_2, e_3 \in \ksep\). Then \(E[2](\ksep) = \{O, (e_1, 0), (e_2, 0), (e_3, 0)\}\) (Example 4.2.9), so \(k(E[2])\) is the splitting field of \(f\). An element of \(\Gal_k\) acts on \(E[2](\ksep)\) by permuting the three non-zero elements as it permutes the roots, and \(\GL_2(\F_2)\) is the group \(S_3\) of all permutations of these three elements. So the image of \(\bar\rho_{E,2}\) is the Galois group of \(f\). For \(y^2 = x^3 - 2\) over \(\Q\) it is all of \(\GL_2(\F_2)\), and \(\Q(E[2]) = \Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \sqrt{-3})\) has degree \(6\). Here \(\chi_2\) is trivial, and every matrix in \(\GL_2(\F_2)\) has determinant \(1\), as Proposition 7.1.4 requires.

Draft8 Oct 20267.2 Cohomology in low degrees

We are in Situation 7.0.1. Cohomology measures the failure of the functor \(A \mapsto A^G\) to preserve surjections. We need the groups \(H^i(G, A)\) only for \(i \leq 2\), and we define them with cochains.

Definition 7.2.1. For \(i = 0, 1, 2, 3\), the group \(C^i(G, A)\) of \(i\)-cochains is the group of continuous maps \(G^i \to A\), under addition of values, so that \(C^0(G, A) = A\). We write \(\xi_g\), \(\eta_{g_1, g_2}\) for the values of a \(1\)-cochain \(\xi\) and a \(2\)-cochain \(\eta\). The coboundary maps \(d^i\colon C^i(G, A) \to C^{i + 1}(G, A)\), for \(i \leq 2\), are

\begin{align*} (d^0a)_g &\coloneqq ga - a, \\ (d^1\xi)_{g_1, g_2} &\coloneqq g_1\xi_{g_2} - \xi_{g_1g_2} + \xi_{g_1}, \\ (d^2\eta)_{g_1, g_2, g_3} &\coloneqq g_1\eta_{g_2, g_3} - \eta_{g_1g_2, g_3} + \eta_{g_1, g_2g_3} - \eta_{g_1, g_2}. \end{align*}

A direct computation gives \(d^1 \circ d^0 = 0\) and \(d^2 \circ d^1 = 0\). The group of \(i\)-cocycles is \(Z^i(G, A) \coloneqq \ker d^i\), the group of \(i\)-coboundaries is \(B^i(G, A) \coloneqq \operatorname{im} d^{i - 1}\) (with \(B^0 \coloneqq 0\)), and the \(i\)-th cohomology group is

\begin{equation*} H^i(G, A) \coloneqq Z^i(G, A)/B^i(G, A), \qquad i = 0, 1, 2. \end{equation*}

Two cocycles with the same class are cohomologous.

Unwinding the definition: \(H^0(G, A) = A^G\), the subgroup of the \(a\) with \(ga = a\) for every \(g \in G\). A left \(1\)-cocycle, or simply \(1\)-cocycle, is a continuous map \(\xi\colon G \to A\) with

\begin{equation} \label{eq:gc-cocycle} \xi_{gh} = \xi_g + g\xi_h \qquad \text{for all } g, h \in G, \tag{7.2.1} \end{equation}

and a left \(1\)-coboundary, or simply \(1\)-coboundary, is a map \(g \mapsto ga - a\), for some \(a \in A\). Every coboundary is continuous, since the stabilizer of \(a\) is open. Taking \(g = h = 1\) in \eqref{eq:gc-cocycle} gives \(\xi_1 = 0\). If \(G\) is finite, every map \(G^i \to A\) is continuous. When the group law of \(A\) is written multiplicatively, as for \(L^\times\) or \(\mu_m(L)\), the conditions read \(\xi_{gh} = \xi_g \cdot g(\xi_h)\) and \(\xi_g = g(a)/a\).

Remark 7.2.2. Silverman's cocycles are right cocycles, written for the right action \(a \mapsto a^\sigma\): \(\xi_{\sigma\tau} = \xi_\sigma^\tau + \xi_\tau\), with coboundaries \(\xi_\sigma = a^\sigma - a\) [Sil09, Section B.1]. Setting \(ga \coloneqq a^{g^{-1}}\) turns a right action into a left one, and \(\xi \mapsto (g \mapsto \xi_{g^{-1}})\) turns right cocycles into left ones, so the two definitions give isomorphic groups. The groups of Definition 7.2.1 are the first three of a sequence of functors \(H^i(G, -)\), \(i \geq 0\), that turn short exact sequences into long exact sequences [Poo02, Section 3], and that can also be defined as derived functors of \(A \mapsto A^G\) [Poo17, Section 6.4]. We need only \(i \leq 2\).

Proposition 7.2.3 (Trivial action). If \(G\) acts trivially on \(A\), then \(H^0(G, A) = A\) and \(H^1(G, A) = \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(G, A)\), the group of continuous homomorphisms.

Proof.

With the trivial action, \eqref{eq:gc-cocycle} says that \(\xi\) is a homomorphism, and every coboundary \(g \mapsto a - a\) is \(0\) [Sil09, Remark B.2.2].

The first example of each of our two families:

  • If \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq k\), then \(\Gal_k\) acts trivially on \(\mu_m(\ksep)\), and \(H^1(k, \mu_m) = \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, \mu_m(\ksep))\). The kernel of such a homomorphism \(\chi\) is an open normal subgroup, the fixed field \(L\) of \(\ker \chi\) is a cyclic extension of \(k\) of degree dividing \(m\), and \(\chi\) identifies \(\Gal(L/k)\) with a subgroup of \(\mu_m(\ksep)\). We return to this in Corollary 7.4.7.

  • If \(E[m](\ksep) \subseteq E(k)\), then \(\Gal_k\) acts trivially on \(E[m](\ksep)\), and

    \begin{equation*} H^1(k, E[m]) = \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, E[m](\ksep)) \cong \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, \Z/m\Z)^2, \end{equation*}

    the isomorphism depending on the choice of a basis.

When the action is not trivial, cocycles are harder to find. For cyclic groups there is a formula.

Proposition 7.2.4.

  1. Let \(G = \langle\sigma\rangle\) be cyclic of order \(n\), and \(N \coloneqq 1 + \sigma + \dots + \sigma^{n - 1}\), acting on \(A\). Then \(\xi \mapsto \xi_\sigma\) induces an isomorphism

    \begin{equation*} H^1(G, A) \to \ker(N\colon A \to A)/(\sigma - 1)A. \end{equation*}
  2. Let \(G = \Zhat\), with topological generator \(\sigma\), and suppose that \(A\) is a torsion group. Then \(\xi \mapsto \xi_\sigma\) induces an isomorphism \(H^1(G, A) \to A/(\sigma - 1)A\).
Proof.

Let \(\xi\) be a \(1\)-cocycle and \(x \coloneqq \xi_\sigma\). By induction on \(i\), using \(\xi_{\sigma^{i + 1}} = \xi_\sigma + \sigma\xi_{\sigma^i}\),

\begin{equation} \label{eq:gc-cyclic-cocycle} \xi_{\sigma^i} = (1 + \sigma + \dots + \sigma^{i - 1})x \qquad (i \geq 0), \tag{7.2.2} \end{equation}

with the empty sum for \(i = 0\). So \(\xi\) is determined by \(x\) on the subgroup \(\sigma^{\Z_{\geq 0}}\), which is all of \(G\) in part 1 and is dense in part 2. Since \(\xi\) is continuous and \(A\) is discrete, \(\xi\) is determined by \(x\) in both cases. The coboundary of \(a\) has \(x = \sigma a - a\). It remains to find the image of \(\xi \mapsto x\).

In part 1, \(Nx = \xi_{\sigma^n} = \xi_1 = 0\). Conversely, for \(x \in \ker N\), define \(\xi_{\sigma^i}\) by \eqref{eq:gc-cyclic-cocycle}. Replacing \(i\) by \(i + n\) adds \(\sigma^iNx = 0\), so \(\xi\) is well defined on \(G\), and it is a cocycle, because

\begin{equation*} \xi_{\sigma^{i + j}} = (1 + \dots + \sigma^{i - 1})x + \sigma^i(1 + \dots + \sigma^{j - 1})x = \xi_{\sigma^i} + \sigma^i\xi_{\sigma^j}. \end{equation*}

In part 2, let \(x \in A\). Its stabilizer is open, so it contains \(r\Zhat\) for some \(r \geq 1\), and \(x\) has finite order \(e\). Define \(\xi_{\sigma^i}\) by \eqref{eq:gc-cyclic-cocycle} for \(i \geq 0\). For \(n \coloneqq re\),

\begin{equation*} \xi_{\sigma^{i + n}} - \xi_{\sigma^i} = \sigma^i(1 + \sigma + \dots + \sigma^{n - 1})x = \sigma^i e(1 + \sigma + \dots + \sigma^{r - 1})x = 0, \end{equation*}

since \(\sigma^r x = x\). So \(\xi\) only depends on \(i \bmod n\), and it extends to a continuous map on \(\Zhat\), through \(\Zhat \to \Z/n\Z\). The cocycle condition holds on the dense subset \(\sigma^{\Z_{\geq 0}}\) by the computation above, hence everywhere by continuity.

Example 7.2.5.

  1. Let \(G = \{1, \sigma\}\) act on \(\Z\) by \(\sigma a = -a\). Then \(N = 1 + \sigma = 0\), so \(H^1(G, \Z) = \Z/2\Z\) and \(H^0(G, \Z) = 0\). With the trivial action instead, \(H^0(G, \Z) = \Z\) and \(H^1(G, \Z) = \Hom(G, \Z) = 0\).
  2. Let \(G = \Gal(\C/\R) = \{1, c\}\). In multiplicative notation \(Nz = z\bar z\), and \((c - 1)\C^\times\) is the set of quotients \(\bar z/z\). Every \(w\) with \(w\bar w = 1\) is such a quotient: if \(w \neq -1\), take \(z \coloneqq 1 + \bar w\), and \(-1 = \bar\imath/i\). So \(H^1(G, \C^\times) = 1\), an instance of Hilbert's Theorem 90 below. On \(\mu_m(\C)\), \(c\) acts by inversion, so \(N = 1\) and \((c - 1)\mu_m(\C) = \mu_m(\C)^2\), and \(H^1(G, \mu_m(\C))\) has order \(\gcd(2, m)\). This is also the order of \(\R^\times/\R^{\times m}\), as Corollary 7.4.6 will explain.

For a group \(A\) that is not commutative, half of the theory survives. It is needed for the general linear group in Hilbert 90 and for automorphism groups in Section 7.6.

Definition 7.2.6. Let \(A\) be a \(G\)-group, written multiplicatively. Then \(H^0(G, A) \coloneqq A^G\). A \(1\)-cocycle is a continuous map \(\xi\colon G \to A\) with \(\xi_{gh} = \xi_g \cdot g\xi_h\) for all \(g, h \in G\). Two \(1\)-cocycles \(\xi, \xi'\) are cohomologous if there is \(b \in A\) with \(\xi'_g = b^{-1}\,\xi_g\,gb\) for every \(g \in G\). This is an equivalence relation, and the set of classes is \(H^1(G, A)\). It is a pointed set: a set with a distinguished element, the class of the trivial cocycle \(g \mapsto 1\). Its elements are the cocycles of the form \(g \mapsto b^{-1}\,gb\).

If \(A\) is commutative, Definitions 7.2.1 and 7.2.6 agree: \(b^{-1}\,\xi_g\,gb\) is \(\xi_g\) times the coboundary of \(b\). In general \(H^1(G, A)\) is not a group [Sil09, Section B.3]. For an algebraic group \(A\) over \(k\), not necessarily commutative, we still write \(H^1(k, A) \coloneqq H^1(\Gal_k, A(\ksep))\).

Draft8 Oct 20267.3 Functoriality, inflation and restriction

We are in Situation 7.0.1. A morphism \(f\colon A \to B\) of discrete \(G\)-modules induces homomorphisms \(f_*\colon H^i(G, A) \to H^i(G, B)\), by composing cochains with \(f\). The main tool for computing with them is the long exact sequence.

Proposition 7.3.1. Let \(0 \to A \xrightarrow{\iota} B \xrightarrow{\psi} C \to 0\) be an exact sequence of discrete \(G\)-modules. There is an exact sequence

\begin{equation*} \begin{gathered} 0 \to A^G \to B^G \to C^G \xrightarrow{\delta} H^1(G, A) \to H^1(G, B) \to H^1(G, C) \\ \xrightarrow{\delta} H^2(G, A) \to H^2(G, B) \to H^2(G, C), \end{gathered} \end{equation*}

where the unlabeled maps are \(\iota_*\) and \(\psi_*\). The connecting homomorphisms are as follows. For \(c \in C^G\), choose \(b \in B\) with \(\psi(b) = c\). Then

\begin{equation} \label{eq:gc-delta} \delta(c) = \text{the class of } g \mapsto gb - b. \tag{7.3.1} \end{equation}

For a \(1\)-cocycle \(\gamma\) with values in \(C\), choose a continuous \(\beta\colon G \to B\) with \(\psi \circ \beta = \gamma\). Then \(\delta([\gamma])\) is the class of \(d^1\beta\), which takes values in \(A\).

Proof.

We check the part through \(H^1(G, B)\), which is what we use most. The cochain \(g \mapsto gb - b\) is \(d^0b\), a cocycle with values in \(B\). Its values lie in \(A\), since \(\psi(gb - b) = gc - c = 0\), so it is a cocycle with values in \(A\). Another choice \(b + a\), with \(a \in A\), adds the coboundary \(d^0a\). So \(\delta\) is well defined, and it is a homomorphism.

Exactness at \(C^G\): \(\delta(c) = 0\) means that \(gb - b = ga - a\) for some \(a \in A\) and every \(g\), that is, \(b - a \in B^G\), and \(\psi(b - a) = c\). Exactness at \(H^1(G, A)\): the class of \(d^0b\) is \(0\) in \(H^1(G, B)\). Conversely, if a cocycle \(\xi\) with values in \(A\) is \(d^0b\) for some \(b \in B\), then \(g\psi(b) - \psi(b) = \psi(\xi_g) = 0\), so \(\psi(b) \in C^G\), and \(\delta(\psi(b)) = [\xi]\). Exactness at \(H^1(G, B)\): \(\psi \circ \iota = 0\). Conversely, if \(\psi \circ \xi = d^0c\) for some \(c \in C\), choose \(b\) with \(\psi(b) = c\). Then \(\xi - d^0b\) is a cocycle with values in \(A\), with the same class as \(\xi\) in \(H^1(G, B)\). The rest is the same kind of diagram chase [Poo02, Theorem 1]. The lift \(\beta\) exists because \(\gamma\) takes finitely many values and is constant on the cosets of an open normal subgroup, as in the proof of Proposition 7.3.6 below.

Students should compute with \eqref{eq:gc-delta}: Sections 7.4 and 7.5 apply it to the two Kummer sequences.

Remark 7.3.2. For an exact sequence \(1 \to A \to B \to C \to 1\) of \(G\)-groups, with \(A\) normal in \(B\), the same formula \eqref{eq:gc-delta}, written \(g \mapsto b^{-1}\,gb\), defines \(\delta\colon C^G \to H^1(G, A)\), and

\begin{equation*} 1 \to A^G \to B^G \to C^G \xrightarrow{\delta} H^1(G, A) \to H^1(G, B) \to H^1(G, C) \end{equation*}

is an exact sequence of pointed sets: at each term, the elements mapping to the distinguished element are the image of the previous map. If \(A\) is central in \(B\), then \(A\) is commutative, and the sequence continues with \(H^1(G, C) \to H^2(G, A)\). For example, \(1 \to (\ksep)^\times \to \GL_n(\ksep) \to \PGL_n(\ksep) \to 1\) gives a map \(H^1(k, \PGL_n) \to H^2(k, \Gm)\) [Poo17, Section 1.5.5].

Next we change the group. Let \(H \subseteq G\) be a closed subgroup. Then \(A\) is also a discrete \(H\)-module, and if \(H\) is normal, then \(A^H\) is a discrete \(G/H\)-module.

Definition 7.3.3. The restriction \(\Res\colon H^i(G, A) \to H^i(H, A)\) maps the class of a cocycle \(\xi\) to the class of its restriction to \(H^i\). If \(H\) is normal, the inflation \(\Inf\colon H^i(G/H, A^H) \to H^i(G, A)\) maps the class of \(\xi\) to the class of the composition \(G^i \to (G/H)^i \xrightarrow{\xi} A^H \subseteq A\). A cocycle of \(G\) of this form is inflated from \(G/H\) [Sil09, Section B.1].

For \(i = 0\), restriction is the inclusion \(A^G \subseteq A^H\), and inflation is the identity of \(A^G\). For a field extension \(L\) of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), we write \(\Res\colon H^i(k, A) \to H^i(L, A)\) for the restriction to \(\Gal_L\).

Remark 7.3.4. If \(H\) is open, there is also a corestriction \(\Cor\colon H^1(H, A) \to H^1(G, A)\), with \(\Cor \circ \Res = [G : H]\) [Mil20, Section IV.1]. For \(i = 0\) it is the norm \(a \mapsto \sum_t ta\), for \(t\) running over representatives of \(G/H\). We do not use it: the consequences we need are proved directly in Corollary 7.3.7.

The group \(G\) acts on \(H^1(H, A)\) for \(H\) normal, through

\begin{equation*} (g \cdot \xi)_h \coloneqq g\,\xi_{g^{-1}hg} \qquad (g \in G,\ h \in H). \end{equation*}

This maps cocycles to cocycles and coboundaries to coboundaries: \(g \cdot d^0a = d^0(ga)\).

Proposition 7.3.5 (Inflation–restriction). Let \(H\) be a closed normal subgroup of \(G\). Then \(H\) acts trivially on \(H^1(H, A)\), and the sequence

\begin{equation*} 0 \to H^1(G/H, A^H) \xrightarrow{\Inf} H^1(G, A) \xrightarrow{\Res} H^1(H, A)^{G/H} \end{equation*}

is exact.

Proof.

Let \(\xi\) be a cocycle on \(G\), or on \(H\) if \(g \in H\). Then \(\xi_{g^{-1}hg} = \xi_{g^{-1}} + g^{-1}\xi_h + g^{-1}h\xi_g\), and \(0 = \xi_{gg^{-1}} = \xi_g + g\xi_{g^{-1}}\), so

\begin{equation*} (g \cdot \xi)_h = \xi_h + h\xi_g - \xi_g. \end{equation*}

So \(g \cdot \xi\) and \(\xi\) differ by the coboundary of \(\xi_g\) on \(H\). For \(g \in H\) this shows that \(H\) acts trivially, and for \(\xi\) defined on \(G\) it shows that \(\Res\) takes values in the invariants.

A cocycle inflated from \(G/H\) is constant on \(H\), equal to \(\xi_1 = 0\), so \(\Res \circ \Inf = 0\). Inflation is injective: if \(\xi\colon G/H \to A^H\) inflates to \(g \mapsto ga - a\), then for \(h \in H\), \(ha - a = \xi_1 = 0\), so \(a \in A^H\) and \(\xi\) is already a coboundary on \(G/H\). Finally let \(\xi\) be a cocycle on \(G\) whose restriction to \(H\) is \(h \mapsto ha - a\). Subtracting the coboundary of \(a\), we may assume \(\xi_h = 0\) for \(h \in H\). Then \(\xi_{gh} = \xi_g + g\xi_h = \xi_g\), so \(\xi\) is constant on the cosets \(gH\), and \(h\xi_g = \xi_h + h\xi_g = \xi_{hg} = \xi_{g(g^{-1}hg)} = \xi_g\), so \(\xi_g \in A^H\). Hence \(\xi\) is inflated from a continuous cocycle on \(G/H\) [Sil09, Proposition B.1.3 and Exercise B.3].

The cohomology of a profinite group is the union of the cohomology of its finite quotients. For \(U \subseteq V\) open normal subgroups of \(G\), inflation \(H^i(G/V, A^V) \to H^i(G/U, A^U)\) makes these groups a direct system.

Proposition 7.3.6. For \(i = 0, 1, 2\), inflation induces an isomorphism

\begin{equation*} \varinjlim_U H^i(G/U, A^U) \to H^i(G, A), \end{equation*}

where \(U\) runs over the open normal subgroups of \(G\). For \(G = \Gal_k\), the groups \(G/U\) are the \(\Gal(L/k)\), for \(L\) finite Galois over \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), and \(A^U = A^{\Gal_L}\). The same holds for \(H^1\) of a \(G\)-group.

Proof.

Let \(f\colon G^i \to A\) be continuous. Since \(G^i\) is compact and \(A\) discrete, \(f\) takes finitely many values, and its fibers are open. Each element of \(G^i\) has a neighborhood \(x U_x^i\), for an open normal subgroup \(U_x\), on which \(f\) is constant. Finitely many of these cover \(G^i\). Let \(U\) be the intersection of the corresponding \(U_x\) and of the stabilizers of the finitely many values of \(f\), shrunk to an open normal subgroup. Then \(f\) is constant on the cosets of \(U^i\) and takes values in \(A^U\), so it is inflated from a cochain \((G/U)^i \to A^U\). Inflation commutes with the coboundary maps, and \(G^i \to (G/U)^i\) is surjective, so \(f\) is a cocycle exactly when the cochain on \(G/U\) is one. So the map is surjective. If an inflated cocycle is the coboundary of a cochain on \(G\), that cochain is itself inflated from some \(G/U'\) with \(U' \subseteq U\), and the cocycle is a coboundary on \(G/U'\). So the map is injective [Sil09, Exercise B.4]. For \(\Gal_k\), see part 1 of Proposition 7.1.1.

Corollary 7.3.7. Let \(i \in \{1, 2\}\).

  1. If \(G\) is finite of order \(n\), then \(nH^i(G, A) = 0\).
  2. The group \(H^i(G, A)\) is torsion.
  3. If \(mA = 0\), then \(mH^i(G, A) = 0\).
  4. If \(L\) is a finite Galois extension of \(k\) inside \(\ksep\), then \(\ker(\Res\colon H^1(k, A) \to H^1(L, A))\) is killed by \([L : k]\).
Proof.

For part 1 with \(i = 1\), let \(\xi\) be a cocycle and \(a \coloneqq \sum_{h \in G}\xi_h\). As \(h\) runs over \(G\), so does \(gh\), so summing \eqref{eq:gc-cocycle} over \(h\) gives \(a = n\xi_g + ga\). Hence \(n\xi = d^0(-a)\). For \(i = 2\), let \(\xi\) be a \(2\)-cocycle and \(\eta_g \coloneqq \sum_{h \in G}\xi_{g, h}\). Summing \(d^2\xi = 0\) over \(g_3 \in G\) gives \(g_1\eta_{g_2} - \eta_{g_1g_2} + \eta_{g_1} - n\xi_{g_1, g_2} = 0\), so \(n\xi = d^1\eta\). Part 2 follows from part 1 and Proposition 7.3.6. Part 3 holds already for cochains. For part 4, the kernel is the image of \(H^1(\Gal(L/k), A^{\Gal_L})\) by Proposition 7.3.5, and part 1 applies.

Example 7.3.8. Let \(L \coloneqq k(E[m])\). Then \(\Gal_L\) acts trivially on \(E[m](\ksep)\), and Proposition 7.3.5 and Proposition 7.2.3 give an exact sequence

\begin{equation*} 0 \to H^1(\Gal(L/k), E[m](\ksep)) \to H^1(k, E[m]) \to \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_L, E[m](\ksep))^{\Gal(L/k)}. \end{equation*}

The group on the left is finite, as \(\Gal(L/k)\) and \(E[m](\ksep)\) are. So, up to a finite group, \(H^1(k, E[m])\) is a group of homomorphisms, that is, of abelian extensions of \(L\). This is how Theorem 7.8.6 will be proved.

Theorem 7.4.1 (Dedekind). Let \(F\) be a field and \(H\) a group. Distinct homomorphisms \(\chi_1, \dots, \chi_r\colon H \to F^\times\) are linearly independent over \(F\): if \(a_1\chi_1 + \dots + a_r\chi_r = 0\) as a function \(H \to F\), with \(a_i \in F\), then \(a_1 = \dots = a_r = 0\).

Proof (Mil22).

See [Mil22, Theorem 5.14].

Theorem 7.4.2 (Hilbert 90). Let \(L/k\) be a finite Galois extension with group \(G\), inside \(\ksep\).

  1. \(H^1(G, L^\times) = 1\), and \(H^1(k, \Gm) = 1\).
  2. \(H^1(G, L) = 0\), and \(H^1(k, \Ga) = 0\).
  3. For every \(n \geq 1\), \(H^1(G, \GL_n(L))\) and \(H^1(k, \GL_n)\) have one element.
Proof.

In each part, the statement over \(k\) follows from the one for finite extensions by Proposition 7.3.6, since \(L^\times = ((\ksep)^\times)^{\Gal_L}\), and likewise for \(L\) and \(\GL_n(L)\).

For part 1, let \(\xi\colon G \to L^\times\) be a cocycle. The elements of \(G\) restrict to distinct homomorphisms \(L^\times \to L^\times\), and the \(\xi_h\) are non-zero, so by Theorem 7.4.1 there is \(c \in L^\times\) with

\begin{equation*} b \coloneqq \sum_{h \in G} \xi_h \cdot h(c) \neq 0. \end{equation*}

The cocycle condition gives \(g(\xi_h) = \xi_g^{-1}\xi_{gh}\), so

\begin{equation*} g(b) = \sum_{h \in G} g(\xi_h) \cdot (gh)(c) = \xi_g^{-1}\sum_{h \in G} \xi_{gh} \cdot (gh)(c) = \xi_g^{-1}b, \end{equation*}

since \(gh\) runs over \(G\) with \(h\). So \(\xi_g = b/g(b) = g(b^{-1})/b^{-1}\), the coboundary of \(b^{-1}\).

For part 2, Theorem 7.4.1 with all coefficients \(1\) gives \(c \in L\) with \(t \coloneqq \Tr_{L/k}(c) \neq 0\). For a cocycle \(\xi\colon G \to L\), let \(b \coloneqq t^{-1}\sum_h \xi_h \cdot h(c)\). Then \(g(\xi_h) = \xi_{gh} - \xi_g\), so

\begin{equation*} g(b) = t^{-1}\sum_{h \in G} (\xi_{gh} - \xi_g) \cdot (gh)(c) = b - \xi_g, \end{equation*}

and \(\xi\) is the coboundary of \(-b\).

For part 3, let \(\xi\colon G \to \GL_n(L)\) be a cocycle, and let \(W\) be \(L^n\) with the action \(g * w \coloneqq \xi_g \cdot gw\), where \(gw\) is the action on coordinates. The cocycle condition says exactly that this is an action, and it is semilinear: \(g * (\lambda w) = (g\lambda)(g * w)\) for \(\lambda \in L\). By Galois descent for vector spaces, every \(n\)-dimensional \(L\)-vector space with a semilinear action of \(G\) is isomorphic to \(L^n\) with the action on coordinates [Poo17, Corollary 1.3.12]. So there is \(\varphi \in \GL_n(L)\) with \(\varphi(gw) = g * \varphi(w) = \xi_g \cdot (g\varphi)(gw)\) for all \(w\), that is, \(\xi_g = \varphi\,(g\varphi)^{-1}\). With \(b \coloneqq \varphi^{-1}\) this is \(\xi_g = b^{-1}\,gb\), so \(\xi\) is cohomologous to the trivial cocycle [Poo17, Proposition 1.3.15].

Part 1 was already used in the proof of Proposition 2.1.17. Hilbert stated it for cyclic extensions.

Corollary 7.4.3. Let \(L/k\) be a finite cyclic extension, with \(\Gal(L/k) = \langle\sigma\rangle\). If \(a \in L^\times\) has \(N_{L/k}(a) = 1\), then \(a = \sigma(b)/b\) for some \(b \in L^\times\).

In multiplicative notation, part 1 of Proposition 7.2.4 identifies \(H^1(\Gal(L/k), L^\times)\) with the group of the \(a\) of norm \(1\), modulo the quotients \(\sigma(b)/b\). It is trivial by part 1 of Theorem 7.4.2.

Example 7.4.4. Let \((x, y)\) be a rational point of the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) over \(\Q\). The element \(a \coloneqq x + iy\) of \(\Q(i)\) has norm \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), so by Corollary 7.4.3, with \(\sigma\) complex conjugation, \(a = \sigma(\beta)/\beta\) for some \(\beta = u - iv \in \Q(i)^\times\). Then

\begin{equation*} x + iy = \frac{u + iv}{u - iv} = \frac{u^2 - v^2}{u^2 + v^2} + i\,\frac{2uv}{u^2 + v^2}, \end{equation*}

which is the usual parametrization of the rational points of the circle.

Definition 7.4.5. The Brauer group of \(k\) is \(\Br(k) \coloneqq H^2(k, \Gm) = H^2(\Gal_k, (\ksep)^\times)\).

This is the cohomological definition. The group \(\Br(k)\) is also the group of similarity classes of central simple algebras over \(k\) [Poo17, Theorem 1.5.12]. Corollary 7.4.6 (Kummer sequence).

  1. The sequence of discrete \(\Gal_k\)-modules

    \begin{equation} \label{eq:gc-kummer-gm} 1 \to \mu_m(\ksep) \to (\ksep)^\times \xrightarrow{x \mapsto x^m} (\ksep)^\times \to 1 \tag{7.4.1} \end{equation}

    is exact.

  2. Its connecting homomorphism induces an isomorphism

    \begin{equation*} \delta\colon k^\times/k^{\times m} \to H^1(k, \mu_m), \qquad \delta(a) = \text{the class of } g \mapsto g(\alpha)/\alpha, \end{equation*}

    for any \(\alpha \in \ksep\) with \(\alpha^m = a\).

  3. There is an isomorphism \(H^2(k, \mu_m) \cong \Br(k)[m]\).
Proof.

For \(a \in (\ksep)^\times\), the polynomial \(x^m - a\) is separable: its derivative \(mx^{m - 1}\) vanishes only at \(0\), since \(m \neq 0\) in \(k\), and \(0\) is not a root. So it has \(m\) distinct roots, all in \(\ksep\). So \(x \mapsto x^m\) is surjective on \((\ksep)^\times\), with kernel \(\mu_m(\ksep)\) of order \(m\), and it commutes with the action of \(\Gal_k\). This is part 1. Proposition 7.3.1 and part 1 of Theorem 7.4.2 give exact sequences

\begin{gather*} k^\times \xrightarrow{m} k^\times \xrightarrow{\delta} H^1(k, \mu_m) \to H^1(k, \Gm) = 1, \\ 1 = H^1(k, \Gm) \to H^2(k, \mu_m) \to \Br(k) \xrightarrow{m} \Br(k), \end{gather*}

which are parts 2 and 3. The formula for \(\delta\) is \eqref{eq:gc-delta}, as \(\alpha\) lifts \(a\) [Sil09, Proposition VIII.2.2].

The proof shows more: injectivity of \(\delta\) on \(k^\times/k^{\times m}\) is elementary, and only its surjectivity uses Hilbert 90. If \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq k\), then \(\delta(a)\) is the homomorphism \(g \mapsto g(\alpha)/\alpha\), and this is Kummer theory.

Corollary 7.4.7 (Kummer theory). Suppose that \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq k\).

  1. The map \(\delta\) is an isomorphism \(k^\times/k^{\times m} \to \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, \mu_m(\ksep))\). For \(a \in k^\times\) and \(\alpha^m = a\), \(\ker\delta(a) = \Gal_{k(\alpha)}\), and \(k(\alpha)/k\) is cyclic of degree the order of \(a\) in \(k^\times/k^{\times m}\).

Proof.

We use that a finite abelian group \(B\) of exponent dividing \(m\) has as many homomorphisms to the cyclic group \(\mu_m(\ksep)\) as elements [Mil22, discussion before Theorem 5.30].

Part 1 is part 2 of Corollary 7.4.6 and Proposition 7.2.3. The kernel of \(\chi \coloneqq \delta(a)\) is the set of \(g\) fixing \(\alpha\), which is \(\Gal_{k(\alpha)}\). It is normal, so \(k(\alpha)/k\) is Galois with group \(\Gal_k/\ker\chi \cong \chi(\Gal_k)\), a subgroup of the cyclic group \(\mu_m(\ksep)\), of order the order of \(\chi\), which is the order of \(a\).

The pairing \(\Gal(L_\Phi/k) \times \Phi \to \mu_m(\ksep)\) has trivial left kernel, so \(\Gal(L_\Phi/k)\) embeds in \(\Hom(\Phi, \mu_m(\ksep))\). It is therefore abelian of exponent dividing \(m\), and \([L_\Phi : k] \leq \#\Phi\). Since \(\Phi \subseteq \Phi_{L_\Phi}\),

\begin{equation*} [L_\Phi : k] \leq \#\Phi \leq \#\Phi_{L_\Phi} = [L_\Phi : k]. \end{equation*}

Draft8 Oct 20267.6 Non-abelian \(H^1\) and twists

We are in Situation 7.0.1. Varieties that become isomorphic over \(\ksep\) are classified by a non-abelian \(H^1\). This section gives the general principle and its simplest examples. The quartic, sextic and cubic twists of elliptic curves, and the torsors under \(E\), are in Chapter 10.

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be \(k\)-varieties. The group \(\Gal_k\) acts on the morphisms \(\varphi\colon X_{\ksep} \to Y_{\ksep}\) by \(g\varphi \coloneqq g \circ \varphi \circ g^{-1}\), where \(g\) acts on \(\ksep\)-points through its action on \(\ksep\). So \((g\varphi)(gP) = g(\varphi(P))\) for \(P \in X(\ksep)\). A morphism is defined over \(k\) exactly when it is fixed by \(\Gal_k\) [Poo17, Section 4.4], and every \(\varphi\) is defined over a finite extension of \(k\), so this makes \(\Aut(X_{\ksep})\) a \(\Gal_k\)-group.

Definition 7.6.1. A twist of a \(k\)-variety \(X\) is a \(k\)-variety \(Y\) such that \(Y_{\ksep} \cong X_{\ksep}\). We write \(\operatorname{Twist}(X/k)\) for the set of \(k\)-isomorphism classes of twists of \(X\), a pointed set with distinguished element the class of \(X\).

Theorem 7.6.2 (Twisting principle). Let \(X\) be a quasi-projective \(k\)-variety. For a twist \(Y\) of \(X\), choose an isomorphism \(\varphi\colon X_{\ksep} \to Y_{\ksep}\). Then \(\xi_g \coloneqq \varphi^{-1} \circ g\varphi\) is a \(1\)-cocycle with values in \(\Aut(X_{\ksep})\), and \(Y \mapsto [\xi]\) is a well defined bijection of pointed sets

\begin{equation*} \operatorname{Twist}(X/k) \to H^1(k, \Aut(X_{\ksep})). \end{equation*}
Proof.

Since \(g(\varphi^{-1} \circ h\varphi) = (g\varphi)^{-1} \circ gh\varphi\),

\begin{equation*} \xi_{gh} = \varphi^{-1} \circ gh\varphi = (\varphi^{-1} \circ g\varphi) \circ (g\varphi)^{-1} \circ gh\varphi = \xi_g \cdot g\xi_h. \end{equation*}

If \(\varphi\) is defined over a finite Galois extension \(L\), then \(\xi_{gh} = \xi_g\) for \(h \in \Gal_L\), so \(\xi\) is continuous. Another choice \(\varphi \circ \alpha\), with \(\alpha \in \Aut(X_{\ksep})\), replaces \(\xi_g\) by \(\alpha^{-1}\,\xi_g\,g\alpha\), a cohomologous cocycle, and composing \(\varphi\) with a \(k\)-isomorphism \(Y \to Y'\) does not change \(\xi\). So the map is well defined, and it maps \(X\) to the trivial class.

Injectivity: suppose that \(Y\) and \(Y'\), with \(\varphi\) and \(\varphi'\), give cohomologous cocycles. Changing \(\varphi\) by an automorphism of \(X_{\ksep}\), we may assume that the cocycles are equal: \(\varphi^{-1} \circ g\varphi = \varphi'^{-1} \circ g\varphi'\) for all \(g\). Then \(\psi \coloneqq \varphi' \circ \varphi^{-1}\colon Y_{\ksep} \to Y'_{\ksep}\) satisfies \(g\psi = \psi\) for all \(g\), so it is defined over \(k\), and \(Y \cong Y'\). Surjectivity is Galois descent: a cocycle defines a descent datum on \(X_L\) for a finite Galois extension \(L\), and quasi-projectivity makes it effective. Its proof generalizes the descent for vector spaces used for part 3 of Theorem 7.4.2. See [Poo17, Corollary 4.4.6 and Theorem 4.5.2] and, for curves, [Sil09, Theorem X.2.2].

The same proof applies to varieties with extra structure, with \(\Aut\) replaced by the automorphisms preserving that structure. For an algebraic group, these are the automorphisms of algebraic groups. For an elliptic curve \(E\), they are the automorphisms of \(E_{\ksep}\) fixing \(O\), the group \(\Aut(E_{\ksep})\) of Chapter 4. A twist of \(E\) in this sense is an elliptic curve \(E'\) over \(k\) with \(E'_{\ksep} \cong E_{\ksep}\) as elliptic curves [Sil09, Proposition X.5.3], [Poo02, Section 5].

Example 7.6.3. A morphism \(\Gm \to \Gm\) over a field is a unit of the ring \(\ksep[t, t^{-1}]\), so it has the form \(t \mapsto ct^n\), and it is a homomorphism exactly when \(c = 1\). So the automorphisms of \(\Gm\) as an algebraic group, over any field, are \(t \mapsto t^{\pm 1}\), and \(\Gal_k\) acts trivially on this group of order \(2\). By the twisting principle and Proposition 7.2.3, the twists of \(\Gm\) as an algebraic group are classified by

\begin{equation*} H^1(k, \Z/2\Z) = \Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, \Z/2\Z), \end{equation*}

that is, by the separable quadratic extensions of \(k\), together with the trivial class. If \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), this group is \(k^\times/k^{\times 2}\) by Corollary 7.4.6, and in characteristic \(2\) it is \(k/\wp(k)\) by Remark 7.4.10.

These twists are the twisted multiplicative groups \(\Gm^{(d)}\) of Example 2.1.8. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\) and \(d\) is not a square, so that \(L_d = k(\sqrt d)\). The homomorphism \(\psi\colon \Gm^{(d)} \to \Gm\), \((x, y) \mapsto x + \sqrt d\,y\), of the proof of Proposition 2.1.9 is an isomorphism over \(L_d\). For \((x, y) \in \Gm^{(d)}(\ksep)\) and \(g \in \Gal_k\),

\begin{equation*} (g\psi)(x, y) = x + g(\sqrt d)\,y = \psi(x, y)^{\chi_d(g)}, \end{equation*}

since \(x - \sqrt d\,y = (x + \sqrt d\,y)^{-1}\) on \(\Gm^{(d)}\), where \(\chi_d\) is the quadratic character of Example 7.4.8. So for \(\varphi \coloneqq \psi^{-1}\), the cocycle \(\varphi^{-1} \circ g\varphi\) is \(t \mapsto t^{\chi_d(g)}\), the class \(\chi_d = \delta(d)\). The twisting principle therefore says that \(\Gm^{(d)} \cong \Gm^{(d')}\) exactly when \(d/d' \in k^{\times 2}\), and that \(\Gm^{(d)} \not\cong \Gm\) when \(d \notin k^{\times 2}\). These are parts 2 and 3 of Proposition 2.1.9, and every twist of \(\Gm\) as an algebraic group is of this form. As a variety, \(\Gm\) has the automorphisms \(t \mapsto ct^{\pm 1}\) as well, and its twists as a variety also include the affine curves \(x^2 - dy^2 = c\), for \(c \in k^\times\).

Example 7.6.4. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(k\) with \(j(E) \neq 0, 1728\). By Theorem 4.7.11, \(\Aut(E_{\ksep}) = \{\pm 1\}\), with the trivial action of \(\Gal_k\), since \([-1]\) is defined over \(k\). So the twists of \(E\) as an elliptic curve are classified by \(\Hom_{\mathrm{conts}}(\Gal_k, \{\pm 1\})\). Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2, 3\), so that \(E\) has a short Weierstrass model. Then this group is \(H^1(k, \mu_2) \cong k^\times/k^{\times 2}\), and the class of \(d\) is the quadratic twist \(E^d\) of Chapter 4. Indeed, for \(E\colon y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\), the isomorphism \(\varphi\colon E \to E^d\), \((x, y) \mapsto (dx, d^{3/2}y)\), satisfies

\begin{equation*} (g\varphi)(x, y) = \bigl(dx, g(d^{3/2})\,y\bigr) = \bigl(dx, \chi_d(g)\,d^{3/2}y\bigr) = \varphi([\chi_d(g)](x, y)), \end{equation*}

as \(g(d^{3/2}) = d \cdot g(\sqrt d)\). So \(\varphi^{-1} \circ g\varphi = [\chi_d(g)]\), the class \(\delta(d)\). This explains the computation before Example 4.1.9: \(E^d\) and \(E^{d'}\) are isomorphic over \(k\) exactly when \(d/d' \in k^{\times 2}\), and every twist of \(E\) is one of them. For \(j(E) = 0\) or \(1728\), and in characteristic \(2\) and \(3\), \(\Aut(E_{\ksep})\) is larger, and so is the set of twists (Chapter 10).

Example 7.6.5. The automorphism group of \(\PP^1_{\ksep}\) is \(\PGL_2(\ksep)\), so the twists of \(\PP^1\) are classified by \(H^1(k, \PGL_2)\). They are the nice curves of genus \(0\) over \(k\) [Poo17, Example 4.5.8], that is, the smooth plane conics. The conic \(C_2\) of Example 2.3.6 is a non-trivial twist of \(\PP^1_\Q\), trivialized over \(\Q(\sqrt 3)\). The map \(H^1(k, \PGL_2) \to \Br(k)\) of Remark 7.3.2 sends a conic to the class of a quaternion algebra [Poo17, Section 4.5], an element of \(\Br(k)[2]\) (Section 7.9). A conic is isomorphic to \(\PP^1\) if and only if it has a rational point [Poo17, Proposition 4.5.10].

Draft8 Oct 20267.7 Local fields: unramified classes

Situation 7.7.1. In addition to Situation 7.0.1, we use the notation of Situation 6.4.1: \(k_v\) is the completion of a global field at a non-archimedean place \(v\), with normalized valuation \(v\), ring of integers \(\calO_v\), uniformizer \(\pi_v\) and residue field \(\F_q\) of characteristic \(p\), and \(I_v \subseteq \Gal_{k_v}\) is the inertia group.

  • \(E\) is an elliptic curve over \(k_v\).
  • \(\sigma \in \Gal(k_v^{\unr}/k_v)\) is the Frobenius automorphism, which corresponds to \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha^q\) under \(\Gal(k_v^{\unr}/k_v) \cong \Gal_{\F_q} \cong \Zhat\), and is a topological generator. It is not the Frobenius isogeny \(\Frob\).
  • For a discrete \(\Gal_{k_v}\)-module \(A\), \(H^i(k_v, A) \coloneqq H^i(\Gal_{k_v}, A)\), and likewise for algebraic groups, with \(k_v^{\operatorname{sep}}\) in place of \(\ksep\).

The inertia group is a closed normal subgroup of \(\Gal_{k_v}\), with quotient \(\Gal_{k_v}/I_v \cong \Gal(k_v^{\unr}/k_v)\) by (6.4.1). A class is unramified when it comes from this quotient.

Definition 7.7.2. Let \(A\) be a discrete \(\Gal_{k_v}\)-module. A class \(\xi \in H^1(k_v, A)\) is unramified if its restriction to \(H^1(I_v, A)\) is trivial. The unramified classes form the subgroup

\begin{equation*} H^1_{\unr}(k_v, A) \coloneqq \ker\bigl(H^1(k_v, A) \xrightarrow{\Res} H^1(I_v, A)\bigr). \end{equation*}

Proposition 7.7.3. Let \(A\) be a discrete \(\Gal_{k_v}\)-module.

  1. Inflation is an isomorphism \(H^1(\Gal(k_v^{\unr}/k_v), A^{I_v}) \to H^1_{\unr}(k_v, A)\).
  2. If \(A\) is a torsion group, then \(\xi \mapsto \xi_\sigma\) induces an isomorphism \(H^1_{\unr}(k_v, A) \cong A^{I_v}/(\sigma - 1)A^{I_v}\).
  3. If \(A\) is finite, then \(\#H^1_{\unr}(k_v, A) = \#A^{\Gal_{k_v}}\).
Proof.

Part 1 is Proposition 7.3.5 with \(H = I_v\). Part 2 follows, by part 2 of Proposition 7.2.4 for the procyclic group \(\Gal(k_v^{\unr}/k_v)\). For part 3, let \(B \coloneqq A^{I_v}\), a finite group. The kernel of \(\sigma - 1\) on \(B\) is the set of elements fixed by \(\sigma\), hence by the closure of \(\sigma^{\Z}\), which is all of \(\Gal_{k_v}/I_v\). So it is \(A^{\Gal_{k_v}}\), and \(\#B/(\sigma - 1)B = \#\ker(\sigma - 1) = \#A^{\Gal_{k_v}}\).

For the multiplicative group, unramified classes are those of units.

Proposition 7.7.4. Suppose \(p \nmid m\), and let \(a \in k_v^\times\). The class \(\delta(a) \in H^1(k_v, \mu_m)\) is unramified if and only if \(v(a) \equiv 0 \pmod m\). So \(\delta\) induces an isomorphism \(\calO_v^\times/\calO_v^{\times m} \to H^1_{\unr}(k_v, \mu_m)\).

Proof.

Let \(\alpha \in k_v^{\operatorname{sep}}\) with \(\alpha^m = a\). The restriction of \(\delta(a)\) to \(I_v\) is the cocycle \(g \mapsto g(\alpha)/\alpha\), with values in \(\mu_m(k_v^{\operatorname{sep}})\), on which \(I_v\) acts trivially by part 1 of Example 6.4.5. So it is a homomorphism, and it is trivial if and only if \(I_v\) fixes \(\alpha\), that is, \(\alpha \in k_v^{\unr}\). If \(v(a) = mn\), then \(a = \pi_v^{mn}u\) with \(u \in \calO_v^\times\). The polynomial \(x^m - \bar u\) is separable over \(\F_q\), so its \(m\) roots lie in a finite field \(\F_{q^r}\), and by Hensel's lemma [Poo17, Theorem 3.5.63] they lift to the \(m\) roots of \(x^m - u\) in the unramified extension of degree \(r\). One of them is \(\alpha/\pi_v^n\), so \(\alpha \in k_v^{\unr}\). Conversely, the valuation of \(k_v^{\unr}\) has value group \(\Z\), so if \(\alpha \in k_v^{\unr}\), then \(v(a) = mv(\alpha) \equiv 0 \pmod m\). The elements \(a\) with \(v(a) \equiv 0 \pmod m\) are those of \(\calO_v^\times k_v^{\times m}\), and \(\calO_v^\times \cap k_v^{\times m} = \calO_v^{\times m}\).

As a check of part 3 of Proposition 7.7.3: by Hensel's lemma, \(\calO_v^\times/\calO_v^{\times m} \cong \F_q^\times/\F_q^{\times m}\), of order \(\gcd(m, q - 1) = \#\mu_m(\F_q) = \#\mu_m(k_v)\). For \(m \geq 2\), the class of \(\pi_v\) is ramified: \(k_v(\pi_v^{1/m})\) is totally ramified, as in part 3 of Example 6.4.5.

For an elliptic curve the definition applies to \(E[m]\). If \(E\) has good reduction and \(p \nmid m\), then \(E[m](k_v^{\operatorname{sep}})\) is unramified by Proposition 6.4.6, so

\begin{equation*} H^1_{\unr}(k_v, E[m]) \cong E[m](k_v^{\operatorname{sep}})/(\sigma - 1)E[m](k_v^{\operatorname{sep}}), \end{equation*}

of order \(\#E(k_v)[m]\). Which classes come from \(E(k_v)\) under the Kummer map is the subject of Chapter 8.

Draft8 Oct 20267.8 Global fields: classes unramified outside \(S\)

Situation 7.8.1. In addition to Situation 7.0.1:

  • \(k\) is a global field, with set of places \(\Omega_k\) and completions \(k_v\).
  • For each \(v \in \Omega_k\), we fix a \(k\)-embedding \(\ksep \to k_v^{\operatorname{sep}}\). Restriction to \(\ksep\) is then an injection \(\Gal_{k_v} \to \Gal_k\), whose image is a decomposition group at \(v\). Another embedding changes it by conjugation.
  • For a discrete \(\Gal_k\)-module \(A\), \(\operatorname{res}_v\colon H^1(k, A) \to H^1(k_v, A)\) is the restriction along \(\Gal_{k_v} \to \Gal_k\). For a commutative algebraic group \(A\) over \(k\), it is composed with \(A(\ksep) \to A(k_v^{\operatorname{sep}})\).
  • \(S \subseteq \Omega_k\) is a finite non-empty set of places containing the archimedean ones and the non-archimedean \(v\) with \(v(m) > 0\), so that \(m\) is a unit outside \(S\).
  • \(\calO_S \coloneqq \{a \in k : v(a) \geq 0 \text{ for all non-archimedean } v \notin S\}\) is the ring of \(S\)-integers, with unit group \(\calO_S^\times\) and ideal class group \(\Cl(\calO_S)\).

A class \(\xi \in H^1(k, A)\) is unramified at a non-archimedean place \(v\) if \(\operatorname{res}_v(\xi)\) is unramified in the sense of Definition 7.7.2. This does not depend on the embedding: conjugating \(I_v\) by \(g \in \Gal_k\) replaces the restriction of \(\xi\) by \(g \cdot \xi\), which is cohomologous to it by the proof of Proposition 7.3.5 [Sil09, Section VIII.2 and Exercise B.6].

Definition 7.8.2. Let \(A\) be a discrete \(\Gal_k\)-module, or a commutative algebraic group over \(k\), and \(T \subseteq \Omega_k\) a finite set of places. The group of classes unramified outside \(T\) is

\begin{equation*} H^1(k, A; T) \coloneqq \{\xi \in H^1(k, A) : \xi \text{ is unramified at every non-archimedean } v \notin T\}. \end{equation*}

Also

\begin{equation*} k(T, m) \coloneqq \{a \in k^\times/k^{\times m} : v(a) \equiv 0 \pmod m \text{ for all non-archimedean } v \notin T\}. \end{equation*}

The Kummer classes unramified outside \(S\) can be computed exactly.

Proposition 7.8.3.

  1. The isomorphism \(\delta\colon k^\times/k^{\times m} \to H^1(k, \mu_m)\) maps \(k(S, m)\) onto \(H^1(k, \mu_m; S)\).

  2. There is an exact sequence

    \begin{equation*} 1 \to \calO_S^\times/\calO_S^{\times m} \to k(S, m) \to \Cl(\calO_S)[m] \to 0, \end{equation*}

    where the map on the right sends \(a\) to the class of the fractional ideal \(\mathfrak a\) with \(a\calO_S = \mathfrak a^m\).

  3. If \(\Cl(\calO_S)\) is finite and \(\calO_S^\times\) is finitely generated, then \(k(S, m)\) and \(H^1(k, \mu_m; S)\) are finite.
Proof.

For part 1, \(\operatorname{res}_v\delta(a) = \delta(a)\) computed over \(k_v\), by the formula of Corollary 7.4.6. For non-archimedean \(v \notin S\) the residue characteristic does not divide \(m\), and Proposition 7.7.4 says that \(\delta(a)\) is unramified at \(v\) exactly when \(v(a) \equiv 0 \pmod m\).

For part 2, the prime ideals of the Dedekind domain \(\calO_S\) are those of the non-archimedean places outside \(S\). If \(a\) represents an element of \(k(S, m)\), the exponents of its fractional ideal \(a\calO_S\) are divisible by \(m\), so it is \(\mathfrak a^m\) for a unique fractional ideal \(\mathfrak a\). Replacing \(a\) by \(ac^m\) replaces \(\mathfrak a\) by \(c\mathfrak a\), so \(a \mapsto [\mathfrak a]\) is a well defined homomorphism, with values in \(\Cl(\calO_S)[m]\). It is surjective: if \(\mathfrak a^m = a\calO_S\), then \(a\) represents an element of \(k(S, m)\) mapping to \([\mathfrak a]\). If \(\mathfrak a = c\calO_S\) is principal, then \(a/c^m \in \calO_S^\times\), so the kernel is the image of \(\calO_S^\times\). Finally, if \(u \in \calO_S^\times\) is \(c^m\) with \(c \in k^\times\), then \(mv(c) = v(u) = 0\) for \(v \notin S\), so \(c \in \calO_S^\times\). Part 3 follows: \(\calO_S^\times/\calO_S^{\times m}\) is a finitely generated group killed by \(m\).

The proofs of parts 2 and 3 use only that \(S\) is finite, non-empty and contains the archimedean places. So these two parts hold for such \(S\) even when \(m\) is not a unit outside \(S\).

Theorem 7.8.4. If \(k\) is a number field, then \(\Cl(\calO_S)\) is finite and \(\calO_S^\times\) is finitely generated (Dirichlet's \(S\)-unit theorem). The same holds for a global function field, since \(S\) is non-empty.

Proof (Sil09).

For number fields, see [Sil09, proof of Proposition VIII.1.6], which quotes them.

Example 7.8.5. Let \(k = \Q\), \(m = 2\) and \(S = \{\infty, 2\}\). Then \(\calO_S = \Z[1/2]\), a principal ideal domain with unit group \(\{\pm 2^n : n \in \Z\}\). So \(\Q(S, 2) = \langle -1, 2 \rangle\), of order \(4\), and its non-trivial elements correspond to \(\Q(i)\), \(\Q(\sqrt 2)\) and \(\Q(\sqrt{-2})\), the quadratic fields unramified outside \(2\) and \(\infty\). More generally, for a finite set \(S\) of primes together with \(\infty\), \(\Q(S, 2)\) is generated by \(-1\) and the primes in \(S\), and has order \(2^{\#S}\). For \(k = \Q(\sqrt{-5})\), \(m = 2\) and \(S\) the archimedean place, which is allowed in parts 2 and 3 of Proposition 7.8.3 by the remark after it, \(\calO_S^\times/\calO_S^{\times 2} = \{\pm 1\}\), and \(\Cl(\calO_S)\) has order \(2\), generated by \(\mathfrak p \coloneqq (2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})\) with \(\mathfrak p^2 = (2)\). So \(k(S, 2) = \langle -1, 2 \rangle\) has order \(4\), although \(2\) is not a unit.

The next theorem is the cohomological form of the finiteness that drives the weak Mordell–Weil theorem.

Theorem 7.8.6. Suppose \(k\) is a number field. Let \(A\) be a finite discrete \(\Gal_k\)-module with \(mA = 0\), and \(T \subseteq \Omega_k\) a finite set of places. Then \(H^1(k, A; T)\) is finite.

Proof.

Enlarging \(T\) enlarges \(H^1(k, A; T)\), so we may assume that \(T\) satisfies the conditions on \(S\) in Situation 7.8.1. Let \(L\) be the fixed field of the kernels of the actions of \(\Gal_k\) on \(A\) and on \(\mu_m(\ksep)\), a finite Galois extension of \(k\), and \(T_L\) the set of places of \(L\) above \(T\). By Proposition 7.3.5, the kernel of \(\Res\colon H^1(k, A) \to H^1(L, A)\) is \(H^1(\Gal(L/k), A)\), which is finite. The restriction maps \(H^1(k, A; T)\) into \(H^1(L, A; T_L)\): for compatible embeddings, the inertia group of \(L\) at a place \(w\) above \(v\) is contained in \(I_v\). So it suffices to show that \(H^1(L, A; T_L)\) is finite.

Over \(L\), the action on \(A\) is trivial, so \(H^1(L, A; T_L)\) is the group of continuous homomorphisms \(\Gal_L \to A\) that are trivial on the inertia groups at the places outside \(T_L\) (Proposition 7.2.3). The finite abelian group \(A\) is killed by \(m\), so it embeds in \((\Z/m\Z)^r\) for some \(r\), and this embeds \(H^1(L, A; T_L)\) into \(H^1(L, \Z/m\Z; T_L)^r\). As \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq L\), the choice of a primitive \(m\)-th root of unity identifies \(\Z/m\Z\) with \(\mu_m(\ksep)\) as \(\Gal_L\)-modules, and \(H^1(L, \mu_m; T_L) \cong L(T_L, m)\) is finite by Proposition 7.8.3 and Theorem 7.8.4 over \(L\) [Sil09, Lemma X.4.3].

For \(A = E[m](\ksep)\) this says that \(H^1(k, E[m]; T)\) is finite. Chapter 8 shows that the image of the Kummer map \(E(k)/m \to H^1(k, E[m])\) lies in such a group, for \(T\) containing the places of bad reduction. The same theorem for \(\mu_m\), with the infinite group \(k^\times/k^{\times m}\) in place of \(E(k)/m\), only says that the elements of \(k^\times/k^{\times m}\) unramified outside \(T\) form a finite group.

Remark 7.8.7. One might hope that an element of \(k^\times\) that is an \(m\)-th power in every completion \(k_v\) is an \(m\)-th power in \(k\), that is, that \(H^1(k, \mu_m) \to \prod_v H^1(k_v, \mu_m)\) is injective. This is usually true, but not always. Let \(k = \Q(\sqrt 7)\) and \(m = 8\). Since

\begin{equation*} x^8 - 16 = (x^2 - 2)(x^2 + 2)(x^2 - 2x + 2)(x^2 + 2x + 2), \end{equation*}

and the last two factors have roots \(\pm 1 \pm i\), the number \(16\) is an \(8\)-th power in a field of characteristic \(0\) exactly when one of \(2\), \(-2\), \(-1\) is a square there. The field \(\Q(\sqrt 7)\) contains none of \(\sqrt 2\), \(\sqrt{-2}\), \(i\), so \(16\) is not an \(8\)-th power in \(k\). But it is one in every completion.

  • At the two real places, \(2 > 0\) is a square.
  • At a place over an odd prime \(\ell \neq 7\), the residue field contains \(\F_\ell\), in which \(\bigl(\frac{-1}{\ell}\bigr)\bigl(\frac{2}{\ell}\bigr) = \bigl(\frac{-2}{\ell}\bigr)\). So one of \(-1, 2, -2\) is a square there, and it lifts by Hensel's lemma.
  • At the place over \(7\), the residue field is \(\F_7\), where \(2 = 3^2\) is a square.
  • At the place over \(2\), \(-7 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\) is a square in \(\Q_2\), so \(\Q_2(\sqrt 7) = \Q_2(\sqrt{-1})\), and \(-1\) is a square.

Over \(\Q\) itself, \(16\) is not an \(8\)-th power in \(\Q_2\), since \(v_2(16) = 4\). This is Wang's counterexample to a theorem claimed by Grunwald. The Grunwald–Wang theorem says that such failures occur only for \(8 \mid m\) and in a precise special case.

Draft8 Oct 20267.9 Cup products and the Brauer group

We are in Situation 7.0.1. Silverman does not treat cup products. We define them in the degrees we need, prove the one property we use, and state the results on Brauer groups of local and global fields. Tate local duality and the Cassels–Tate pairing, which are built from them, are in Chapter 10.

Let \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) be discrete \(G\)-modules and \(A \times B \to C\), \((a, b) \mapsto a \cdot b\), a bilinear map with \(g(a \cdot b) = ga \cdot gb\).

Definition 7.9.1. The cup product \(H^i(G, A) \times H^j(G, B) \to H^{i + j}(G, C)\), for \(i, j \in \{0, 1\}\), is induced by the following products of cocycles:

\begin{equation*} a \cup b \coloneqq a \cdot b, \qquad (a \cup \eta)_g \coloneqq a \cdot \eta_g, \qquad (\xi \cup b)_g \coloneqq \xi_g \cdot b, \qquad (\xi \cup \eta)_{g, h} \coloneqq \xi_g \cdot g\eta_h, \end{equation*}

for \(a \in A^G\), \(b \in B^G\), and \(1\)-cocycles \(\xi\) and \(\eta\).

Proposition 7.9.2. The cup product is well defined and bilinear. In degrees \(1\) and \(1\), let \(\cup'\) be the cup product for the bilinear map \(B \times A \to C\), \((b, a) \mapsto a \cdot b\). Then \(\xi \cup \eta = -(\eta \cup' \xi)\) in \(H^2(G, C)\).

Proof.

We treat degrees \(1\) and \(1\). The others are similar and shorter. Let \(c \coloneqq \xi \cup \eta\). Using \(\xi_{g_1g_2} = \xi_{g_1} + g_1\xi_{g_2}\),

\begin{equation*} (d^2c)_{g_1, g_2, g_3} = \xi_{g_1} \cdot g_1\bigl(\eta_{g_2g_3} - \eta_{g_2} - g_2\eta_{g_3}\bigr) = 0. \end{equation*}

If \(\xi = d^0a\), then \(c = d^1(g \mapsto a \cdot \eta_g)\), and if \(\eta = d^0b\), then \(c = d^1(g \mapsto -\xi_g \cdot gb)\), as one checks with the cocycle conditions. So the class of \(c\) depends only on the classes of \(\xi\) and \(\eta\). Bilinearity is clear. Finally, let \(f_g \coloneqq -\xi_g \cdot \eta_g\). Expanding \(\xi_{gh} \cdot \eta_{gh} = (\xi_g + g\xi_h) \cdot (\eta_g + g\eta_h)\) gives

\begin{equation*} (d^1f)_{g, h} = \xi_g \cdot g\eta_h + g\xi_h \cdot \eta_g = (\xi \cup \eta)_{g, h} + (\eta \cup' \xi)_{g, h}. \end{equation*}

Cup products are also compatible with restriction and inflation, since these act on cochains by composition.

The multiplicative group. Suppose \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq k\), and fix a primitive \(m\)-th root of unity \(\zeta\). The pairing \(\mu_m(\ksep) \times \mu_m(\ksep) \to \mu_m(\ksep)\), \((\zeta^i, \zeta^j) \mapsto \zeta^{ij}\), is bilinear and \(\Gal_k\)-equivariant, as \(\Gal_k\) acts trivially. For \(a, b \in k^\times\), let

\begin{equation*} (a, b)_\zeta \coloneqq \delta(a) \cup \delta(b) \in H^2(k, \mu_m) \cong \Br(k)[m], \end{equation*}

by Corollary 7.4.6. The pairing is symmetric in its two arguments, so Proposition 7.9.2 gives \((b, a)_\zeta = -(a, b)_\zeta\). The class \((a, b)_\zeta\) is, up to sign, the class of the cyclic algebra built from \(a\), \(b\) and \(\zeta\) [Poo17, Section 1.5.7.4].

Example 7.9.3. Let \(m = 2\) and \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), so that \(\zeta = -1\) is canonical, and write \((a, b) \coloneqq (a, b)_{-1} \in \Br(k)[2]\). It is the class of the quaternion algebra \((a, b \mid k)\) of Definition 4.7.1: the sign does not matter in a group of exponent \(2\) [Poo17, Section 1.5.7.4 and Remark 1.5.24]. Since \(\Br(k)\) is the group of similarity classes of central simple algebras [Poo17, Theorem 1.5.12], \((a, b) = 0\) if and only if \((a, b \mid k)\) is split. By Proposition 4.7.5, this holds if and only if \(b\) is a norm from \(k(\sqrt a)\), that is, if and only if \(ax^2 + by^2 = z^2\) has a non-trivial solution in \(k\): if \(b = z^2 - ax^2\), take \(y = 1\), and conversely a solution with \(y \neq 0\) writes \(b\) as a norm, while one with \(y = 0\) makes \(a\) a square. For instance, the conic \(C_2\) of Example 2.3.6 is \(3X^2 + 3Y^2 = (3Z)^2\), and it has no rational point, so \((3, 3) \neq 0\) in \(\Br(\Q)\).

Over local and global fields the Brauer group is known completely. These theorems are by-products of class field theory, and we only state them.

Theorem 7.9.4.

  1. If \(k_v\) is a non-archimedean local field, there is an isomorphism \(\inv_v\colon \Br(k_v) \to \Q/\Z\). Also \(\Br(\R) \cong \frac12\Z/\Z\) and \(\Br(\C) = 0\).

  2. If \(k\) is a global field, the sequence

    \begin{equation*} 0 \to \Br(k) \to \bigoplus_{v \in \Omega_k} \Br(k_v) \xrightarrow{\sum_v \inv_v} \Q/\Z \to 0 \end{equation*}

    is exact, where \(\inv_v\) is the inclusion of part 1 for archimedean \(v\).

Proof.

See [Poo17, Theorems 1.5.34 and 1.5.36]. Part 2 is the theorem of Albert, Brauer, Hasse and Noether.

For a local field \(k_v\) containing \(\mu_m(k_v^{\operatorname{sep}})\), the Hilbert symbol of \(a, b \in k_v^\times\) is the root of unity \(\zeta^{m\inv_v((a, b)_\zeta)}\), also for archimedean \(v\). For \(m = 2\) it is \(1\) if \(ax^2 + by^2 = z^2\) has a non-trivial solution in \(k_v\), and \(-1\) otherwise. If \(\mu_m(\ksep) \subseteq k\) and \(a, b \in k^\times\), the class \((a, b) \in \Br(k)\) has \(\sum_v \inv_v = 0\) by part 2 of Theorem 7.9.4, so the Hilbert symbols satisfy the product formula \(\prod_v (a, b)_v = 1\). For \(k = \Q\) and \(m = 2\), the product formula is equivalent to quadratic reciprocity.

The elliptic curve. The Weil pairing \(e_m\colon E[m](\ksep) \times E[m](\ksep) \to \mu_m(\ksep)\) is bilinear and \(\Gal_k\)-equivariant (Proposition 4.6.3). In multiplicative notation for \(\mu_m(\ksep)\), it gives a cup product

\begin{equation*} H^1(k, E[m]) \times H^1(k, E[m]) \to H^2(k, \mu_m) \cong \Br(k)[m]. \end{equation*}

Proposition 7.9.5. The cup product with the Weil pairing on \(H^1(k, E[m])\) is symmetric.

Proof.

Since \(e_m\) is alternating, \(e_m(T, S) = e_m(S, T)^{-1}\) (part 2 of Proposition 4.6.3). So the cup product \(\cup'\) of Proposition 7.9.2, for the pairing \((T, S) \mapsto e_m(S, T)\), is the inverse of the cup product for \(e_m\) itself. Proposition 7.9.2 then gives \(\xi \cup \eta = (\eta \cup' \xi)^{-1} = \eta \cup \xi\).

Combined with \(\inv_v\) over a local field, this pairing is Tate local duality, and over a global field it underlies the Cassels–Tate pairing. Both are in Chapter 10.

Draft8 Oct 20267.10 Étale cohomology and the geometric point of view

We are in Situation 7.0.1. This section is a survey, without proofs, of the geometric meaning of the groups of this chapter. It follows [Poo17, Sections 5.12 and 6.2–6.6].

Sheaves on a point. An étale \(k\)-scheme is a finite disjoint union of spectra of finite separable extensions of \(k\). A sheaf on the small étale site of \(\Spec k\) is determined by its value on \(\Spec \ksep\), as a direct limit, and this is an equivalence between sheaves of sets and discrete \(\Gal_k\)-sets, and between abelian sheaves and discrete \(\Gal_k\)-modules. Under it, \(H^i_{\et}(\Spec k, F) \cong H^i(k, F(\ksep))\) [Poo17, Theorem 6.4.6]. So \(\Spec k\), which has a single point, behaves like a topological space whose only invariant is its fundamental group \(\Gal_k\): finite étale covers play the role of covering spaces, and Galois cohomology plays the role of the cohomology \(H^i(\pi_1(X), M)\) of a space \(X\) with contractible universal cover.

Definition 7.10.1. Let \(G\) be a smooth algebraic group over \(k\). A \(G\)-torsor is a \(k\)-variety \(X\) with a right action \(X \times G \to X\) such that \(X_{\ksep}\), with its \(G_{\ksep}\)-action, is isomorphic to \(G_{\ksep}\) with the action by right translation. It is trivial if it is isomorphic to \(G\) with this action [Poo17, Definition 5.12.3].

Proposition 7.10.2. Let \(G\) be a smooth algebraic group over \(k\).

  1. For a \(G\)-torsor \(X\) and \(x \in X(\ksep)\), there is a unique \(\xi_g \in G(\ksep)\) with \(gx = x\,\xi_g\), and \(\xi\) is a \(1\)-cocycle. Its class \([X] \in H^1(k, G)\) does not depend on \(x\), and \(X \mapsto [X]\) is a bijection from the isomorphism classes of \(G\)-torsors to \(H^1(k, G)\).
  2. A \(G\)-torsor \(X\) is trivial if and only if \(X(k)\) is non-empty, if and only if \([X]\) is the distinguished element.
Proof.

See [Poo17, Section 5.12.4, Remark 5.12.13 and Proposition 5.12.14]. The cocycle condition is the computation \(x\,\xi_{gh} = g(hx) = g(x\,\xi_h) = x\,\xi_g\,g\xi_h\).

Example 7.10.3.

  1. Every \(\Gm\)-torsor is trivial, by part 1 of Theorem 7.4.2. This is Hilbert 90 restated.
  2. For \(a \in k^\times\), the curve \(X_a\colon x^m = a\) in \(\Gm\), with \(\mu_m\) acting by multiplication, is a \(\mu_m\)-torsor. For a root \(\alpha \in X_a(\ksep)\), \(g(\alpha) = \alpha \cdot g(\alpha)/\alpha\), so \([X_a] = \delta(a)\). By Corollary 7.4.6, every \(\mu_m\)-torsor is some \(X_a\).
  3. For \(P \in E(k)\), the fiber \([m]^{-1}(P) \subseteq E\), with \(E[m]\) acting by translation, is an \(E[m]\)-torsor. For \(Q\) in it, \(gQ = Q + (gQ - Q)\), so its class is \(\delta(P)\): the Kummer map of Proposition 7.5.1 sends \(P\) to this torsor, which is trivial exactly when \(P \in mE(k)\).

Torsors under \(E\) itself are the genus one curves with Jacobian \(E\) [Poo17, Example 5.12.12], and they form the group \(H^1(k, E)\), the Weil–Châtelet group of Chapter 10.

Twists without cocycles. Let \(T\) be a \(G\)-torsor and \(X\) a quasi-projective \(k\)-variety with a left action of \(G\). The contracted product \(T \times^G X\), the quotient of \(T \times X\) by \((t, x) \mapsto (tg^{-1}, gx)\), is a twist of \(X\), and its class is the image of \([T]\) under \(H^1(k, G) \to H^1(k, \Aut(X_{\ksep}))\) [Poo17, Section 5.12.5.2]. Conversely, when the automorphisms of \(X\) form an algebraic group, a twist \(Y\) gives the torsor of isomorphisms from \(X\) to \(Y\). A cocycle is then nothing but a choice of a \(\ksep\)-point of this torsor, that is, of an isomorphism over \(\ksep\), as in Theorem 7.6.2.

Over a base. Over a locally noetherian scheme \(T\), torsors under an fppf group scheme \(G\) are trivialized fppf locally, and they are classified by \(H^1_{\fppf}(T, G)\) [Poo17, Theorem 6.5.10]. When \(G\) is smooth, this is \(H^1_{\et}(T, G)\) [Poo17, Remark 6.6.3]. Over \(\Spec k\), "étale locally" means "over a finite separable extension", and this recovers Proposition 7.10.2. Hilbert 90 becomes \(H^1_{\et}(T, \Gm) \cong \Pic T\) [Poo17, Proposition 6.6.1], and at \(T = \Spec k\) the Picard group is \(0\). The Brauer group of a scheme is \(\Br T \coloneqq H^2_{\et}(T, \Gm)\) [Poo17, Definition 6.6.4].

On \(T = \Spec \calO_S\) of Situation 7.8.1, where \(m\) is invertible, the Kummer sequence \(1 \to \mu_m \to \Gm \xrightarrow{m} \Gm \to 1\) is exact for the étale topology, and the argument of Corollary 7.4.6 gives

\begin{equation*} 1 \to \calO_S^\times/\calO_S^{\times m} \to H^1_{\et}(\Spec \calO_S, \mu_m) \to \Pic(\calO_S)[m] \to 0, \end{equation*}

with \(\Pic(\calO_S) = \Cl(\calO_S)\). This is the sequence of part 2 of Proposition 7.8.3: a class of \(H^1(k, \mu_m)\) is unramified outside \(S\) exactly when its torsor extends to a \(\mu_m\)-torsor over \(\Spec \calO_S\).

Characteristic \(p\). If \(\operatorname{char} k = p\), the group schemes \(\mu_p\) and \(E[p]\) are not étale, and Galois cohomology sees only their \(\ksep\)-points, such as \(\mu_p(\ksep) = \{1\}\). The Kummer sequence for \(m = p\) is exact for the fppf topology, and \(H^1_{\fppf}(\Spec k, \mu_p) \cong k^\times/k^{\times p}\) [Poo17, Remark 1.5.14]. This is the setting for \(p\)-descent in characteristic \(p\).
