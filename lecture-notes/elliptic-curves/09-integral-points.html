Situation 9.0.1.

  • Let \(k\) be a number field, with set of places \(\Places_k\) and completions \(k_v\). As in Situation 8.5.1, for \(v \in \Places_k\) the absolute value \(\lvert\ \rvert_v\) on \(k_v\) restricts to the usual absolute value on \(\R\) or to the \(p\)-adic absolute value with \(\lvert p \rvert_p = p^{-1}\), and \(n_v \coloneqq [k_v : \Q_v]\) is the local degree.
  • We write \(\kbar = \Qbar\), and for each \(v \in \Places_k\) we fix a \(k\)-embedding \(\kbar \to \overline{k_v}\), as in Situation 8.0.1. Through the embedding, it defines \(\lvert \alpha \rvert_v\) for \(\alpha \in \kbar\).
  • The height relative to \(k\) is \(H_k\), as in Definition 8.5.3, and \(H_k(x) \coloneqq H_k([x : 1])\) for \(x \in k\).

Draft10 Nov 20269.1 Diophantine approximation

Diophantine approximation asks how well an irrational number can be approximated by rational numbers. For every rational number \(p/q\) the distance \(\lvert p/q - \sqrt 2 \rvert\) is positive, and since \(\Q\) is dense in \(\R\), a good choice of \(p/q\) makes it as small as we like. The question is how small it can be made without making \(p\) and \(q\) large. Two elementary results set the scale. Throughout, a rational number \(p/q\) is written with \(p, q \in \Z\) and \(q \geq 1\).

Proposition 9.1.1 (Dirichlet). Let \(\alpha \in \R \setminus \Q\). There are infinitely many rational numbers \(p/q\), in lowest terms, with

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ip-dirichlet} \Bigl\lvert \frac{p}{q} - \alpha \Bigr\rvert < \frac{1}{q^2}. \tag{9.1.1} \end{equation}
Proof.

Let \(N \geq 1\) be an integer. For \(t \in \R\), let \(\{t\} \coloneqq t - \lfloor t \rfloor \in [0, 1)\) be its fractional part. As \(\alpha\) is irrational, the \(N + 1\) numbers \(\{q\alpha\}\) with \(0 \leq q \leq N\) are distinct. The \(N\) intervals \([j/N, (j + 1)/N)\), for \(0 \leq j < N\), cover \([0, 1)\), so by the pigeonhole principle one of them contains \(\{q_1\alpha\}\) and \(\{q_2\alpha\}\) for some \(0 \leq q_1 < q_2 \leq N\). Let \(q \coloneqq q_2 - q_1\) and \(p \coloneqq \lfloor q_2\alpha \rfloor - \lfloor q_1\alpha \rfloor\). Then \(1 \leq q \leq N\) and \(\lvert q\alpha - p \rvert = \lvert \{q_2\alpha\} - \{q_1\alpha\} \rvert < 1/N\), so \[ \Bigl\lvert \frac{p}{q} - \alpha \Bigr\rvert < \frac{1}{qN} \leq \frac{1}{q^2}. \] Writing \(p/q = p'/q'\) in lowest terms, \(q' \leq q\), so \(p'/q'\) satisfies \eqref{eq:ip-dirichlet} too.

So there is at least one solution. Suppose there were only finitely many, \(p_1/q_1, \dots, p_r/q_r\). As \(\alpha\) is irrational, \(\delta \coloneqq \min_i \lvert p_i/q_i - \alpha \rvert > 0\). The construction with \(N > 1/\delta\) gives a rational number \(p/q\) satisfying \eqref{eq:ip-dirichlet} with \(\lvert p/q - \alpha \rvert < 1/(qN) \leq 1/N < \delta\), so it is none of the \(p_i/q_i\), a contradiction [Sil09, Proposition IX.1.2].

Proposition 9.1.1 has a picture. Around each rational number \(p/q\) in lowest terms, draw the disk in \(\C\) of radius \(1/q^2\). An irrational real number lies in infinitely many of these disks. A rational number lies in only finitely many. Indeed, let \(a/b\) be in lowest terms. For \(p/q \neq a/b\), \[ \Bigl\lvert \frac{p}{q} - \frac{a}{b} \Bigr\rvert = \frac{\lvert pb - aq \rvert}{bq} \geq \frac{1}{bq}, \] and this is at least \(1/q^2\) as soon as \(q \geq b\). So \(a/b\) lies in its own disk and in disks with \(q < b\) only. In Figure 9.1.1 the disks are translucent, so the shade darkens where many of them overlap. The cusps that point at the integers are made of the disks around \(n \pm 1/q\): they come within \(1/q - 1/q^2\) of \(n\), and never reach it.

Remark 9.1.2. Hurwitz showed that \(1/q^2\) in \eqref{eq:ip-dirichlet} can be replaced by \(1/(\sqrt 5\, q^2)\), and that \(\sqrt 5\) cannot be replaced by any larger constant [Sil09, Remark IX.1.2.1].

Dirichlet's theorem says how well every irrational number can be approximated. For algebraic numbers there is a bound in the other direction.

Proposition 9.1.3 (Liouville). Let \(\alpha \in \C\) be algebraic over \(\Q\) of degree \(d \geq 2\). There is a constant \(c > 0\), depending on \(\alpha\), such that every rational number \(p/q\) satisfies

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ip-liouville} \Bigl\lvert \frac{p}{q} - \alpha \Bigr\rvert \geq \frac{c}{q^d}. \tag{9.1.2} \end{equation}
Proof.

If \(\alpha \notin \R\), then \(\lvert p/q - \alpha \rvert \geq \lvert \operatorname{Im} \alpha \rvert \geq \lvert \operatorname{Im} \alpha \rvert/q^d\). Suppose that \(\alpha \in \R\). Let \(f = a_0T^d + a_1T^{d - 1} + \dots + a_d \in \Z[T]\) be a minimal polynomial of \(\alpha\), and let \[ c_1 \coloneqq \sup\{\lvert f'(t) \rvert : \alpha - 1 \leq t \leq \alpha + 1\}. \] Here \(c_1 > 0\), since \(f'\) is a nonzero polynomial. If \(\lvert p/q - \alpha \rvert \geq 1\), then \eqref{eq:ip-liouville} holds with \(c = 1\). Otherwise, by the mean value theorem, \[ \Bigl\lvert f\Bigl(\frac{p}{q}\Bigr) \Bigr\rvert = \Bigl\lvert f\Bigl(\frac{p}{q}\Bigr) - f(\alpha) \Bigr\rvert \leq c_1 \Bigl\lvert \frac{p}{q} - \alpha \Bigr\rvert. \] The number \(q^d f(p/q) = a_0p^d + a_1p^{d - 1}q + \dots + a_dq^d\) is an integer. It is nonzero, since \(f\) is irreducible of degree \(d \geq 2\) and so has no rational root. So \(\lvert f(p/q) \rvert \geq q^{-d}\), and \eqref{eq:ip-liouville} holds with \(c = 1/c_1\). So for a real \(\alpha\), \(c \coloneqq \min(1, 1/c_1)\) works [Sil09, Proposition IX.1.3].

Remark 9.1.4. The constant \(c\) in Proposition 9.1.3 is explicit in terms of \(\alpha\). Liouville used his theorem to write down the first transcendental numbers. Let \(\alpha \coloneqq \sum_{n \geq 1} 10^{-n!}\), and for \(N \geq 1\) let \(p_N/q_N\) be the partial sum up to \(n = N\), with \(q_N \coloneqq 10^{N!}\). Then \[ 0 < \alpha - \frac{p_N}{q_N} < \frac{2}{10^{(N + 1)!}} = \frac{2}{q_N^{N + 1}}. \] For every \(d \geq 2\) and \(c > 0\), this contradicts \eqref{eq:ip-liouville} once \(N\) is large. The decimal expansion of \(\alpha\) is not eventually periodic, so \(\alpha\) is not rational either. So \(\alpha\) is transcendental [Sil09, Remark IX.1.3.1 and Exercise 9.2].

9.1.1 The approximation exponent

Dirichlet's theorem approximates every real number to within \(1/q^2\), and Liouville's theorem says that an algebraic number of degree \(d\) cannot be approximated to within \(c/q^d\). For quadratic irrationals the two exponents agree. For \(d \geq 3\) we may ask for the best exponent. There is no reason to restrict the approximations to \(\Q\), or to the usual absolute value, so we let them range over a number field \(k\) and measure the distance at a place of \(k\). The size of \(q\) becomes the height \(H_k(x)\).

Definition 9.1.5. Let \(v \in \Places_k\), and let \(\tau\colon \Z_{\geq 1} \to \R_{> 0}\) be a function. We say that \(k\) has approximation exponent \(\tau\) at \(v\) if the following holds. For every \(\alpha \in \kbar\), with \(d \coloneqq [k(\alpha) : k]\), and every real number \(C > 0\), the set of \(x \in k\) with

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ip-approximation} \lvert x - \alpha \rvert_v < C\, H_k(x)^{-\tau(d)} \tag{9.1.3} \end{equation}

is finite.

Remark 9.1.6.

  1. Equivalently, for every \(\alpha \in \kbar\) and every \(C > 0\), all but finitely many \(x \in k\) satisfy \[ \lvert x - \alpha \rvert_v \geq C\, H_k(x)^{-\tau(d)}. \] This is a lower bound for the distance from \(\alpha\) to the elements of \(k\), like Liouville's \eqref{eq:ip-liouville}: it says that \(\alpha\) cannot be approximated too well. A smaller \(\tau\) is a stronger statement. If \(\tau \leq \tau'\) and \(k\) has approximation exponent \(\tau\) at \(v\), then it also has approximation exponent \(\tau'\) at \(v\), since \(H_k(x) \geq 1\) gives \(H_k(x)^{-\tau'(d)} \leq H_k(x)^{-\tau(d)}\).
  2. The definition does not depend on the embedding \(\kbar \to \overline{k_v}\) of Situation 9.0.1. Two \(k\)-embeddings \(\iota, \iota'\) have the same image, the algebraic closure of \(k\) in \(\overline{k_v}\), so \(\iota' = \iota \circ \sigma\) for some \(\sigma \in \Gal_k\). Then \(\lvert \iota'(x - \alpha) \rvert = \lvert \iota(x - \sigma(\alpha)) \rvert\) for \(x \in k\), and \(\sigma(\alpha)\) has the same degree as \(\alpha\). Since \(\alpha\) ranges over all of \(\kbar\), the definition covers every extension of \(\lvert\ \rvert_v\) to \(k(\alpha)\). This is how [Sil09, Section IX.1] phrases it.
  3. For \(k = \Q\) and \(x = p/q\) in lowest terms, \([x : 1] = [p : q]\) has \(\lvert [p : q] \rvert_\ell = 1\) at every prime \(\ell\), so \(H_\Q(x) = \max(\lvert p \rvert, q)\).

Let \(\infty\) be the archimedean place of \(\Q\). In these terms, Liouville's theorem gives an approximation exponent of \(\Q\) at \(\infty\).

Corollary 9.1.7. For every \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(\Q\) has approximation exponent \(\tau(d) = d + \varepsilon\) at \(\infty\).

Proof.

Let \(\alpha \in \Qbar\), with \(d \coloneqq [\Q(\alpha) : \Q]\), and let \(C > 0\). Let \(x = p/q\) in lowest terms satisfy \eqref{eq:ip-approximation}. By part 3 of Remark 9.1.6, \(H_\Q(x) = \max(\lvert p \rvert, q) \geq q\), so \[ \lvert x - \alpha \rvert < C\, H_\Q(x)^{-d - \varepsilon} \leq \frac{C}{q^{d + \varepsilon}}. \] If \(d \geq 2\), let \(c\) be the constant of Proposition 9.1.3. Then \(c/q^d < C/q^{d + \varepsilon}\), that is, \(q^\varepsilon < C/c\). If \(d = 1\), then \(\alpha = a/b\) in lowest terms, and for \(x \neq \alpha\), \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert \geq 1/(bq)\) as in the discussion of Figure 9.1.1, so \(q^\varepsilon < bC\). In both cases \(q\) is bounded, apart from \(x = \alpha\) when \(d = 1\). Then \(\lvert p \rvert \leq q(\lvert \alpha \rvert + C)\) is bounded too, so there are finitely many such \(x\).

Remark 9.1.8. The \(\varepsilon\) in Corollary 9.1.7 cannot be removed for \(d = 2\). More generally, Dirichlet's theorem shows that \(\Q\) has no approximation exponent \(\tau\) at \(\infty\) with \(\tau(d) \leq 2\) for some \(d \geq 2\). Let \(\alpha \in \R\) be algebraic of degree \(d\), for example \(\alpha = \sqrt[d]{2}\). By Proposition 9.1.1 there are infinitely many \(x = p/q\) in lowest terms with \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert < 1/q^2\). For them \(\lvert p \rvert < q(\lvert \alpha \rvert + 1)\), so \(H_\Q(x) < (\lvert \alpha \rvert + 1)q\), and \[ \lvert x - \alpha \rvert < \frac{1}{q^2} < \frac{(\lvert \alpha \rvert + 1)^2}{H_\Q(x)^2} \leq (\lvert \alpha \rvert + 1)^2 H_\Q(x)^{-\tau(d)}. \] So \eqref{eq:ip-approximation} with \(C = (\lvert \alpha \rvert + 1)^2\) has infinitely many solutions.

Remark 9.1.9. Corollary 9.1.7 also holds at every prime \(p\) of \(\Q\), with the product formula in place of the mean value theorem. Let \(\alpha \in \Qbar\) have degree \(d\), and let \(f = a_0T^d + \dots + a_d \in \Z[T]\) be a minimal polynomial of \(\alpha\) with coprime coefficients. Let \(x = a/b \in \Q\) in lowest terms, and let \(N \coloneqq b^d f(x) = \sum_i a_i a^{d - i} b^i\). This is an integer, and it is nonzero unless \(x = \alpha\), since \(f\) is irreducible. Let \(x \neq \alpha\), and let \(A \coloneqq \sum_i \lvert a_i \rvert\). Then \(\lvert N \rvert \leq A\, H_\Q(x)^d\). Since \(\lvert N \rvert_\ell \leq 1\) for every prime \(\ell\), the product formula gives \[ \lvert N \rvert_p \geq \frac{1}{\lvert N \rvert} \geq \frac{1}{A\, H_\Q(x)^d}. \] Now suppose that \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert_p \leq 1\), so that \(\lvert x \rvert_p \leq B \coloneqq \max(1, \lvert \alpha \rvert_p)\). Write \(f(X) - f(Y) = (X - Y)g(X, Y)\) with \(g \in \Z[X, Y]\) of degree \(d - 1\). As \(\lvert\ \rvert_p\) is non-archimedean, \(\lvert g(x, \alpha) \rvert_p \leq B^{d - 1}\), and \[ \lvert N \rvert_p \leq \lvert f(x) \rvert_p = \lvert f(x) - f(\alpha) \rvert_p \leq B^{d - 1} \lvert x - \alpha \rvert_p. \] So \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert_p \geq c\, H_\Q(x)^{-d}\) with \(c \coloneqq 1/(AB^{d - 1})\). Let \(C > 0\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\), and let \(x\) satisfy \eqref{eq:ip-approximation} with \(\tau(d) = d + \varepsilon\). If \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert_p > 1\), then \(H_\Q(x)^{d + \varepsilon} < C\). Otherwise \(H_\Q(x)^\varepsilon < C/c\). In both cases the height of \(x\) is bounded, and by part 3 of Remark 9.1.6 there are finitely many such \(x\).

Liouville's exponent was improved several times [Sil09, Section IX.1].

Year Approximation exponent Due to
1851 \(\tau(d) = d + \varepsilon\) Liouville
1909 \(\tau(d) = d/2 + 1 + \varepsilon\) Thue
1921 \(\tau(d) = 2\sqrt d + \varepsilon\) Siegel
1947 \(\tau(d) = \sqrt{2d} + \varepsilon\) Gelfond, Dyson
1955 \(\tau(d) = 2 + \varepsilon\) Roth

By Remark 9.1.8, Roth's exponent is essentially the best possible. It is conjectured that \(\varepsilon\) can be replaced by some \(\varepsilon(d)\) with \(\varepsilon(d) \to 0\) as \(d \to \infty\). Mahler handled several absolute values at once, and Schmidt the simultaneous approximation of several irrational numbers [Sil09, Section IX.1].

Theorem 9.1.10 (Roth). Let \(v \in \Places_k\) and \(\varepsilon > 0\). Then \(k\) has approximation exponent \(\tau(d) = 2 + \varepsilon\) at \(v\).

Proof.

Not proved in these notes. The ideas are elementary, but the details are long. See [Sil09, Theorem IX.1.4 and Section IX.8] for a sketch, and [HS00, Part D] for a proof.

Theorems on Diophantine approximation give finiteness results for Diophantine equations.

Example 9.1.11. Let \(a \in \Z\). We show that the equation \(x^3 - 2y^3 = a\) has finitely many solutions \((x, y) \in \Z^2\). If \(y = 0\), then \(x^3 = a\), which has at most one solution. Let \(\beta \coloneqq \sqrt[3]{2} \in \R\), and let \(\zeta \in \C\) be a primitive cube root of \(1\). For a solution with \(y \neq 0\), \[ \Bigl(\frac{x}{y} - \beta\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{x}{y} - \zeta\beta\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{x}{y} - \zeta^2\beta\Bigr) = \frac{a}{y^3}. \] For \(t \in \R\), \(\lvert t - \zeta\beta \rvert\) and \(\lvert t - \zeta^2\beta \rvert\) are at least \(\lvert \operatorname{Im}(\zeta\beta) \rvert = (\sqrt 3/2)\beta\). So \[ \Bigl\lvert \frac{x}{y} - \beta \Bigr\rvert \leq \frac{c_a}{\lvert y \rvert^3}, \qquad c_a \coloneqq \frac{4\lvert a \rvert}{3\beta^2}. \] If \(\lvert x/y - \beta \rvert \geq 1\), then \(\lvert y \rvert^3 \leq c_a\), and each of these finitely many \(y\) has at most one \(x\). Otherwise \(\lvert x \rvert < (\beta + 1)\lvert y \rvert\). Since \(x/y\) need not be in lowest terms, part 3 of Remark 9.1.6 gives only \(H_\Q(x/y) \leq \max(\lvert x \rvert, \lvert y \rvert) < (\beta + 1)\lvert y \rvert\), but this suffices: \[ \Bigl\lvert \frac{x}{y} - \beta \Bigr\rvert \leq c_a(\beta + 1)^3 H_\Q(x/y)^{-3} < C\, H_\Q(x/y)^{-5/2}, \qquad C \coloneqq c_a(\beta + 1)^3 + 1. \] As \(\beta\) has degree \(3\) over \(\Q\), Theorem 9.1.10 with \(\varepsilon = 1/2\) leaves finitely many values for \(r \coloneqq x/y\). Thue's exponent \(\tau(3) = 5/2 + \varepsilon\) would do as well. Each value of \(r\) comes from at most one solution: \(y^3(r^3 - 2) = a\) with \(r^3 \neq 2\) determines \(y\), and then \(x = ry\). This is [Sil09, Example IX.1.5], with the details filled in.

Remark 9.1.12. Theorem 9.1.10 says that a set is finite, but its proof gives no procedure to find the elements of the set. It is ineffective. So Example 9.1.11 gives no bound on \(\lvert x \rvert\) and \(\lvert y \rvert\) in terms of \(a\), and the finiteness results that rely on Roth's theorem are ineffective too. Liouville's theorem, with its explicit constant, is effective, but too weak for these applications. Effective bounds come from a different method, lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms [Sil09, Remark IX.1.6 and Section IX.5].

Draft10 Nov 20269.2 Distance functions and local heights

A Diophantine inequality \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert_v < C\, H_k(x)^{-\tau(d)}\) has two parts. The height \(H_k(x)\) measures the arithmetic size of \(x\), as in Chapter 8. The quantity \(\lvert x - \alpha \rvert_v\) measures how close \(x\) is to \(\alpha\) in the \(v\)-adic topology. This section defines a \(v\)-adic distance on a curve, following [Sil09, Section IX.2], compares it with an honest metric, and restates Theorem 9.1.10 in its terms. The distance turns out to be one place's share of a height: summed over all places, the distances to the poles of a function give its height. This is the theory of local heights.

Situation 9.2.1. We keep Situation 9.0.1.

  • Fix a place \(v \in \Places_k\) and a nice curve \(C\) over \(k\). Through \(k \subseteq k_v\), \(C(k) \subseteq C(k_v)\), and through the embedding \(\kbar \to \overline{k_v}\), \(C(\kbar) \subseteq C(\overline{k_v})\).
  • The set \(C(k_v)\) carries its \(v\)-adic topology (Lemma 9.2.4). The notation \(P \to_v Q\) appears only below a limit, as in \(\lim_{P \to_v Q}\) or \(\liminf_{P \in C(k),\ P \to_v Q}\). There it means that \(P \neq Q\) ranges over \(C(k_v)\), or over the subset named below the limit, and tends to \(Q\) in this topology. In the text we say that \(P\) is \(v\)-adically close to \(Q\).
  • For functions \(f, g\) on a set \(X\) with values in \([0, \infty)\), we write \(f \asymp g\) if there is a constant \(c \geq 1\) with \(c^{-1} g \leq f \leq c\, g\) on \(X\).

9.2.1 The chordal distance

The honest metric comes from projective space. For a point \(P = [x_0 : \dots : x_N]\) of \(\PP^N(\overline{k_v})\) we write \(\lVert P \rVert_v\) for the norm of its coordinates: \(\max_i \lvert x_i \rvert_v\) if \(v\) is non-archimedean, written \(\lvert P \rvert_v\) in Situation 8.5.1, and \(\bigl(\sum_i \lvert x_i \rvert_v^2\bigr)^{1/2}\) if \(v\) is archimedean. For a second point \(P' = [x'_0 : \dots : x'_N]\) of \(\PP^N(\overline{k_v})\), we write \(\lVert P \wedge P' \rVert_v\) for \(\max_{i < j} \lvert x_ix'_j - x_jx'_i \rvert_v\) if \(v\) is non-archimedean, and for \(\bigl(\sum_{i < j} \lvert x_ix'_j - x_jx'_i \rvert_v^2\bigr)^{1/2}\) if \(v\) is archimedean. Both depend on the choice of coordinates.

Definition 9.2.2. The chordal distance between \(P_1, P_2 \in \PP^N(\overline{k_v})\) is \[ \delta_v(P_1, P_2) \coloneqq \frac{\lVert P_1 \wedge P_2 \rVert_v}{\lVert P_1 \rVert_v \lVert P_2 \rVert_v}. \]

Rescaling the coordinates of \(P_1\) by \(\lambda\) multiplies the numerator and the denominator by \(\lvert \lambda \rvert_v\), so \(\delta_v\) does not depend on the coordinates.

Proposition 9.2.3. The chordal distance \(\delta_v\) is a metric on \(\PP^N(\overline{k_v})\), with values in \([0, 1]\). If \(v\) is non-archimedean, it is an ultrametric: \(\delta_v(P_1, P_3) \leq \max(\delta_v(P_1, P_2), \delta_v(P_2, P_3))\).

Proof.

The minors \(x_ix'_j - x_jx'_i\) all vanish if and only if the coordinate vectors are proportional, so \(\delta_v(P_1, P_2) = 0\) if and only if \(P_1 = P_2\). Symmetry is clear.

Let \(v\) be non-archimedean. Choose coordinates \(x, y, w\) of \(P_1, P_2, P_3\) of norm \(1\), by dividing by a coordinate of largest absolute value. Then each minor has absolute value at most \(1\), so \(\delta_v \leq 1\). Choose \(l\) with \(\lvert y_l \rvert_v = 1\). Expanding the right-hand side shows that \[ y_l(x_iw_j - x_jw_i) = (x_iy_l - x_ly_i)w_j - (x_jy_l - x_ly_j)w_i + x_l(y_iw_j - y_jw_i). \] Since \(\lvert w_i \rvert_v, \lvert w_j \rvert_v, \lvert x_l \rvert_v \leq 1\), this gives \(\lvert x_iw_j - x_jw_i \rvert_v \leq \max(\lVert P_1 \wedge P_2 \rVert_v, \lVert P_2 \wedge P_3 \rVert_v)\), which is the ultrametric inequality.

Let \(v\) be archimedean, so that \(\overline{k_v} = \C\). Choose coordinates \(p, q \in \C^{N+1}\) of \(P_1, P_2\) of norm \(1\), and let \(\langle p, q \rangle \coloneqq \sum_i p_i \bar q_i\). Lagrange's identity \(\lVert p \rVert^2 \lVert q \rVert^2 - \lvert \langle p, q \rangle \rvert^2 = \sum_{i < j} \lvert p_iq_j - p_jq_i \rvert^2\) gives \(\delta_v(P_1, P_2)^2 = 1 - \lvert \langle p, q \rangle \rvert^2 \leq 1\). The Hermitian matrix \(\pi_{P_1} \coloneqq p\bar p^{\mathsf T}\), the orthogonal projection onto the line \(P_1\), depends only on \(P_1\), and for the Hilbert–Schmidt norm \(\lVert A \rVert^2 \coloneqq \operatorname{tr}(A\bar A^{\mathsf T})\), \[ \lVert \pi_{P_1} - \pi_{P_2} \rVert^2 = 2 - 2\lvert \langle p, q \rangle \rvert^2 = 2\,\delta_v(P_1, P_2)^2. \] So \(\sqrt 2\, \delta_v\) is the distance of a norm, restricted to the matrices \(\pi_P\), and satisfies the triangle inequality.

For \(N = 1\) and \(v\) archimedean, \(\delta_v\) is the length of the chord between the images of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) on the Riemann sphere of diameter \(1\), whence the name.

Lemma 9.2.4.

  1. Let \(Q = [1 : a_1 : \dots : a_N] \in \PP^N(k_v)\). There is a constant \(c \geq 1\) such that every \(P = [1 : x_1 : \dots : x_N] \in \PP^N(k_v)\) with \(\lvert x_j - a_j \rvert_v \leq 1\) for all \(j\) satisfies

    \begin{equation} \label{eq:ip-chart} c^{-1} \max_j \lvert x_j - a_j \rvert_v \leq \delta_v(P, Q) \leq c \max_j \lvert x_j - a_j \rvert_v. \tag{9.2.1} \end{equation}

    So \(\delta_v\) induces the topology of \(k_v^N\) on each standard affine chart, and \(\PP^N(k_v)\) is compact for it.

  2. Let \(C \subseteq \PP^N\) be a closed embedding. Then \(C(k_v)\) is a compact subset of \(\PP^N(k_v)\). Its topology does not depend on the embedding, and every \(f \in k_v(C)\) defines a continuous map \(C(k_v) \to \PP^1(k_v)\).
  3. Let \(Q \in C(k_v)\), and let \(u \in k_v(C)\) be a uniformizer at \(Q\). Then \(u\) maps an open neighborhood of \(Q\) in \(C(k_v)\) homeomorphically onto an open neighborhood of \(0\) in \(k_v\). In particular, \(Q\) is not an isolated point of \(C(k_v)\).
  4. The set \(C(k_v)\) is closed in \(C(\overline{k_v})\) for \(\delta_v\).
Proof.

For part 1, the set of coordinate vectors of norm \(1\) is closed and bounded in \(k_v^{N+1}\), hence compact, since \(k_v\) is locally compact. It maps onto \(\PP^N(k_v)\), continuously since \(\lVert x \wedge y \rVert_v = \lVert x \wedge (y - x) \rVert_v \leq \lVert y - x \rVert_v\) for vectors of norm \(1\). So \(\PP^N(k_v)\) is compact. For \(P\) as in the statement, the norms \(\lVert P \rVert_v\) lie between \(1\) and a constant depending on \(Q\). The minors of the pair \((P, Q)\) are \(a_j - x_j\), and \(x_ia_j - x_ja_i = a_j(x_i - a_i) - a_i(x_j - a_j)\). So \(\lVert P \wedge Q \rVert_v\) is at least \(\max_j \lvert x_j - a_j \rvert_v\) and at most a constant times it, which is \eqref{eq:ip-chart}. Let \(U\) be the set of \(P = [1 : x_1 : \dots : x_N]\) with \(\lvert x_j - a_j \rvert_v < 1\) for all \(j\). Its complement is the image of the closed set of coordinate vectors \(x\) of norm \(1\) with \(\lvert x_j - a_jx_0 \rvert_v \geq \lvert x_0 \rvert_v\) for some \(j\). So it is compact, it does not contain \(Q\), and \(\delta_v(\cdot, Q)\) is bounded below on it by a positive constant. Hence small \(\delta_v\)-balls around \(Q\) lie in \(U\), where \eqref{eq:ip-chart} compares them with the polydiscs of \(k_v^N\) around \((a_1, \dots, a_N)\). After permuting the coordinates, this holds for every \(Q\).

So \(\delta_v\) defines the analytic topology of [Poo17, Section 2.6.2] on \(\PP^N(k_v)\). Part 2 follows: \(C(k_v)\) is closed, as the common zero set of polynomials, the analytic topology of \(C(k_v)\) does not depend on the embedding, and morphisms are continuous for it [Poo17, Section 2.6.2]. By Proposition 3.2.1, \(f\) is a morphism \(C_{k_v} \to \PP^1\).

For part 3, the uniformizer \(u\) is regular on an open neighborhood \(U\) of \(Q\), and \(u\colon U \to \Aff^1\) is étale at \(Q\), since \(\ord_Q(u) = 1\) and the residue field of \(Q\) is \(k_v\). Shrinking \(U\), it is étale on \(U\), and by [Poo17, Proposition 3.5.73] it is a local homeomorphism \(U(k_v) \to k_v\). As \(k_v\) has no isolated points, neither does \(C(k_v)\).

For part 4, \(\PP^N(k_v)\) is compact, hence closed in the metric space \(\PP^N(\overline{k_v})\), and \(C(k_v) = C(\overline{k_v}) \cap \PP^N(k_v)\).

9.2.2 The \(v\)-adic distance on a curve

The chordal distance depends on an embedding into projective space. Silverman's distance uses a single function instead. By Riemann–Roch, a nice curve \(C\) of genus \(g\) over \(k_v\) has, for every \(Q \in C(k_v)\) and \(e \geq \max(2g, 1)\), a function \(f \in k_v(C)\) with a pole of order \(e\) at \(Q\) and no other poles. Indeed, part 2 of Corollary 3.5.5 gives \(\ell(eQ) - \ell((e - 1)Q) = 1\). Then \(t_Q \coloneqq 1/f\) has a zero of order \(e\) at \(Q\) and no other zeros in \(C(\overline{k_v})\) [Sil09, footnote in Section IX.2].

Definition 9.2.5. Let \(C\) be a nice curve over \(k_v\), let \(Q \in C(k_v)\), and let \(t_Q \in k_v(C)\) have a zero of order \(e \geq 1\) at \(Q\) and no other zeros in \(C(\overline{k_v})\). The \(v\)-adic distance from \(P \in C(k_v)\) to \(Q\) is \[ d_v(P, Q) \coloneqq \min\bigl(\lvert t_Q(P) \rvert_v^{1/e}, 1\bigr), \] with \(d_v(P, Q) \coloneqq 1\) if \(P\) is a pole of \(t_Q\).

For a nice curve over \(k\), we apply the definition to \(C_{k_v}\). By part 2 of Lemma 9.2.4, \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) is continuous on \(C(k_v)\), with values in \([0, 1]\), and it vanishes only at \(Q\). It depends on the choice of \(t_Q\), but only up to bounded factors.

Lemma 9.2.6. Let \(d_v\) and \(d'_v\) be defined from two choices \(t_Q\) and \(t'_Q\), with orders \(e\) and \(e'\) at \(Q\). Then \(d_v(\cdot, Q) \asymp d'_v(\cdot, Q)\) on \(C(k_v)\).

Proof.

The function \(g \coloneqq t'^{\,e}_Q/t_Q^{e'}\) has neither a zero nor a pole at \(Q\). So there is an open neighborhood \(U\) of \(Q\) on which \(\lvert g \rvert_v\) is bounded above and below by positive constants, and \(\lvert t_Q \rvert_v, \lvert t'_Q \rvert_v < 1\). There \(d'_v(P, Q)/d_v(P, Q) = \lvert g(P) \rvert_v^{1/(ee')}\) for \(P \neq Q\). On the compact set \(C(k_v) \setminus U\), both functions are continuous with values in \((0, 1]\), so both are bounded below by a positive constant.

The class of \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) under \(\asymp\) contains an honest metric.

Theorem 9.2.7. Let \(C \subseteq \PP^N\) be a closed embedding of a nice curve over \(k_v\), and let \(Q \in C(k_v)\). Then \(d_v(\cdot, Q) \asymp \delta_v(\cdot, Q)\) on \(C(k_v)\).

Proof.

Permuting the coordinates, write \(Q = [1 : a_1 : \dots : a_N]\) with \(a_j \in k_v\), and let \(x_j \coloneqq X_j/X_0 \in k_v(C)\). The local ring \(\calO_{C,Q}\) is a quotient of the local ring of \(\PP^N_{k_v}\) at \(Q\), whose maximal ideal is generated by the \(x_j - a_j\). So the \(x_j - a_j\) generate the maximal ideal of the discrete valuation ring \(\calO_{C,Q}\), and one of them, say \(u \coloneqq x_1 - a_1\), is a uniformizer. Then \(x_j - a_j = u g_j\) with \(g_j\) regular at \(Q\) and \(g_1 = 1\), and \(t_Q = u^e h\) with \(h\) regular and non-zero at \(Q\). On a small open neighborhood of \(Q\), the \(\lvert g_j \rvert_v\) are bounded, \(\lvert h \rvert_v\) is bounded above and below by positive constants, and \eqref{eq:ip-chart} applies. There \[ \delta_v(P, Q) \asymp \max_j \lvert u(P) g_j(P) \rvert_v \asymp \lvert u(P) \rvert_v \asymp \lvert t_Q(P) \rvert_v^{1/e} = d_v(P, Q). \] Away from this neighborhood, both functions are continuous and positive on a compact set, as in the proof of Lemma 9.2.6.

Theorem 9.2.7 makes [Sil09, Remark IX.2.2.1] precise. That remark defines a distance from several functions with no other common zero, such as the \(x_j - a_j\), and [Sil09, Exercise 9.15] compares it with \(d_v\). The constant in \(\asymp\) depends on \(Q\). A distance with constants uniform in both variables is a function on \(C \times C\), and \(-\log\) of it is a local height for the diagonal of \(C \times C\) [Sil87].

Remark 9.2.8. The \(v\)-adic distance is not a metric, and is not meant to be one. It is defined one \(Q\) at a time, after choosing \(t_Q\), and \(c\, t_Q\) is another choice for every \(c \in k_v^\times\). So it makes no sense to ask whether \(d_v(P, Q) = d_v(Q, P)\), or for the triangle inequality, until the choices are coordinated. Even natural choices fail. On \(\PP^1\), let \(a \in k_v\) with \(\lvert a \rvert_v > 1\), and take \(t_a \coloneqq x - a\) and \(t_\infty \coloneqq 1/x\). The rational point \(\infty\) is a pole of \(t_a\), so \(d_v(\infty, a) = 1\), while \(d_v(a, \infty) = \min(\lvert 1/a \rvert_v, 1) = 1/\lvert a \rvert_v\).

What is well defined is the class of \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) under \(\asymp\), by Lemma 9.2.6, and by Theorem 9.2.7 it contains the honest metric \(\delta_v(\cdot, Q)\). The results of this chapter depend only on this class. They concern \(\log d_v(P, Q)\) as \(P\) tends to \(Q\), and replacing \(d_v\) by a function \(g\) with \(g \asymp d_v\) changes \(\log d_v\) by a bounded amount, which is negligible against \(\log d_v \to -\infty\).

The two examples below take \(Q\) at infinity. The first one is the source of all heights.

Example 9.2.9. Let \(C = \PP^1\), with affine coordinate \(x\), and let \(Q \coloneqq \infty = [1 : 0]\). The function \(t_\infty \coloneqq 1/x\) has a simple zero at \(\infty\) and no other zeros, so for \(P = [x : 1]\) with \(x \in k_v\), \[ d_v(P, \infty) = \min\bigl(1, \lvert x \rvert_v^{-1}\bigr) = \frac{1}{\max(1, \lvert x \rvert_v)}. \] The chordal distance is \(\delta_v(P, \infty) = \lvert x \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 \rvert_v/(\lVert P \rVert_v \cdot 1)\). If \(v\) is non-archimedean, this is \(1/\max(1, \lvert x \rvert_v) = d_v(P, \infty)\), so here \(d_v(\cdot, \infty)\) is exactly the ultrametric distance to \(\infty\). If \(v\) is archimedean, it is \(1/\sqrt{1 + \lvert x \rvert_v^2}\), and \(1 \leq d_v(P, \infty)/\delta_v(P, \infty) \leq \sqrt 2\).

Now let \(x \in k\). The height of Definition 8.5.3, computed with the coordinates \([x : 1]\), is \(H_k(x) = \prod_v \max(1, \lvert x \rvert_v)^{n_v}\). So

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ip-height-p1} \log H_k(x) = \sum_{v \in \Places_k} n_v \bigl(-\log d_v(x, \infty)\bigr). \tag{9.2.2} \end{equation}

The height of \(x\) measures how close \(x\) is to \(\infty\), at all places at once. An element of \(k\) has large height when it is very close to \(\infty\) at one place, or moderately close at many.

Example 9.2.10. Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(k_v\), given by a Weierstrass equation, and let \(Q \coloneqq O\). The function \(x\) has a double pole at \(O\) and no other poles, so we may take \(t_O \coloneqq 1/x\), with \(e = 2\): \[ d_v(P, O) = \min\bigl(1, \lvert x(P) \rvert_v^{-1/2}\bigr). \] The uniformizer \(x/y\) at \(O\) is not allowed, since it also vanishes at some \(\overline{k_v}\)-point with \(x = 0\). For \(P_1, P_2 \in E(k_v)\), let \(\hat d_v(P_1, P_2) \coloneqq d_v(P_1 - P_2, O)\). It is a \(v\)-adic distance to \(P_2\) in the sense of Definition 9.2.5, defined by the function \(P \mapsto 1/x(P - P_2)\), which has a double zero at \(P_2\) and no other zeros.

Let \(v\) be non-archimedean, and let the equation be minimal. A \(k_v\)-point \(P\) with \(v(x(P)) < 0\) reduces to \(\bar O\). So if \(P \notin E_1(k_v)\), then \(v(x(P)) \geq 0\), and \(d_v(P, O) = 1\). If \(P \in E_1(k_v) \setminus \{O\}\), then \(3v(x(P)) = 2v(y(P)) = -6r\) for some \(r \geq 1\) [Sil09, proof of Proposition VII.2.2]. Let \(z \coloneqq -x/y\). Then \(\lvert x(P) \rvert_v^{-1/2} = \lvert z(P) \rvert_v\), so \[ d_v(P, O) = \begin{cases} \lvert z(P) \rvert_v, & P \in E_1(k_v), \\ 1, & P \notin E_1(k_v). \end{cases} \] For non-archimedean \(v\), \(\hat d_v\) is a translation-invariant ultrametric on \(E(k_v)\). Indeed, \(z\) identifies \(E_1(k_v)\) with the group \(\hat E(\mm_v)\) of the formal group of \(E\), whose group law and inverse are power series \(F(T_1, T_2) = T_1 + T_2 + \cdots\) and \(i(T) = -T + \cdots\) with coefficients in \(\calO_v\) [Sil09, Section IV.1 and Proposition VII.2.2]. So \(\lvert z(P_1 + P_2) \rvert_v \leq \max(\lvert z(P_1) \rvert_v, \lvert z(P_2) \rvert_v)\) and \(\lvert z(-P) \rvert_v = \lvert z(P) \rvert_v\) on \(E_1(k_v)\). If \(P_1 - P_2\) or \(P_2 - P_3\) is not in the subgroup \(E_1(k_v)\), the ultrametric inequality reads \(\hat d_v(P_1, P_3) \leq 1\). So on \(E\), the class of every \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) contains a translation-invariant ultrametric, defined by the group law.

Let \(v\) be archimedean, and let the equation be \(y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\). By Theorem 1.7.1, \(E(\C) \cong \C/\Lambda\) for a lattice \(\Lambda\). Let \(P \in E(k_v)\) correspond to \(s + \Lambda\), so that \(x(P) = \wp_\Lambda(s)\). The Laurent series \(\wp_\Lambda(s) = s^{-2} + 3G_4(\Lambda)s^2 + \cdots\) [Sil09, Theorem VI.3.5] gives \(\lvert x(P) \rvert_v^{-1/2} = \lvert s \rvert (1 + O(\lvert s \rvert^4))\) near \(s = 0\). By the compactness argument of Lemma 9.2.6, \(d_v(P, O) \asymp \min_{\omega \in \Lambda} \lvert s - \omega \rvert\) on \(E(k_v)\). The right-hand side is the distance from \(s\) to \(\Lambda\), so \(\hat d_v\) is \(\asymp\) to the flat metric of the torus \(\C/\Lambda\).

The next result says that \(d_v\) measures the order of vanishing of every function at \(Q\).

Proposition 9.2.11. Let \(Q \in C(k_v)\), and let \(F \in k_v(C)\) vanish at \(Q\). Then \[ \lim_{P \to_v Q} \frac{\log \lvert F(P) \rvert_v}{\log d_v(P, Q)} = \ord_Q(F). \] In particular, the limit does not depend on the choice of \(t_Q\).

Proof.

We follow [Sil09, Proposition IX.2.2]. Let \(e \coloneqq \ord_Q(t_Q)\) and \(m \coloneqq \ord_Q(F) \geq 1\). The function \(g \coloneqq F^e/t_Q^m\) has neither a zero nor a pole at \(Q\), so \(\lvert g \rvert_v\) is bounded above and below by positive constants on a neighborhood of \(Q\). For \(P \neq Q\) close to \(Q\), \(F(P) \neq 0\), \(d_v(P, Q) = \lvert t_Q(P) \rvert_v^{1/e} < 1\), and \[ \log \lvert F(P) \rvert_v = m \log d_v(P, Q) + \tfrac1e \log \lvert g(P) \rvert_v. \] Divide by \(\log d_v(P, Q)\), which tends to \(-\infty\) as \(P\) tends to \(Q\).

The next result is about the effect of a morphism on distances. It depends on the ramification, not on the degree.

Proposition 9.2.12. Let \(\varphi\colon C_1 \to C_2\) be a non-constant morphism of nice curves over \(k_v\), and let \(Q \in C_1(k_v)\). Then \[ \lim_{P \to_v Q} \frac{\log d_v(\varphi(P), \varphi(Q))}{\log d_v(P, Q)} = e_\varphi(Q). \]

Proof.

We follow [Sil09, Proposition IX.2.3]. Let \(t_{\varphi(Q)} \in k_v(C_2)\) define \(d_v(\cdot, \varphi(Q))\), with \(e_2 \coloneqq \ord_{\varphi(Q)}(t_{\varphi(Q)})\). Writing \(t_{\varphi(Q)}\) as the \(e_2\)-th power of a uniformizer times a unit at \(\varphi(Q)\), Definition 3.2.7 gives \(\ord_Q(\varphi^* t_{\varphi(Q)}) = e_\varphi(Q) e_2\). The morphism \(\varphi\) is continuous, by part 2 of Lemma 9.2.4, and its fibers are finite. So for \(P \neq Q\) close to \(Q\), \(\varphi(P) \neq \varphi(Q)\) is close to \(\varphi(Q)\), and \[ \log d_v(\varphi(P), \varphi(Q)) = \frac{1}{e_2} \log \lvert (\varphi^* t_{\varphi(Q)})(P) \rvert_v. \] Apply Proposition 9.2.11 to \(F \coloneqq \varphi^* t_{\varphi(Q)}\).

Compare with heights. For a morphism \(F\colon \PP^N \to \PP^M\) of degree \(d\), \(h \circ F = d\,h + O(1)\) by Theorem 8.5.19: heights see the degree. Proposition 9.2.12 says that distances see the ramification. For an unramified morphism of large degree, such as the multiplication \([m]\) on an elliptic curve over a field of characteristic \(0\), the log-ratio of distances tends to \(1\) while heights are multiplied by \(m^2\). This contrast drives the proof of Siegel's theorem.

9.2.3 Local heights

Example 9.2.9 writes the height of \(x \in k\) as a sum over the places of \(-\log\) of a distance to \(\infty\). Local heights generalize this to any divisor on any curve [HS00, Section B.8]. Intuitively, the local height of \(P\) for a divisor \(D\) is \(-\log\) of the \(v\)-adic distance from \(P\) to \(D\).

Definition 9.2.13. Let \(C\) be a nice curve over \(k_v\), and let \(D = \sum_Q n_Q Q\) be a divisor on \(C\). A local height for \(D\) at \(v\) is a continuous function \(\lambda\colon C(k_v) \setminus \operatorname{supp} D \to \R\) such that, for each \(Q \in C(k_v)\) with \(n_Q \neq 0\) and a uniformizer \(u_Q\) at \(Q\), the function \(\lambda + n_Q \log \lvert u_Q \rvert_v\) is bounded on a punctured neighborhood of \(Q\).

The closed points of the support of \(D\) that are not \(k_v\)-points impose no condition, since they are not in \(C(k_v)\).

Proposition 9.2.14. Let \(D = \sum_Q n_Q Q\) be a divisor on a nice curve \(C\) over \(k_v\).

  1. Two local heights for \(D\) differ by a bounded function.
  2. The function \(-\sum_{Q \in C(k_v)} n_Q \log d_v(\cdot, Q)\) is a local height for \(D\).
  3. Let \(f \in k_v(C)^\times\). Then \(-\log \lvert f \rvert_v\) is a local height for \(\divv(f)\).
  4. Let \(f \in k_v(C)\) be non-constant, with pole divisor \(D_\infty \coloneqq \sum_{\ord_Q(f) < 0} (-\ord_Q f) Q\). Then \(\log \max(1, \lvert f \rvert_v)\) is a local height for \(D_\infty\).
Proof.

For part 1, the difference of two local heights is continuous on \(C(k_v)\) minus the finitely many \(k_v\)-points of \(\operatorname{supp} D\), and bounded on a punctured neighborhood of each of them. The complement of these neighborhoods is compact.

For part 2, each \(-\log d_v(\cdot, Q)\) is continuous away from \(Q\). Near \(Q\), write \(t_Q = u_Q^e h\) with \(h\) a unit at \(Q\). Then \(-\log d_v(\cdot, Q) + \log \lvert u_Q \rvert_v = -\tfrac1e \log \lvert h \rvert_v\) is bounded.

For part 3, near a \(k_v\)-point \(Q\) with \(n \coloneqq \ord_Q(f)\), write \(f = u_Q^n g\) with \(g\) a unit at \(Q\). Then \(-\log \lvert f \rvert_v + n \log \lvert u_Q \rvert_v = -\log \lvert g \rvert_v\) is bounded. For part 4, \(\log \max(1, \lvert f \rvert_v)\) is continuous away from the poles, and equals \(\log \lvert f \rvert_v\) near a pole. Conclude as for part 3.

Parts 1 and 2 say that the local heights for \(D\) are the functions \(-\sum n_Q \log d_v(\cdot, Q) + O(1)\). For instance, let \(f \in k_v(C)\) be non-constant. The pole divisor of \(1/f\) is the divisor of zeros of \(f\), and \(\log \max(1, \lvert 1/f \rvert_v) = -\log \min(1, \lvert f \rvert_v)\). So part 4, applied to \(1/f\), gives \[ \log \min(1, \lvert f \rvert_v) = \sum_{Q \in C(k_v),\ f(Q) = 0} \ord_Q(f) \log d_v(\cdot, Q) + O(1) \] on \(C(k_v)\) minus the zeros of \(f\) [Sil09, Exercise 9.16].

The local heights of a rational function add up to its height.

Proposition 9.2.15. Let \(C\) be a nice curve over \(k\), let \(f \in k(C)\) be non-constant, and let \(P \in C(k)\) not be a pole of \(f\). Then \[ \log H_k(f(P)) = \sum_{v \in \Places_k} n_v \log \max(1, \lvert f(P) \rvert_v). \] If the only pole of \(f\) in \(C(\kbar)\) is a \(k\)-point \(Q\), of order \(e\), and \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) is defined by \(t_Q \coloneqq 1/f\) at every place \(v\), then \(\log H_k(f(P)) = e \sum_{v \in \Places_k} n_v \bigl(-\log d_v(P, Q)\bigr)\).

Proof.

The first formula is Definition 8.5.3 for \([f(P) : 1]\). For the second, \(t_Q = 1/f\) has a zero of order \(e\) at \(Q\) and no other zeros, and \(-e \log d_v(P, Q) = -\log \min(\lvert f(P) \rvert_v^{-1}, 1) = \log \max(1, \lvert f(P) \rvert_v)\).

For \(C = \PP^1\) and \(f = x\), this is \eqref{eq:ip-height-p1}. Each term is a local height at \(v\), by part 4 of Proposition 9.2.14. The sum is finite, since almost all terms vanish. For an arbitrary choice of local heights, the bounded errors at infinitely many places need not add up to a bounded error. The general theory controls them uniformly in \(v\) [HS00, Theorem B.8.1], but we only need the exact identity of Proposition 9.2.15.

Remark 9.2.16. Let \(k'\) be a finite extension of \(k\), and \(w\) a place of \(k'\) above \(v\). The absolute values of Situation 9.0.1 are not normalized: \(\lvert\ \rvert_w\) restricts to \(\lvert\ \rvert_v\) on \(k_v\). So a function \(t_Q \in k_v(C)\) that defines \(d_v(\cdot, Q)\) also defines \(d_w(\cdot, Q)\), and the two agree on \(C(k_v)\).

Remark 9.2.17. A local height is defined only up to a bounded function, by part 1 of Proposition 9.2.14. On an elliptic curve \(E\), the group law selects a canonical one, as Tate's limit of Proposition 8.4.9 selects the canonical height among equivalent quasi-quadratic heights. Néron's local height \(\lambda_v\) is the unique continuous function on \(E(k_v) \setminus \{O\}\) such that \(\lambda_v(P) - \tfrac12 \log \lvert x(P) \rvert_v\) has a limit as \(P\) tends to \(O\), and that satisfies the duplication formula of [Sil09, Proposition C.18.1(iii)]. In particular it is a local height for the divisor \(O\). Let \(\hat h\) be the canonical height on \(E(k)\) of [Sil09, Section VIII.9], not yet constructed in these notes. It satisfies \(\hat h(P) = (2[k : \Q])^{-1} \log H_k(x(P)) + O(1)\), and it is the sum of the local heights: \(\hat h(P) = [k : \Q]^{-1} \sum_v n_v \lambda_v(P)\) for \(P \in E(k) \setminus \{O\}\) [Sil09, Theorem C.18.2], [HS00, Section B.9]. Proposition 9.2.15 with \(f = x\) gives \(\log H_k(x(P)) = 2 \sum_v n_v \bigl(-\log d_v(P, O)\bigr)\), so the functions \(-\log d_v(\cdot, O)\) add up to \(\hat h\) as well, up to a bounded function.

If \(v\) is non-archimedean and \(E\) has good reduction, with a minimal Weierstrass equation, then \(\lambda_v(P) = \log \max(1, \lvert x(P) \rvert_v^{1/2}) = -\log d_v(P, O)\), with \(t_O = 1/x\) as in Example 9.2.10 [Sil09, Theorem C.18.3(b)]. So in this case the choice of Example 9.2.10 is the canonical one. At a place of split multiplicative reduction, let \(N \coloneqq -v(j(E))\), and identify \(E(k_v)/E_0(k_v)\) with \(\Z/N\Z\). A \(k_v\)-point in the class of \(n\), with \(1 \leq n \leq N - 1\), has \(\lambda_v = \tfrac12 B_2(n/N) \log \lvert j(E) \rvert_v\), with \(B_2(T) \coloneqq T^2 - T + \tfrac16\) [Sil09, Theorem C.18.3(c)]. For \(N = 2\) and \(n = 1\), \(B_2(1/2) = -1/12\) and \(\lvert j(E) \rvert_v > 1\), so \(\lambda_v < 0\) there. Since \(d_v\) and \(\delta_v\) are at most \(1\), \(\lambda_v\) is then neither \(-\log d_v(\cdot, O)\), for any choice of \(t_O\), nor \(-\log \delta_v(\cdot, O)\). Canonical does not mean metric.

9.2.4 Roth's theorem in terms of distances

Theorem 9.1.10 says how fast elements of \(k\) can approach an algebraic number. In terms of distances, it says how fast \(k\)-points of a curve can approach a \(\kbar\)-point.

Corollary 9.2.18. Let \(C\) be a nice curve over \(k\), let \(f \in k(C)\) be non-constant, and let \(Q \in C(\kbar)\). Then \[ \liminf_{P \in C(k),\ P \to_v Q} \frac{\log d_v(P, Q)}{\log H_k(f(P))} \geq -2. \] If \(Q \notin C(k_v)\), or if \(Q\) is not a limit of \(k\)-points, the \(\liminf\) is taken to be \(0\).

Proof.

We follow [Sil09, Corollary IX.2.4]. If \(Q \notin C(k_v)\), then \(Q\) is not a limit of \(k_v\)-points, by part 4 of Lemma 9.2.4. So let \(Q \in C(k_v)\), and let \((P_i)\) be a sequence of distinct \(k\)-points, different from \(Q\), tending to \(Q\). We may replace \(f\) by \(1/f\). Indeed \(f\) has finitely many zeros, and \(H_k(1/a) = H_k(a)\) for \(a \in k^\times\), since \([1/a : 1] = [1 : a]\) and permuting the coordinates does not change the height. So we may assume that \(f\) is regular at \(Q\). Let \(\alpha \coloneqq f(Q)\). It lies in \(\kbar\), since \(Q \in C(\kbar)\), and in \(k_v\), since \(Q \in C(k_v)\). Let \(e \coloneqq \ord_Q(f - \alpha) \geq 1\). By Proposition 9.2.11, \[ \lim_{i \to \infty} \frac{\log \lvert f(P_i) - \alpha \rvert_v}{\log d_v(P_i, Q)} = e. \] The fibers of \(f\) are finite. So \(f(P_i) \neq \alpha\) for large \(i\), each element of \(k\) is \(f(P_i)\) for finitely many \(i\), and \(H_k(f(P_i)) \to \infty\) by Theorem 8.5.13. Let \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(\tau \coloneqq 2 + \varepsilon\). By Theorem 9.1.10, in the form of part 1 of Remark 9.1.6 with constant \(1\), \(\lvert f(P_i) - \alpha \rvert_v \geq H_k(f(P_i))^{-\tau}\) for all large \(i\). Then, for large \(i\), \[ \frac{\log d_v(P_i, Q)}{\log H_k(f(P_i))} = \frac{\log d_v(P_i, Q)}{\log \lvert f(P_i) - \alpha \rvert_v} \cdot \frac{\log \lvert f(P_i) - \alpha \rvert_v}{\log H_k(f(P_i))}, \] where the first factor is positive and tends to \(1/e\), and the second lies in \([-\tau, 0)\). So the \(\liminf\) is at least \(-\tau/e \geq -2 - \varepsilon\), for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).

Remark 9.2.19. Corollary 9.2.18 says nothing new for \(Q \in C(k)\). Then \(\alpha = f(Q) \in k\). For \(w \in \Places_k\), let \(c_w \coloneqq 2\) if \(w\) is archimedean and \(c_w \coloneqq 1\) otherwise, so that \(\prod_w c_w^{n_w} = 2^{[k : \Q]}\). For \(a \in k\) with \(a \neq \alpha\), the product formula (part 2 of Proposition 8.5.2) gives \[ \begin{aligned} \lvert a - \alpha \rvert_v^{n_v} = \prod_{w \neq v} \lvert a - \alpha \rvert_w^{-n_w} &\geq \prod_{w} \bigl(c_w \max(1, \lvert a \rvert_w) \max(1, \lvert \alpha \rvert_w)\bigr)^{-n_w} \\ &= \frac{1}{2^{[k : \Q]} H_k(\alpha) H_k(a)}, \end{aligned} \] So the proof gives a \(\liminf\) of at least \(-1/(e\,n_v) \geq -1\), which is better than \(-2\). This is the argument for rational \(\alpha\) in the proof of Corollary 9.1.7. The content of Corollary 9.2.18 is for \(\kbar\)-points that are not \(k\)-points.

Siegel's theorem, in the next section, improves the bound to \(0\) when \(C\) is an elliptic curve with infinitely many \(k\)-points. Let \(S \subseteq \Places_k\) be finite, and let \(P\) be a \(k\)-point with \(\lvert x(P) \rvert_w \leq 1\) for all \(w \notin S\). In Proposition 9.2.15 for \(f = x\), only the places of \(S\) contribute, so \(P\) is \(v\)-adically very close to \(O\) for some \(v \in S\). Writing \(P = [m]P' + R\) with \(R\) in a finite set, Proposition 9.2.12 for the unramified morphism \(P' \mapsto [m]P' + R\) keeps the log-ratio of distances near \(1\), while the height of \(P'\) is about \(m^{-2}\) times that of \(P\) [Sil09, Section IX.3].
