Chapter 2Algebraic varieties

This chapter follows [Poo06, Chapter 1] and [Sil09, Chapter I]. Unlike these sources, we do not assume that the ground field is perfect.

The goal of this chapter is to describe the categories of affine and projective varieties over a field \(k\) through their functor of points. The goal is to give a quick and easy way to think about scheme theoretic concepts concretely in the context of varieties.

Situation 2.0.1. The field \(k\) is arbitrary, and as in the preface \(\ksep \subseteq \kbar\) are a separable and an algebraic closure, and \(\Gal_k \coloneqq \Gal(\ksep/k)\). We do not assume \(k\) perfect: the difference between a property of \(X\) and the same property of \(X_{\kbar}\) is one of the subjects of the chapter.

2.1 Varieties and their points

Recall that a \(k\)-algebra \(A\) is of finite type if it admits a surjective homomorphism from a polynomial ring \(k[x_1,\dots,x_n] \to A\). In other words, every such \(k\)-algebra is isomorphic to \[ k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/I \] for some ideal \(I\). Since polynomial rings are Noetherian, the ideal \(I\) is generated by a finite number of polynomials \(f_1, \dots, f_m\).

Definition 2.1.1. An affine \(k\)-variety is a scheme over \(k\) isomorphic to \(\Spec A\) for some \(k\)-algebra \(A\) of finite type.

Recall that a topological space \(X\) is Hausdorff if and only if the diagonal subset \(\Delta(X) = \{(x_1,x_2) \in X\times X : x_1 = x_2\}\) is closed. Affine \(k\)-varieties are usually not Hausdorff (the opens in the Zariski topology are too big), but they always satisfy that the diagonal morphism \(\Delta \colon X \to X \times_{k} X\), which is induced by the multiplication homomorphism \(A\otimes_k A \to A\), is a closed embedding. Schemes with this property are called separated.

Definition 2.1.2. A \(k\)-variety is a separated scheme of finite type over \(k\). A morphism of \(k\)-varieties is a morphism of \(k\)-schemes.

Whatever the precise definition of these words is, the bottom line is that a \(k\)-variety is a topological space \(X\) that can be glued from open affine subvarieties in such a way that the end result is separated. Likewise, a morphism of \(k\)-schemes \(f\colon X \to Y\) between \(k\)-varieties is one that can be glued from morphisms of the form \(\Spec(\phi)\colon \Spec B \to \Spec A\), where \(\phi\colon A \to B\) is a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism.

If you don't know what \(\Spec\) means yet (go read this chapter in Vakil [Vak23] as soon as possible!), it will be enough for the time being to understand \(k\)-varieties \(X\) through their sets of \(R\)-points, denoted \(X(R)\), as we range over all \(k\)-algebras \(R\). For an affine \(k\)-variety \(X = \Spec A\) there is a bijection, natural in \(R\),

\begin{equation*} X(R) \cong \Hom_k(A, R) \cong \left\{ \mathbf{r} \in R^n : f_1(\mathbf{r}) = \dots = f_m(\mathbf{r}) = 0\right\}. \end{equation*}

In general,

Definition 2.1.3. For a \(k\)-algebra \(R\), the set of \(R\)-points of a \(k\)-variety \(X\) is

\begin{equation*} X(R) \coloneqq \Hom_k(\Spec R, X). \end{equation*}

The elements of \(X(k)\) are the rational points of \(X\).

Observe that this assignment is functorial: given a \(k\)-algebra homomorphism \(\psi\colon R \to S\), we obtain a function \(X(R) \to X(S)\). Indeed, if \(\mathbf{r} \in X(R)\), since the defining equations \(f_1, \dots, f_m\) have coefficients in \(k\), we see that \(f(\psi(\mathbf{r})) = \psi(f(\mathbf{r})) = \psi(0) = 0 \) for each \(f \in I\), so \(\psi(\mathbf{r}) \in X(S)\).

Example 2.1.4. Given \(n \geq 1\), affine \(n\)-space is \(\AA^n_k \coloneqq \Spec k[x_1, \dots, x_n]\). A \(k\)-algebra homomorphism \(k[x_1, \dots, x_n] \to R\) is determined by the images of the variables \(x_i\), so \(\AA^n(R) = R^n\) for every \(k\)-algebra \(R\). An ideal \(I \subseteq k[x_1, \dots, x_n]\) defines the closed subvariety \(V(I) \coloneqq \Spec k[x_1, \dots, x_n]/I\) of \(\AA^n_k\), with

\begin{equation*} V(I)(R) = \{\mathbf{r} \in R^n : f(\mathbf{r}) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}. \end{equation*}

Example 2.1.5. Let \(X = \Spec k[x]\), let \(Y = \Spec k[y]\), and let \(f\colon X \to Y\) be the map of \(k\)-varieties induced by the ring homomorphism \(\varphi\colon k[y] \to k[x]\), \(y \mapsto x^2\). The induced function on \(R\)-points, denoted \(f(R)\colon X(R) \to Y(R)\), sends the \(R\)-point \(r \in R = X(R)\) to \(r^2 \in R = Y(R)\). Indeed, the \(R\)-point \(r\) is the \(k\)-algebra homomorphism \(k[x] \to R\), \(x \mapsto r\), and composing it with \(\varphi\) gives the commutative diagram of \(k\)-algebras

whose composite is the \(R\)-point \(r^2\) of \(Y\).

Example 2.1.6. The multiplicative group is \(\Gm \coloneqq \Spec k[x, x^{-1}]\). Since \[ k[x, x^{-1}] \cong k[x, y]/(xy - 1), \] \(\Gm\) can be embedded in the affine plane as \(\Gm \cong V(xy - 1) \subseteq \AA^2_k\). It is also the open subvariety \(\{x \neq 0\}\) of \(\AA^1_k\). A \(k\)-algebra homomorphism \(k[x, x^{-1}] \to R\) is determined by the image of \(x\), which can be any unit of \(R\), so

\begin{equation*} \Gm(R) = R^\times \end{equation*}

for every \(k\)-algebra \(R\). The group structure on \(R^\times\) is natural in \(R\); this is the sense in which \(\Gm\) is a group.

The multiplicative group is our first example of a group variety [Poo06, Section 1.11].

Definition 2.1.7. A group variety over \(k\) is a \(k\)-variety \(G\) with morphisms \(m\colon G \times G \to G\), \(i\colon G \to G\) and \(e\colon \Spec k \to G\) satisfying the group axioms, written as commutative diagrams of morphisms. A homomorphism of group varieties is a morphism that respects \(m\), \(i\) and \(e\).

Equivalently, by Yoneda's lemma, a group variety is a \(k\)-variety \(G\) with a group structure on \(G(R)\) for every \(k\)-algebra \(R\), natural in \(R\); this is how we saw that \(\Gm\) is a group variety in Example 2.1.6.

The group varieties of dimension \(1\) that are not elliptic curves are affine, and there are few of them [Poo08, Section 6.2]. To list them we need a quadratic algebra. Fix \(d \in k\), with \(d \neq 0\) if \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), and let

\begin{equation} \label{eq:var-quadratic-algebra} L_d \coloneqq \begin{cases} k[t]/(t^2 - d) & \text{if } \operatorname{char} k \neq 2, \\ k[t]/(t^2 + t + d) & \text{if } \operatorname{char} k = 2. \end{cases} \tag{2.1.1} \end{equation}

In both cases the polynomial is separable, so \(L_d\) is either a separable quadratic extension field of \(k\) or isomorphic to \(k \times k\). Its non-trivial automorphism sends \(t\) to \(-t\), respectively \(t + 1\), and the norm of \(x + yt\) is

\begin{equation} \label{eq:var-quadratic-norm} \Nm(x + yt) = \begin{cases} x^2 - dy^2 & \text{if } \operatorname{char} k \neq 2, \\ x^2 + xy + dy^2 & \text{if } \operatorname{char} k = 2. \end{cases} \tag{2.1.2} \end{equation}

Example 2.1.8. The following are group varieties of dimension \(1\) over \(k\): the additive group \(\Ga\), the multiplicative group \(\Gm\) of Example 2.1.6, and the twisted multiplicative group \(\Gm^{(d)}\).

\(G\) variety \(m\bigl((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2)\bigr)\) \(G(k)\)
\(\Ga\) \(\AA^1\) \(x_1 + x_2\) \(k\), under \(+\)
\(\Gm\) \(xy = 1\) \((x_1x_2, y_1y_2)\) \(k^\times\)
\(\Gm^{(d)}\) \(\Nm(x + yt) = 1\) \((x_1x_2 + dy_1y_2,\ x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)\) \(\ker(\Nm\colon L_d^\times \to k^\times)\)

The group law of \(\Gm^{(d)}\) is multiplication in \(L_d\), with \((x, y)\) standing for \(x + yt\); in characteristic \(2\), where \(t^2 = t + d\), its second coordinate is \(x_1y_2 + x_2y_1 + y_1y_2\). The identity is \((1, 0)\) and the inverse is conjugation. More generally \(\Ga(R) = R\), \(\Gm(R) = R^\times\) and

\begin{equation*} \Gm^{(d)}(R) = \ker\bigl(\Nm\colon (L_d \otimes_k R)^\times \to R^\times\bigr) \end{equation*}

for every \(k\)-algebra \(R\); an element of norm \(1\) is a unit, its inverse being its conjugate. Over \(\R\), \(\Gm^{(-1)}\) is the circle group \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\), and over \(\Q\), \(\Gm^{(2)}\) is the "Pell equation torus" \(x^2 - 2y^2 = 1\) of [Poo06, Example 1.11.3].

Proposition 2.1.9. Let \(d\) and \(L_d\) be as in \eqref{eq:var-quadratic-algebra}.

  1. If \(L_d \cong k \times k\), then \(\Gm^{(d)} \cong \Gm\). In particular \(\Gm^{(1)} \cong \Gm\) if \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), and \(\Gm^{(0)} \cong \Gm\) if \(\operatorname{char} k = 2\).
  2. \((\Gm^{(d)})_{L_d} \cong (\Gm)_{L_d}\). The isomorphism class of \(\Gm^{(d)}\) depends only on the class of \(d\) in \(k^\times/k^{\times 2}\), respectively in \(k/\{c^2 + c : c \in k\}\).
  3. If \(L_d\) is a field, then \(\Gm^{(d)} \not\cong \Gm\) over \(k\).
  4. If \(k = \F_q\) and \(L_d\) is a field, then \(\Gm^{(d)}(\F_q)\) is cyclic of order \(q + 1\).
Proof.

For part 1, \(L_d \cong k \times k\) means that \(t^2 - d\), respectively \(t^2 + t + d\), has a root \(\delta \in k\). Let \(\delta'\) be the other one, \(-\delta\), respectively \(\delta + 1\), so that \(\delta' \neq \delta\). Then \(\Nm(x + yt) = (x + \delta y)(x + \delta' y)\), and \((x, y) \mapsto x + \delta y\), the restriction of the ring homomorphism \(L_d \to k\), \(t \mapsto \delta\), is a homomorphism \(\Gm^{(d)} \to \Gm\). Its inverse is

\begin{equation*} u \mapsto \left(\frac{\delta'u - \delta u^{-1}}{\delta' - \delta},\ \frac{u^{-1} - u}{\delta' - \delta}\right), \end{equation*}

since \(x + \delta' y = (x + \delta y)^{-1}\) on \(\Gm^{(d)}\).

For part 2, the image of \(t\) is a root in \(L_d\), and \(L_d \otimes_k L_d \cong L_d \times L_d\), so part 1 applies over \(L_d\). Replacing \(d\) by \(c^2d\) with \(c \in k^\times\), respectively by \(d + c^2 + c\) with \(c \in k\), gives an isomorphic group variety, through \((x, y) \mapsto (x, y/c)\), respectively \((x, y) \mapsto (x + cy, y)\): both are the isomorphisms \(L_{c^2d} \to L_d\), \(t \mapsto ct\), respectively \(L_{d + c^2 + c} \to L_d\), \(t \mapsto t + c\), written in coordinates.

For part 3, the projective closure \(\overline{G} \coloneqq V_+(\Nm(X + YT) - Z^2) \subseteq \PP^2_k\) of \(\Gm^{(d)}\), where \(\Nm(X + YT)\) is the quadratic form \eqref{eq:var-quadratic-norm} in \(X\) and \(Y\), is a smooth conic: its partial derivatives vanish together only at \((0, 0, 0)\). The complement \(\overline{G} - \Gm^{(d)} = V_+(\Nm(X + YT), Z)\) is a single closed point with residue field \(L_d\), because \(\Nm(X + YT)\) is irreducible over \(k\). The complement of \(\Gm\) in its projective closure \(\PP^1_k\) is two rational points, \(0\) and \(\infty\). An isomorphism \(\Gm^{(d)} \to \Gm\) and its inverse extend to morphisms between the projective closures (Proposition 3.2.1), inverse to each other, which would match the complements.

For part 4, \(L_d = \F_{q^2}\), whose non-trivial automorphism is \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha^q\), so the norm is \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha^{q + 1}\). Its kernel in the cyclic group \(\F_{q^2}^\times\) of order \(q^2 - 1\) is cyclic of order \(q + 1\).

Definition 2.1.10. A group variety \(T\) over \(k\) is a torus if \(T_{\kbar} \cong (\Gm^n)_{\kbar}\) for some \(n \geq 0\), and a split torus if \(T \cong \Gm^n\) over \(k\) [Poo06, Definition 1.11.4].

By Proposition 2.1.9, \(\Gm^{(d)}\) is a torus of dimension \(1\), split exactly when \(L_d \cong k \times k\); for \(d = 2\) over \(\Q\) this is [Poo06, Exercise 1.7]. Over a perfect field of characteristic not \(2\), every connected group variety of dimension \(1\) is an elliptic curve, \(\Ga\), \(\Gm\) or some \(\Gm^{(d)}\) [Poo08, Section 6.2], [Mil20, Section II.3]. In Chapter 4 the last three appear as the nonsingular parts of singular Weierstrass cubics.

In the complex analytic world, \(\PP^n(\C)\) is the set of lines through the origin in \(\C^{n+1}\). For \(n = 1, 2\) we saw that it is covered by \(n + 1\) copies of \(\C^n\): see Example 1.1.4 and Section 1.6. In the algebraic world, we want to define \(\PP^n_k\) in a similar way, as the quotient of \(\AA^{n+1}_k - \{0\}\) by the scaling action of \(\Gm\) (Example 2.1.6). This is what the \(\Proj\) construction achieves. Still, it is quite tricky to describe the set \(\PP^n(R)\).

Example 2.1.11. Projective \(n\)-space is \(\PP^n_k \coloneqq \Proj k[X_0, \dots, X_n]\). A homogeneous ideal \(I \subseteq k[X_0, \dots, X_n]\) defines the closed subvariety \(V_+(I) \coloneqq \Proj k[X_0, \dots, X_n]/I\) of \(\PP^n_k\).

Let \(R\) be a \(k\)-algebra. To describe a point of \(\PP^n(R)\), consider the tuples \((P, s_0, \dots, s_n)\), where \(P\) is an invertible \(R\)-module – a finitely generated projective \(R\)-module of constant rank \(1\) – and \(s_0, \dots, s_n\) are elements generating \(P\). Call \((P, s_0, \dots, s_n)\) and \((P', s_0', \dots, s_n')\) equivalent if some isomorphism of \(R\)-modules \(\varphi\colon P \to P'\) satisfies \(\varphi(s_i) = s_i'\) for every \(i\). A point of \(\PP^n(R)\) is then an equivalence class \([P, s_0, \dots, s_n]\) [Har77, Theorem II.7.1].

To evaluate a homogeneous polynomial at such a point, note that the tensor power \(P^{\otimes d} \coloneqq P \otimes_R \dots \otimes_R P\) is again invertible. Write \(\bfX \coloneqq (X_0, \dots, X_n)\) and \(\bfs \coloneqq (s_0, \dots, s_n)\), and for a multi-index \(\bfa = (a_0, \dots, a_n)\) of non-negative integers write \(\lvert \bfa \rvert \coloneqq a_0 + \dots + a_n\). A monomial \(\bfX^{\bfa} = X_0^{a_0} \dotsm X_n^{a_n}\) with \(\lvert \bfa \rvert = d\) has a value

\begin{equation*} \bfs^{\bfa} \coloneqq \underbrace{s_0 \otimes \dots \otimes s_0}_{a_0} \otimes \dots \otimes \underbrace{s_n \otimes \dots \otimes s_n}_{a_n} \in P^{\otimes d}. \end{equation*}

So a homogeneous \(F = \sum_{\lvert \bfa \rvert = d} c_{\bfa} \bfX^{\bfa} \in k[X_0, \dots, X_n]\) of degree \(d\) has a value

\begin{equation*} F(\bfs) \coloneqq \sum_{\lvert \bfa \rvert = d} c_{\bfa} \bfs^{\bfa} \in P^{\otimes d}, \end{equation*}

and \(\varphi^{\otimes d}\) carries \(F(\bfs)\) to \(F(\bfs')\), so whether this value vanishes depends only on the \(R\)-point. The \(R\)-point lies in \(V_+(I)(R)\) if and only if \(F(\bfs) = 0\) for every homogeneous \(F \in I\).

If every invertible \(R\)-module is free, e.g. if \(R\) is a field, a local ring or a principal ideal domain, then choosing a basis of \(P\) gives

\begin{equation*} \PP^n(R) = \{(a_0, \dots, a_n) \in R^{n+1} : a_0R + \dots + a_nR = R\}/R^\times \end{equation*}

and

\begin{equation*} V_+(I)(R) = \{(a_0 : \dots : a_n) \in \PP^n(R) : F(a) = 0 \text{ for all homogeneous } F \in I\}. \end{equation*}

When \(R\) is a field, the condition \(a_0R + \dots + a_nR = R\) says that \(a \neq 0\), so

\begin{equation*} \PP^n(R) = \bigl(R^{n+1} - \{0\}\bigr)/R^\times. \end{equation*}

For general \(R\) there is no such description: an \(R\)-point whose \(P\) is not free has no homogeneous coordinates.

The open subschemes \(U_i \coloneqq \{X_i \neq 0\}\) of \(\PP^n_k\) are isomorphic to \(\AA^n_k\), and cover it; the affine coordinates on \(U_0\) are the ratios \(x_i = X_i/X_0\). For a closed subvariety \(X \subseteq \PP^n_k\), the \(X \cap U_i\) are the standard affine patches of \(X\); the equations of \(X \cap U_0\) are obtained by setting \(X_0 = 1\) (dehomogenization).

Conversely, identify \(\AA^n_k\) with the patch \(U_0 \subseteq \PP^n_k\). The projective closure of a closed subvariety \(X = V(I) \subseteq \AA^n_k\) is the Zariski closure \(\overline{X}\) of \(X\) in \(\PP^n_k\). It is cut out by the homogenizations of the elements of \(I\). The homogenization of \(f \in k[x_1, \dots, x_n]\) is the homogeneous polynomial \(F \in k[X_0, \dots, X_n]\) of the same degree given by

\begin{equation*} F(X_0, \dots, X_n) \coloneqq X_0^{\deg f}\, f(X_1/X_0, \dots, X_n/X_0), \end{equation*}

and \(\overline{X} = V_+(J)\), where \(J\) is the homogeneous ideal generated by the homogenizations of all elements of \(I\). For a single non-zero \(f\) this says that the projective closure of \(V(f)\) is \(V_+(F)\). For several equations one must homogenize every element of the ideal, not only a set of generators.

Example 2.1.12. The twisted cubic is the affine curve \(C \coloneqq V(I) \subseteq \AA^3_k\), where

\begin{equation*} I \coloneqq (x_2 - x_1^2,\ x_3 - x_1^3), \end{equation*}

the image of \(t \mapsto (t, t^2, t^3)\). Homogenizing the two generators of \(I\) gives the quadric surface \(V_+(X_0X_2 - X_1^2)\) and the cubic surface \(V_+(X_0^2X_3 - X_1^3)\) in \(\PP^3_k\). On the hyperplane \(X_0 = 0\) both equations reduce to \(X_1 = 0\), so their intersection contains the whole line \(\{X_0 = X_1 = 0\}\). But \(\overline{C}\) meets \(X_0 = 0\) only in \((0 : 0 : 0 : 1)\), the limit of the points \((1 : t : t^2 : t^3)\) of \(C\) for large \(t\); the projective closure is therefore strictly smaller.

The identity

\begin{equation*} x_1x_3 - x_2^2 = x_1(x_3 - x_1^3) + (x_1^2 - x_2)(x_1^2 + x_2) \end{equation*}

shows that \(x_1x_3 - x_2^2\) lies in \(I\). Its homogenization \(X_1X_3 - X_2^2\) is again of degree \(2\), and the quadric surface \(V_+(X_1X_3 - X_2^2)\) it defines cuts the spurious line down to the rational point \((0 : 0 : 0 : 1)\): on \(\{X_0 = X_1 = 0\}\) the form \(X_1X_3 - X_2^2\) restricts to \(-X_2^2\), which forces \(X_2 = 0\). So this quadric is needed to cut out \(\overline{C}\), although it is not in the ideal generated by the homogenizations of the two generators of \(I\).

Definition 2.1.13. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety.

  1. \(X\) is irreducible if its underlying topological space is non-empty and is not the union of two proper closed subsets.
  2. \(X\) is reduced if for every open \(U \subseteq X\), the ring \(\calO_X(U)\) has no non-zero nilpotent elements.
  3. \(X\) is integral if for every non-empty open \(U \subseteq X\), the ring \(\calO_X(U)\) is an integral domain.

A \(k\)-variety is integral if and only if it is reduced and irreducible [Har77, Proposition II.3.1]. For an affine variety \(X = V(I) \subseteq \AA^n_k\): \(X\) is reduced if and only if \(I\) is radical, irreducible if and only if \(\sqrt{I}\) is prime, and integral if and only if \(I\) is prime.

Warning 2.1.14. Varieties need not be reduced or irreducible (Definition 2.1.13). The variety \(V(x^2) \subseteq \AA^1_k\) is irreducible but not reduced, and \(V(xy) \subseteq \AA^2_k\) is reduced but not irreducible. For every field \(L\), both \(V(x^2)(L)\) and \(V(x)(L)\) consist of the single \(L\)-point \(0\), but \(V(x^2) \neq V(x)\): their points with values in \(R = k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\) differ.

The rational points do not determine a variety either.

Example 2.1.15. Consider the three closed subvarieties

\begin{equation*} C_1 \coloneqq V_+(X^2 + Y^2 + Z^2), \qquad C_2 \coloneqq V_+(X^2 + Y^2 - 3Z^2), \qquad C_3 \coloneqq V_+(1) \end{equation*}

of \(\PP^2_\Q\). None of them has a rational point.

For \(C_1\) there is nothing to prove over \(\Q\): already \(C_1(\R) = \emptyset\), since a sum of three squares of real numbers vanishes only when all three do. The variety \(C_3\) is empty, so it has no points with values in any \(\Q\)-algebra at all.

For \(C_2\) we argue by descent. A point of \(C_2(\Q)\) can be scaled to a solution \((X_0, Y_0, Z_0) \in \Z^3\) with \(\gcd(X_0, Y_0, Z_0) = 1\). Reduce the equation \(X_0^2 + Y_0^2 = 3Z_0^2\) modulo \(3\). Since \(-1\) is not a square modulo \(3\), the congruence \(X_0^2 + Y_0^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) forces \(3 \mid X_0\) and \(3 \mid Y_0\). Then \(9 \mid 3Z_0^2\), so \(3 \mid Z_0\), contradicting \(\gcd(X_0, Y_0, Z_0) = 1\).

The three varieties are nevertheless pairwise distinct: their homogeneous ideals differ, and this is already visible on points with values in larger fields. Indeed \(C_3(\C) = \emptyset\), while \(C_1(\C) \neq \emptyset\); and \(C_1(\R) = \emptyset\), while \((\sqrt 3 : 0 : 1) \in C_2(\R)\).

Example 2.1.16. The cubic \(E \coloneqq V_+(Y^2Z - X^3 - 17Z^3) \subseteq \PP^2_\Q\) is the projective closure of the affine curve \(y^2 = x^3 + 17\), where \(x = X/Z\) and \(y = Y/Z\). Setting \(Z = 0\) leaves \(X^3 = 0\), so \(E\) has a single point at infinity, \((0 : 1 : 0)\), and

\begin{equation*} E(\Q) = \{(x, y) \in \Q^2 : y^2 = x^3 + 17\} \cup \{(0 : 1 : 0)\}, \end{equation*}

which contains for instance \((-2, 3)\) and \((5234, 378661)\) [Sil09, Examples I.1.3.3 and I.2.8].

The Galois group \(\Gal_k\) acts on \(X(\ksep)\) through its action on \(\ksep\); on \(\ksep\)-points of \(\AA^n\) or \(\PP^n\) it acts on coordinates.

Proposition 2.1.17. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety. Then \(X(k) = X(\ksep)^{\Gal_k}\).

Proof.

Every \(\ksep\)-point of \(X\) lies in \(U(\ksep)\) for some affine open subvariety \(U \subseteq X\), and \(U(\ksep)\) is stable under \(\Gal_k\). So it suffices to treat closed subvarieties of \(\AA^n_k\), where the claim follows from Galois theory: \((\ksep)^{\Gal_k} = k\).

For a closed subvariety of \(\PP^n_k\) one can also argue in homogeneous coordinates: let \(P = (a_0 : \dots : a_n) \in X(\ksep)\) be fixed by \(\Gal_k\). Then \(\sigma(a_i) = \lambda_\sigma a_i\) for some \(\lambda_\sigma \in (\ksep)^\times\), and \(\sigma \mapsto \lambda_\sigma\) is a \(1\)-cocycle. By Hilbert's Theorem 90 there is \(\alpha \in (\ksep)^\times\) with \(\lambda_\sigma = \alpha/\sigma(\alpha)\), and then all \(\alpha a_i\) lie in \(k\).

Warning 2.1.18. [Sil09] works with \(\kbar\) instead of \(\ksep\). The fixed field of \(\Aut(\kbar/k)\) is the perfect closure \(k^{\perf}\), so \(X(\kbar)^{\Aut(\kbar/k)} = X(k^{\perf})\), which can be larger than \(X(k)\) when \(k\) is imperfect. For example, if \(k = \F_p(t)\) and \(X = V(x^p - t) \subseteq \AA^1_k\), then \(X(k) = \emptyset\), while the unique point \(t^{1/p}\) of \(X(\kbar)\) is fixed by \(\Aut(\kbar/k)\).

Remark 2.1.19. In [Sil09, Section I.1], whose ground field \(K\) is our \(k\), a variety \(V/K\) is a subset of \(\AA^n(\kbar)\) or \(\PP^n(\kbar)\), cut out by an ideal \(I(V/K)\) with coefficients in \(K\). It corresponds to the \(k\)-variety \(X \coloneqq V(I(V/K))\) or \(V_+(I(V/K))\): then \(V = X(\kbar)\) and \(V(K) = X(k)\). The standing assumptions of [Sil09] (that \(I(V/K)\) generates the ideal of \(V\), and that it is prime over \(\kbar\)) say that \(X\) is geometrically integral (Definition 2.2.2).

Draft21 Sep 20262.2 Base extension and geometric properties

Definition 2.2.1. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety and \(L \supseteq k\) a field extension. The base extension of \(X\) to \(L\) is the \(L\)-variety \(X_L \coloneqq X \times_{\Spec k} \Spec L\).

Concretely, \(X_L\) is defined by the same equations as \(X\), with the coefficients viewed in \(L\): if \(X = V(f_1, \dots, f_m) \subseteq \AA^n_k\), then \(X_L = V(f_1, \dots, f_m) \subseteq \AA^n_L\), and likewise for projective varieties. We have \(X(L) = X_L(L)\) [Poo06, Section 1.3].

Definition 2.2.2. A \(k\)-variety \(X\) is geometrically reduced, geometrically irreducible or geometrically integral if \(X_{\kbar}\) is reduced, irreducible or integral (Definition 2.1.13), respectively.

Definition 2.2.3. Let \(X\) be an integral \(k\)-variety. Its function field \(k(X)\) is the local ring at the generic point of \(X\): for \(X = \Spec A\) it is \(\Frac A\), and in general it is the function field of any non-empty affine open subvariety. Its elements are the rational functions on \(X\) [Poo06, Definitions 1.4.4 and 1.4.8].

Proposition 2.2.4. Let \(X\) be an integral \(k\)-variety.

  1. \(X\) is geometrically irreducible if and only if \(k\) is separably closed in \(k(X)\).
  2. \(X\) is geometrically reduced if and only if the extension \(k(X)/k\) is separable, i.e. separably generated.
  3. If \(k\) is perfect, then \(X\) is geometrically reduced.
Proof.

Part 1 is [Poo06, Remarks 1.4.6 and 1.4.7]. Part 3 follows from part 2, since every finitely generated extension of a perfect field is separably generated.

Example 2.2.5. The curve \(X \coloneqq V(x^2 - 2y^2) \subseteq \AA^2_\Q\) is integral, since \(x^2 - 2y^2\) is irreducible in \(\Q[x, y]\). Over \(\Q(\sqrt 2)\) it becomes the union of the lines \(x = \pm\sqrt 2\, y\), so \(X\) is not geometrically irreducible. Accordingly \(x/y \in \Q(X)\) satisfies \((x/y)^2 = 2\) [Poo06, Example 1.4.3].

Example 2.2.6. Let \(k \coloneqq \F_p(a)\) and \(f \coloneqq x^p + ay^p\). Then \(f\) is irreducible in \(k[x, y]\), so \(X \coloneqq V(f) \subseteq \AA^2_k\) is integral. Over \(\kbar\), \(f = (x + \alpha y)^p\) with \(\alpha^p = a\), so \(X\) is not geometrically reduced [Mil20, Example I.1.2]. Accordingly \((x/y)^p = -a\) in \(k(X)\), so \(k(X)\) contains the inseparable extension \(k(a^{1/p})\) of \(k\).

Draft21 Sep 20262.3 Morphisms and rational maps

Definition 2.3.1. Let \(X\) be an integral \(k\)-variety and \(Y\) a \(k\)-variety. A rational map \(\varphi\colon X \dashrightarrow Y\) is an equivalence class of morphisms \(U \to Y\) with \(U \subseteq X\) a non-empty open subvariety, two being equivalent if they agree on the intersection of their domains. It is dominant if its image is dense. Integral \(k\)-varieties \(X\) and \(Y\) are birational if there are dominant rational maps \(X \dashrightarrow Y\) and \(Y \dashrightarrow X\) inverse to each other [Poo06, Definitions 1.5.2, 1.5.3 and 1.5.5].

Explicitly, a rational map \(\varphi\colon X \dashrightarrow \PP^n_k\) is a tuple \((f_0 : \dots : f_n)\) of elements of \(k(X)\), not all zero, modulo multiplication by \(k(X)^\times\). It is regular at a point \(P\) of \(X\) if there is \(g \in k(X)^\times\) such that every \(gf_i\) lies in \(\calO_{X,P}\) and some \((gf_i)(P) \in k(P)\) is non-zero; then \(\varphi(P) \coloneqq ((gf_0)(P) : \dots : (gf_n)(P))\). A rational map to a closed subvariety \(Y \subseteq \PP^n_k\) is one whose image lies in \(Y\), and it is a morphism if it is regular at every point [Poo06, Definitions 1.5.8 and 1.5.9], [Sil09, Section I.3]. For \(X \subseteq \PP^m_k\), clearing denominators writes \(\varphi\) as a tuple of homogeneous polynomials of the same degree, not all in the ideal of \(X\) [Sil09, Remark I.3.2].

Remark 2.3.2. A morphism of \(k\)-varieties is automatically defined over \(k\). A map "defined over \(\kbar\)" in the sense of [Sil09, Section I.3] is a morphism \(X_{\kbar} \to Y_{\kbar}\) [Poo06, Warning 1.5.7]. Varieties \(X\) and \(Y\) are isomorphic over \(L\) if \(X_L \cong Y_L\).

Theorem 2.3.3. The functor \(X \mapsto k(X)\) is an equivalence between the category of integral \(k\)-varieties with dominant rational maps and the opposite of the category of finitely generated field extensions of \(k\) with \(k\)-algebra homomorphisms. In particular, integral \(k\)-varieties \(X\) and \(Y\) are birational if and only if \(k(X) \cong k(Y)\) over \(k\).

Proof (Poo06).

See [Poo06, equation (1.5.4) and Definition 1.5.6].

Example 2.3.4. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), and let \(C \coloneqq V_+(X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2) \subseteq \PP^2_k\). The rational map \(\varphi \coloneqq (X + Z : Y)\colon C \dashrightarrow \PP^1_k\) is regular away from the rational point \((1 : 0 : -1)\). Since \((X + Z)(X - Z) = -Y^2\) on \(C\), also \(\varphi = (-Y : X - Z)\), which is regular at \((1 : 0 : -1)\) with value \((0 : 1)\). So \(\varphi\) is a morphism. The morphism

\begin{equation*} \psi\colon \PP^1_k \to C, \qquad (S : T) \mapsto (S^2 - T^2 : 2ST : S^2 + T^2) \end{equation*}

is inverse to \(\varphi\), so \(C \cong \PP^1_k\) [Sil09, Example I.3.5].

Example 2.3.5. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\), and let \(C \coloneqq V_+(Y^2Z - X^3 - X^2Z) \subseteq \PP^2_k\). The morphism

\begin{equation*} \psi\colon \PP^1_k \to C, \qquad (S : T) \mapsto \bigl((S^2 - T^2)T : (S^2 - T^2)S : T^3\bigr) \end{equation*}

and the rational map \(\varphi \coloneqq (Y : X)\colon C \dashrightarrow \PP^1_k\) are inverse to each other where defined, so \(C\) is birational to \(\PP^1_k\). But \(\varphi\) is not regular at \((0 : 0 : 1)\), and \(\psi\) sends both \((1 : 1)\) and \((1 : -1)\) to \((0 : 0 : 1)\), so \(C \not\cong \PP^1_k\). The rational point \((0 : 0 : 1)\) is singular (Example 2.4.5) [Sil09, Example I.3.7].

Example 2.3.6. Let \(C \coloneqq V_+(X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2)\) and \(C_2 \coloneqq V_+(X^2 + Y^2 - 3Z^2)\) in \(\PP^2_\Q\). By Examples 2.3.4 and 2.1.15, \(C(\Q)\) is infinite and \(C_2(\Q)\) is empty, so \(C \not\cong C_2\). But \((X : Y : Z) \mapsto (X : Y : \sqrt 3\, Z)\) is an isomorphism \((C_2)_{\Q(\sqrt 3)} \cong C_{\Q(\sqrt 3)}\) [Sil09, Example I.3.8].

Draft21 Sep 20262.4 Dimension, regularity and smoothness

The dimension of a \(k\)-variety is its dimension as a topological space. If \(X\) is integral, then \(\dim X = \trdeg_k k(X)\), and \(\dim X_L = \dim X\) for every field extension \(L \supseteq k\) [Poo06, Section 1.6].

Definition 2.4.1. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety.

  1. \(X\) is regular at a point \(P\) if the local ring \(\calO_{X,P}\) is regular, i.e. \(\dim_{k(P)} \mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 = \dim \calO_{X,P}\). It is regular if it is regular at every point.
  2. Suppose \(X = V(f_1, \dots, f_m) \subseteq \AA^n_k\) has pure dimension \(d\). Then \(X\) is smooth at \(P \in X(\kbar)\) if the Jacobian matrix \((\partial f_i/\partial x_j(P))\) has rank \(n - d\). A projective variety is smooth at \(P\) if some (equivalently, every) affine patch containing \(P\) is. \(X\) is smooth if it is smooth at every \(P \in X(\kbar)\) [Poo06, Definition 1.7.1].
  3. \(X\) is nice if it is smooth, projective and geometrically integral [Poo06, Definition 1.7.8].

Smoothness is tested at \(\kbar\)-points, so it is a geometric property: \(X\) is smooth if and only if \(X_L\) is, for any field \(L \supseteq k\) [Poo06, Remark 1.7.6]. Regularity is not.

Proposition 2.4.2. Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-variety.

  1. \(X\) is smooth if and only if \(X_{\kbar}\) is regular. In particular, a smooth variety is regular.
  2. If \(k\) is perfect, then \(X\) is smooth if and only if it is regular.
  3. Let \(X\) be integral, and \(P \in X(\kbar)\). Then \(X\) is smooth at \(P\) if and only if \(\dim_{\kbar} \mathfrak{m}_P/\mathfrak{m}_P^2 = \dim X\), where \(\mathfrak{m}_P\) is the maximal ideal of \(\calO_{X_{\kbar}, P}\).
Proof.

Part 3 is [Sil09, Proposition I.1.7], and it gives part 1 at closed points. Over an imperfect field, regular does not imply smooth: see Example 2.4.6.

Remark 2.4.3. The "smooth curves" of [Sil09, Chapter II] are the nice \(k\)-varieties of dimension \(1\).

A singular point of the hypersurface \(V(f) \subseteq \AA^n_k\) is a common zero of \(f\) and its partial derivatives [Poo06, Example 1.7.4].

Example 2.4.4. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). On \(y^2 = x^3 + x\), a singular point satisfies \(2y = 3x^2 + 1 = 0\) and \(x(x^2 + 1) = 0\), which is impossible. So this curve is smooth [Sil09, Example I.1.6].

Example 2.4.5. Suppose \(\operatorname{char} k \neq 2\). On \(y^2 = x^3 + x^2\), the conditions \(2y = 3x^2 + 2x = 0\) and \(x^2(x + 1) = 0\) leave only \((0, 0)\), which is a singular point [Sil09, Example I.1.6].

Example 2.4.6. Let \(k \coloneqq \F_3(t)\), let \(F \coloneqq Y^2Z - X^3 + tZ^3\), and let \(E \coloneqq V_+(F) \subseteq \PP^2_k\), the curve \(y^2 = x^3 - t\). Let \(s \in \kbar\) with \(s^3 = t\). Since \(\operatorname{char} k = 3\),

\begin{equation*} \frac{\partial F}{\partial X} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial Y} = 2YZ, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial Z} = Y^2, \end{equation*}

and \(F\) vanishes at \((s : 0 : 1)\) too, so this is a singular point and \(E\) is not smooth. Over \(\kbar\) the equation is \(y^2 = (x - s)^3\), a cuspidal cubic, which is integral; so \(E\) is geometrically integral. Yet \(E\) is regular. Every other \(\kbar\)-point is smooth, and the closed point \(P \coloneqq V(y, x^3 - t)\), with residue field \(k(s)\) of degree \(3\), has maximal ideal \((y, x^3 - t) = (y)\) in \(\calO_{E, P}\), because \(x^3 - t = y^2\) there. So \(\calO_{E,P}\) is a discrete valuation ring.
